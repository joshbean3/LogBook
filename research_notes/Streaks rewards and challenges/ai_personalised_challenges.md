# AI-personalized short challenges ("one day sprints", "week sprints"): design, evidence, LLM guardrails, safety, rules

Researched 2026-11-13. Scope: adults, consumer iPhone app, United States, with EU notes. English-language sources.

How to read these notes:

- **[Platform rule]** = Apple, FTC or EU text. **[Peer-reviewed]** = journal or conference paper. **[Preprint]** = arXiv, not peer reviewed. **[Company]** = the company's own blog, help page or manual (practice as they report it). **[Commentary]** = reviewers, forums, law-firm alerts, vendor blogs.
- **(opened)** = I opened and read the page in this session. **(snippet)** = seen only in a search result summary. Treat snippets as leads and check before quoting a number in public.
- I did not run any of the apps. Nothing here is a first-hand screen observation.
- Nothing here is legal advice.

## 1. Challenge design in practice: how leading apps choose difficulty, duration, enrollment, concurrency and failure handling

### Takeaway
The common pattern is a small, fixed stack (a few daily quests, one weekly, one monthly), opt-in for anything social or branded, and silent expiry on failure with no penalty. Only a few products scale the target from the person's own history (Apple's monthly challenge, Garmin Auto Goal, Whoop's strain target, Oura's activity goal), and their makers publish almost nothing about the formula. User complaints about Apple's version show the main risk of naive history scaling: a ratchet.

### Cited Findings

**Duolingo (daily quests, friends quests, monthly challenge)**
- [Company] (opened) Friends Quests: each week a learner is paired at random with one friend and given a random shared challenge (for example a set amount of XP or a number of perfect lessons). "Once you have your assignment, you have five days to complete the challenge!" Pairing is automatic after 1 p.m. EST on Tuesdays, both people must follow each other, and both must have done at least one lesson that week. "You can't pick a friend to team up with." Partners are matched by activity level so nobody "has to carry the quest all by yourself." [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/friends-quests/)
- [Company] (opened) Friends Quests are automatic by default with an off switch (Profile, gear, General, toggle off "Friends Quest"). The post states no penalty for failing a quest and reports no A/B result for the feature. The only number is correlational: learners who follow friends are "5.6x more likely" to finish their course. [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/friends-quests/)
- [Commentary] (opened, third-party guide dated January 2025) Daily Quests: three new quests each day, refreshed every 24 hours. Tasks include earning XP, finishing a number of lessons, practicing for a set time, and perfect lessons. Each completed quest opens a chest (gems, or a 15 minute XP boost for gold). The monthly badge needs 25 to 50 quests "depending on activity level". A friends quest example is 30 lessons in 4 days, split however the pair likes, worth 100 gems, a 30 minute boost and 5 quests toward the month. [DuolingoGuides](https://duolingoguides.com/what-is-a-quest-in-duolingo/)
- [Commentary] (snippet) Another third-party source says the monthly target rose from 50 quests in early 2023 to 100 by June 2024. This conflicts with the "25 to 50" figure above. Neither is official. [Duoplanet newsletter](https://newsletter.duoplanet.com/posts/how-many-quests-duolingo)
- [Company] (snippet) Duolingo's lesson difficulty (not quests) is personalized by its Birdbrain model, which aims for a "Goldilocks" level that is "not too easy and not too hard". [Duolingo blog: right level of difficulty](https://blog.duolingo.com/right-level-of-difficulty); [Duolingo blog: Birdbrain](https://blog.duolingo.com/learning-how-to-help-you-learn-introducing-birdbrain)

**Apple Fitness (monthly challenge, limited edition awards)**
- [Commentary] (snippet) Apple publishes no formula. Apple Support Community replies say the monthly challenge is "based on your previous month's activity", that challenges are generated from current activity goals and "how often you missed, met and exceeded those goals", or from "your latest workout efforts". The answers disagree on the window. [Apple discussions 251319320](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/251319320); [Apple discussions 253626525](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/253626525)

Reviewers call the challenges "semi-personalized" (for example a calorie total or exercise minutes over the month). - [Commentary] (snippet) Ratchet complaints run both ways. Users report that a month of going far over a goal becomes the next month's target, an older user asks how to lower the difficulty, and other users say the challenges are "no longer challenging" or "always the same". There is no user control over difficulty. [Apple discussions 256111936](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/256111936); [Apple discussions 252825370](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/252825370); [Apple discussions 254604633](https://origin-discussions2-us-dr-prz.apple.com/thread/254604633)
- [Commentary] (snippet) Limited edition awards are one-day, date-bound and the same for everyone (for example Heart Month: close the Exercise ring on February 14). "Once they're gone, they're gone." ### Inferences
- A "one day sprint" plus a "week sprint" plus an optional monthly layer matches the stack users already know from Duolingo and Apple. One active challenge per horizon is the norm for personalized challenges. Only fixed, catalog challenges run many at once.
- No mainstream product punishes a failed challenge except Habitica. The default is quiet expiry.
- Personalized targets are usually automatic, and fixed public challenges are usually opt-in. For an LLM-written challenge drawn from private data, offering it with an accept step is the safer blend. It also fits the consent posture in section 5.
- Scaling a target only from recent performance creates a ratchet (Apple complaints). A cap on growth, a floor, and a user control ("easier", "harder", "not today") address the complaints directly. Oura's lack of manual override is a known limitation by its own admission.
- Whoop and Oura show the recovery-aware pattern to copy: the target moves down on poor recovery and a rest or illness mode suspends goals outright.

### Gaps
- Apple, Garmin, Whoop and Oura publish no formulas. Apple's own user guide text on monthly challenges was not found. Everything on Apple's method is community or reviewer inference.
- No official Duolingo source on daily quest targets, whether they adapt per user, or measured effects of quests.
- Headspace and Calm: I found no reliable official description of their daily programs or challenge mechanics. Todoist: nothing retrieved (its Karma, daily and weekly goals and vacation mode are not covered here). Zwift, Peloton and NRC details are thin.
- None of the companies report how often challenges are completed, so real-world success rates are unknown.

## 2. Evidence on challenge and goal parameters

### Takeaway
The best-supported choices are short, proximal goals, targets adapted to the person's own recent baseline, and built-in slack so one miss does not end the attempt. The "85 percent rule" is a result about error rates in simple machine and perceptual learning, not about motivation, so it is at most a loose heuristic. Prompt effects fade within weeks, so anything delivered daily needs variety and restraint.

### Cited Findings

**Optimal difficulty and the 85 percent rule**
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia and Cohen (Nature Communications 2019) derive an optimal training accuracy of about 85% (error rate about 15.87%) for binary classification tasks learned by stochastic gradient descent, under a Gaussian noise assumption. They tested it on artificial neural networks and a model of perceptual learning. [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- [Commentary] (snippet) Limits: the work used two-choice tasks with clear right and wrong answers. The lead author said it does not justify advice like aiming for a B average, and that it most plausibly applies to perceptual learning. It measures learning speed, not motivation or adherence. [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/11/191105113457.htm)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) In physical activity, a meta-analysis (McEwan et al. 2016, as summarized by Swann et al.) found goal setting had a medium effect on activity even when goals were vague, and specific goals did not beat vague ones. Swann et al. argue specific performance goals may suit already-active people less than learning or "open" goals suit inactive beginners. In walking-session experiments, SMART goals produced more pressure and tension, and open goals ("see how far you can go") produced more interest in repeating the session. Evidence is from short sessions, not long-term behavior. [Swann et al. critical review](https://ro.uow.edu.au/sspapers/4660); [Swann et al. follow-up](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10413200.2020.1815100)

**Short horizons and proximal subgoals**
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Bandura and Schunk (1981, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 41(3)): children with large math deficits who worked toward proximal subgoals "progressed rapidly in self-directed learning", gained mastery, self-efficacy and intrinsic interest. Distal goals "had no demonstrable effects". A later thesis using self-set proximal goals did not reproduce the effect. [Bandura and Schunk 1981](https://core.ac.uk/download/357623113.pdf)

**Adaptive, personalized goals in physical activity**
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Adams et al. 2013 (PLoS ONE): overweight adults got either a percentile-based adaptive daily step goal (built from their own recent days) or a static 10,000 step goal. Adaptive rose 2,728 steps a day from baseline, static rose 1,598, a difference of 1,130 steps a day (Cohen's d = .74). Small trial (N = 20, 6 months per one summary). [Adams et al. 2013](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/87x69/an-adaptive-physical-activity-intervention-for-overweight-adults-a-randomized-controlled-trial); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:plo:pone00:0082901)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) WalkIT Arizona: 2x2 factorial trial, 512 insufficiently active adults, one year, adaptive versus static goals crossed with immediate versus delayed reinforcement. Adaptive goals made people more likely to do any moderate to vigorous activity on a given day. Immediate reinforcement produced larger increases in daily minutes. The combination did best. [IJBNPA 2023](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com:443/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12966-023-01494-2.pdf); [JMIR Formative Research 2020](https://formative.jmir.org/2020/12/e19863/PDF)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Zhou et al. 2018 (JMIR mHealth and uHealth): 10-week RCT, 64 adults, fully automated machine-learning personalized daily step goals versus a constant 10,000. Design confirmed, result not retrieved in this session (see Gaps). [Zhou et al. 2018](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/1/e28/)

**Just-in-time adaptive interventions and habituation**
- [Peer-reviewed] (opened) HeartSteps V1 micro-randomized trial (Klasnja et al. 2019, Annals of Behavioral Medicine): 44 recruited, 37 analyzed, 42 days, up to five decision points a day. At each available point the system sent nothing (0.4), a walking suggestion (0.3) or an anti-sedentary suggestion (0.3), averaging 2.4 suggestions a day. Any suggestion raised the next 30 minutes' steps by 14% (about 35 steps, p = .06). Walking suggestions raised it by 24% (about 59 steps, p = .02). The walking effect started at 107% (about 271 steps) and fell about 3% a day, undetectable by day 29. Anti-sedentary suggestions had no detectable effect. [Klasnja et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6401341/)
- [Peer-reviewed] (opened) The authors attribute the decay mainly to habituation. In exit interviews participants said the suggestions became boring after 2 to 4 weeks. Suggestions were withheld when the person was walking or running, had just finished activity, was driving, or was offline (about 20% of decision points). Tailoring used time of day, weekday or weekend, weather and location. [Klasnja et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6401341/)

**Fresh starts and Monday effects**
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Dai, Milkman and Riis (Management Science 2014): gym visits and goal-related behavior rise after temporal landmarks: the start of a week, month, semester or year, and after birthdays. The proposed mechanism is separation from the "past, imperfect self". [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/why-monday-is-the-best-day-for-setting-new-goals.html); [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=13852)

**Does failing demotivate? Streaks, slack and repair**
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Silverman and Barasch (Journal of Consumer Research 2023, seven studies): showing an intact streak raises later engagement relative to showing a broken one. The drop is larger when people blame themselves and smaller when they can "repair" the streak. In one study about 66% chose the same activity again with an intact streak versus about 58% with a broken one. The authors advise apps not to highlight breaks ("Hey, you broke your streak!"). [CU Boulder summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [University of Delaware](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Sharif and Shu (Journal of Marketing Research 2017; OBHDP 2021): goals framed with "emergency reserves" (for example 7 days a week with 2 emergency skip days) are preferred and lead to more persistence after a miss than both an easy goal (5 days) and a hard goal (7 days). In a step-count field experiment people with reserves took up to 20% more steps and hit their goal on up to 40% more days. A student replication in a diet setting found no advantage. [UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/); [CHIBE record](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/nudging-persistence-after-failure-through-emergency-reserves/); [Field experiment paper](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf); [Utrecht thesis replication](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/44466)

**Commitment and opt-in**
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Opt-out framing raises enrollment (nearly triple, to 38%, in a diabetes behavioral program; 3.6% versus 1.0% in the GAMEPAD activity trial with no difference in protocol completion). The Penn authors also caution that opt-out defaults can bring in less motivated people and lower engagement. These are enrollment studies for programs, not for individual challenges. [CHIBE: opt-in vs opt-out trial](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/a-randomized-controlled-trial-of-opt-in-versus-opt-out-enrollment-into-a-diabetes-behavioral-intervention); [GAMEPAD study, American Heart Journal 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ahj.2025.107285)

### Inferences
- Target a high but not certain completion rate and tune it from the person's own record. A workable rule in code: set the target near the level the person reached on roughly 60 to 85 percent of comparable recent days or weeks (Adams used a percentile of the person's own recent days). The 85 percent figure is a convenient ceiling borrowed by analogy. It is not evidence about motivation, and the notes should not present it as such.
- Short horizons are well supported in principle (Bandura and Schunk) and in practice (every product in section 1). A day and a week are both proximal. Tie the week sprint to Monday and the day sprint to the morning to borrow the fresh start effect.
- Build slack in: "5 of 7 days" or "one skip allowed" framed as a reserve. On a miss, show nothing punitive, keep past wins visible, and offer a small repair or a new sprint. This follows Silverman and Barasch and Sharif and Shu directly.
- For newer or lapsed users, an open-ended wording ("see how many you can clear before lunch") may work better than a tight numeric target (Swann). The assistant can pick between the two forms from a menu.
- Expect any repeated prompt to lose most of its effect in 2 to 4 weeks (HeartSteps). Vary challenge types, skip days when context says the person is unavailable, and keep the surprise variant rare.
- Accept-to-start is defensible: default enrollment gets more starts but probably weaker commitment. For a single-user personal app, quality of commitment matters more than reach.

### Gaps
- No study I found tests success-rate targets for challenge completion in consumer apps. The 60 to 85 percent band above is my inference.
- No direct evidence found on surprise or novelty challenges, on time-limited or scarcity framing in health apps, or on how often a "random" challenge can appear before it stops feeling special.
- Zhou et al. 2018 result was not retrieved. My unverified recollection is that the personalized group's steps fell much less over 10 weeks than the 10,000-step control group's. Check the paper before citing.
- Korinek et al. 2018 ("Just Walk") results were not retrieved. HeartSteps V2 and its reinforcement learning results were not researched.
- McEwan 2016 and the Swann open-goal experiments were seen only in search summaries. Original effect sizes not checked.

## 3. LLM-generated coaching and challenges: evidence, failure modes, guardrails

### Takeaway
Up to 2026, LLM-written coaching text is rated as good as or better than human-written text by lay users and often by experts, but almost all evidence is ratings in simulated or lab settings, not behavior change in live trials. The documented weak points are numerical reasoning over personal data, confident errors, incompleteness, over-caution, and inconsistency between runs. The products and papers that perform best keep arithmetic and data access in code and tools, and score outputs against explicit rubrics.

### Cited Findings

**Quality, personalization and acceptance**
- [Preprint, Google] (snippet) PH-LLM, a Gemini model fine-tuned for sleep and fitness coaching, was tested on 857 expert-written case studies with rubrics for domain knowledge, use of user data, personalization and potential for harm. In fitness, model answers were "not statistically different from expert performance". In sleep, experts stayed ahead. [Cosentino et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.06474)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) A sleep coaching comparison of human coaches and an LLM found no clear difference on five metrics for expert and GPT-4 raters, and lay users rated the LLM answers as significantly more helpful. [PubMed 39454500](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39454500/)
- [Peer-reviewed, CHI 2025] (snippet) Haag et al., "The Last JITAI?": GPT-4 produced 450 just-in-time intervention decisions and messages for three cardiac rehab personas across five contexts each. Twenty-seven lay raters scored the GPT-4 messages above those written by 10 laypeople and 10 healthcare professionals on appropriateness, engagement, effectiveness and professionality. Simulated personas, not a live trial. [Haag et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.08658v3); [JKU record](https://research.jku.at/en/publications/the-last-jitai-exploring-large-language-models-for-issuing-just-i/)
- [Preprint] (opened, abstract) GPTCoach: a GPT-4 chatbot that runs an evidence-based coaching onboarding conversation, uses motivational interviewing strategies and reads wearable data to form an activity plan. Lab study, 16 participants, three months of their historical data. The authors report "promising evidence" that it gathers rich context and that people were comfortable sharing concerns. [Jörke et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06061)
- [Preprint] (opened, abstract) Song et al. 2025: a 30-day physical activity study comparing equal randomization, a contextual bandit, an LLM alone, an LLM with history, and a hybrid in which the bandit picks the intervention type (self-monitoring, gain frame, loss frame, social comparison) and the LLM writes the message inside that type. The hybrid "retains the perceived acceptance of LLM-generated messages", uses fewer tokens and gives an explicit decision rule. The abstract does not claim better step counts. [Song et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07275)

**Failure modes**
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) ChatGPT exercise recommendations (JMIR Medical Education, UConn): about 41% of the content expected in a gold-standard recommendation was present, and what was present was about 91% accurate. It wrongly told people with hypertension to avoid vigorous exercise. [UConn Today](https://today.uconn.edu/2024/02/are-chatgpt-exercise-recommendations-just-what-the-doctor-ordered)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) An expert evaluation of GPT-4 exercise programs found them generally safety-conscious but imprecise for individual conditions and goals, "often prioritizing excessive safety over the effectiveness of training". A separate small study of GPT-4o plans for type 2 diabetes profiles found "serious safety issues" in some plans for patients with complications (three fictional profiles, three expert reviewers). [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1035771); [Sports (MDPI) 2025](https://mdpi.com/2075-4663/13/4/92)
- [Preprint, 2026] (snippet) A repeated-generation study across three current models found exercise prescriptions "differ markedly across models in terms of semantic consistency and output reproducibility", with near-ceiling scores on safety language in all three. [arXiv 2604.19598](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.19598.pdf)
- [Peer-reviewed, Nature Communications 2026] (snippet) Wearable data needs numerical reasoning that plain LLMs handle poorly. Google's Personal Health Insights Agent, which writes and runs code and uses retrieval, scored 84% exact-match accuracy on objective numerical questions versus 74% for a code-generation baseline, in a 650-hour expert evaluation over 4,000-plus questions. Even the agent was wrong on about one in six numerical questions. [Merrill et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12855967); [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06464)
- [Commentary] (snippet) A Washington Post test (reported by TechRepublic) had general chatbots grade heart health from Apple Watch data. Physicians disputed the grades, and researchers said the models made logical or information errors while answering with confidence. [TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/de/article/news-chatgpt-claude-apple-health-grades-doctors-dispute/)

**Guardrails as practiced by the leading products**
- [Company] (opened) Google's Fitbit coach splits the work: a conversational agent, a data science agent that "iteratively" fetches and analyzes data "using code generation when needed", and a domain expert agent that writes plans. It benchmarks against the user's own baselines and population statistics. It is evaluated on SHARP: safety, helpfulness, accuracy, relevance, personalization, with "over 1 million human annotations and more than 100k hours of human evaluation", plus automated raters. A footnote says it is "not a medical device", is "intended for general wellness and fitness purposes only", and that users should "consult a qualified healthcare professional for any health concerns". [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/how-we-are-building-the-personal-health-coach/)
- [Preprint, Google] (snippet) The related Personal Health Agent paper describes the same three-role split and reports evaluation on 10 benchmark tasks with more than 7,000 annotations and 1,100 hours from experts and end users. [arXiv 2508.20148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.20148v2)
- [Company] (opened) Oura scores Advisor against a rubric: scope and boundaries, escalation (suggest a clinician when metrics are outside wellness ranges), data use (read signals correctly and admit uncertainty), tone and care, and clinical grounding. Hard rules: the response "must not invent personal context", must stay in scope, must not give unsafe reassurance, and when data is incomplete it "needs to say so clearly". Oura uses clinician-written benchmarks, several LLM judges from different providers cross-checked against human grading, and regression tests on every model, prompt or data change. It says conversations are "explicitly never used to train third-party AI models". [Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/how-oura-evaluates-generative-ai-to-earn-trust/)
- [Company] (snippet) WHOOP Coach's FAQ: "a tool for informational and wellness purposes ... not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment." WHOOP says conversations are not accessed without consent, data is not stored with third parties, and personal physiological data is not used to train public models. One reviewer reported the model provider keeps conversations briefly, which conflicts with a "zero retention" description elsewhere. [WHOOP](https://www.whoop.com/ae/en/thelocker/whoop-unveils-the-new-whoop-coach-powered-by-openai/); [Wareable](https://www.wareable.com/wearable-tech/whoop-launches-gpt-4-ai-coach); [Boardroom](https://boardroom.tv/tech-talk-reviews-whoop-coach-openai/)

### Inferences
Recommended architecture for a small app, assembled from the evidence above (my synthesis, not a published standard):
- **Code decides what is allowed, the model decides what fits and how to say it.** Code builds a short candidate menu from the person's data (habit to revive, to-do backlog to clear, journal prompt, a run or lift inside safe bounds). The model returns structured output: a menu id, parameters chosen only from ranges that code supplied, a title, and a one-line reason. This mirrors Song et al.'s bandit-picks-type, LLM-writes-message split and Google's agent split.
- **All numbers come from code.** Baselines, targets, caps and streak counts are computed in code and passed in. The model never does arithmetic on raw data (PHIA: even a tool-using agent misses about 16% of numeric questions).
- **Validate, then fall back.** After generation, code checks the schema, that the id is on the menu, that parameters are in bounds, that any number in the text matches the supplied facts, and that banned topics and words are absent. On any failure, timeout or refusal, use the rule-based template for the same candidate. The person should not be able to tell the difference in safety, only in wording.
- **Ground the reason in facts the person can verify.** Show a human-readable "why this" line built from supplied facts ("You ran 3 times last week. Longest was 4.2 mi."). Never let the model assert personal context that was not in its input (Oura's rule).
- **Say when data is thin.** With too little history, issue a generic, easy challenge and say it is a starter.
- **Tone:** plain, not over-familiar, no guilt. The Haag and sleep-coach results suggest users like LLM wording, so the risk is less blandness than false intimacy and confident error.
- **Keep a feedback control** like Strava's (helpful, not helpful, offensive) plus "easier", "harder", "not today", and log outcomes for tuning.
- **Regression test prompts** against a fixed set of synthetic user states on every model or prompt change (Oura), including edge cases: no data, illness flag, injury note in journal, very high recent load.

### Gaps
- I found no published randomized trial showing LLM-generated challenges or coaching change behavior over months. Evidence is ratings, lab probes and short studies.
- I found no paper that directly measures "hallucinated facts about the user" rates or over-familiar tone in health coaching. These are named risks in company rubrics (Oura) rather than quantified failure rates.
- No published study specifically validates "constrained menu plus code bounds" against free generation for safety. The recommendation rests on the numerical-reasoning results and on company practice.
- Apple's Workout Buddy (generative voice feedback) wording and disclosures were not found. A study on personality-informed LLM messages (90 participants) was seen only in an aggregator listing and is not relied on.
- GPTCoach's full-paper limitations were not read (abstract only).

## 4. Safety for training challenges

### Takeaway
The strongest current evidence says to bound the single session against the person's own recent longest session, not to police weekly totals: runs more than 10% longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days carried a 52% to 128% higher overuse injury rate in a 5,205-runner cohort, while the weekly-change ratio and the acute to chronic workload ratio did not predict injury. Apple's rules add a hard line: an app must not urge people into challenges that risk physical harm.

### Cited Findings

**Progression and overload**
- [Peer-reviewed] (opened) Frandsen et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine 2025;59:1203-1210. 5,205 runners with Garmin data (mean age 45.8, 22% female), 588,071 sessions, 18 months, 35% reported a running-related injury (72% of those overuse). Compared with a session at or below 110% of the longest run in the past 30 days, the adjusted hazard rate ratio for overuse injury was 1.64 (95% CI 1.31 to 2.05) for a spike of more than 10% up to 30%, 1.52 (1.16 to 2.00) for more than 30% up to 100%, and 2.28 (1.50 to 3.48) for more than 100%. [Frandsen et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110/)
- [Peer-reviewed] (opened) Same study: the week-to-week ratio showed no relationship (hazard ratios 0.88 to 0.97, none significant), and the acute to chronic workload ratio showed an inverse pattern (0.75, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.96, for a large spike). Authors' advice: "Runners should avoid running a distance in their current session that exceeds 10% of the longest distance covered in the previous 30 days." [Frandsen et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110/)
- [Peer-reviewed] (opened) Limits stated by the authors: self-reported injuries, distance as the only exposure, binned categories, observational design so no causal claim, and progression across several sessions is not captured. Randomized trials are needed. A coach's critique adds that most of the cohort ran three or fewer times a week. [Frandsen et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110/); [Running with Rock critique](https://runningwithrock.com/critique-too-much-running/)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) The weekly 10 percent rule failed its main trial. Buist et al. 2008 (GRONORUN, American Journal of Sports Medicine): 532 novice runners, a graded 13-week program built on the 10% rule versus a standard 8-week program. Injury incidence was 20.8% versus 20.3%. [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/20220); [Pogo Physio review](https://www.pogophysio.com.au/blog/the-10-rule-does-it-hold-true/)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Acute to chronic workload ratio: Impellizzeri et al. (Sports Medicine 2020) argue the ratio is a rescaling with "no predictive advantage" and that it is "time to dismiss ACWR and its underlying theory". Supporters (Gabbett and colleagues) dispute this and cite supporting evidence in elite team sport. The 2025 cohort above did not support it for recreational runners. [Impellizzeri et al.](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/145169); [Frontiers editorial on the debate](https://dx.doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2021.669687)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) The IOC consensus statement on load (Soligard et al., BJSM 2016) concluded that poor load management is a major risk factor for injury, and its companion (Part 2) that it is a risk factor for acute illness and overtraining. It reviewed rapid changes in load, calendar congestion and psychological load. [Soligard et al. 2016](https://repository.up.ac.za/handle/2263/57024?show=full)

**How recovery-aware products handle rest, illness and injury**
- [Company] (snippet) Oura lowers the activity goal on low readiness, prompts Rest Mode when temperature is elevated, and Rest Mode mutes activity goals. Whoop lowers or raises the strain target with recovery. [Oura: Activity Score](https://ouraring.com/blog/activity-score/); [Whoop: Strain Coach](https://www.whoop.com/the-locker/strain-coach/)

**Platform rule on risky challenges**
- [Platform rule] (opened) App Store Review Guideline 1.4.5: "Apps should not urge customers to participate in activities (like bets, challenges, etc.) or use their devices in a way that risks physical harm to themselves or others." [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- [Platform rule] (opened) Guideline 1.4.1: medical apps "that could provide inaccurate data or information, or that could be used for diagnosing or treating patients may be reviewed with greater scrutiny", and "Apps should remind users to check with a doctor in addition to using the app and before making medical decisions." [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)

**Disordered eating and compulsive exercise risk**
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) A 2025 Flinders University systematic review of 38 studies found regular users of diet and fitness apps were more likely to show problematic habits around food and exercise, including fixation on weight loss, calorie counting and excessive exercise. The authors call for more research on benefits and risks. [Flinders University](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2025/02/22/fitness-apps-fuelling-disordered-eating/)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Simpson and Mazzeo 2017 (493 college students): calorie tracking and fitness tracking were both associated with eating disorder symptoms, and fitness tracking had a unique association. Causation is contested. One review says trackers attract people with existing symptoms rather than cause disorders, and that risk is higher when the motive is weight or shape. [Simpson and Mazzeo 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28214452/); [National Center for Health Research](https://www.center4research.org/fitness-tracking-apps-eating-disorders/)

**How consumer apps word health prompts**
- [Company] Google: "not a medical device", "intended for general wellness and fitness purposes only", "consult a qualified healthcare professional for any health concerns". [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/how-we-are-building-the-personal-health-coach/) (opened). WHOOP: "informational and wellness purposes ... not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment". [WHOOP](https://www.whoop.com/ae/en/thelocker/whoop-unveils-the-new-whoop-coach-powered-by-openai/) (snippet). Oura: "wellness companions, not as a replacement for doctors ... not designed to diagnose or prescribe". [Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/how-oura-evaluates-generative-ai-to-earn-trust/) (opened).

### Inferences
Bounds to compute in code before any training challenge is offered (my synthesis from the findings above):
- **Single-session cap:** never propose a run longer than 110% of the longest run in the past 30 days. Consider a lower cap, since the authors suggest coaches "may consider using a session-specific cut-off below 10%". Apply the same logic to lifting volume by analogy, noting there is no equivalent evidence for strength work here.
- **No weekly-total heroics:** the weekly 10 percent rule and the workload ratio lack support as injury predictors, so do not present them as safety guarantees. A weekly cap is still reasonable as a conservative product rule, but the session cap is the evidence-based one.
- **Respect the plan and rest days:** a sprint must never add a hard session on a planned rest day or stack on top of the day's planned key session. Prefer challenges that reward doing the plan as written, recovery behaviors, or non-training areas.
- **Suspend on flags:** if the person logged illness, injury or pain (journal, a "not feeling well" toggle, or a rest mode), offer no training challenge. Offer habit, to-do or journal sprints only.
- **Never-ask list** (follows from Guideline 1.4.5, the disordered-eating literature and the no-diagnosis rubrics): fasting or skipping meals; calorie restriction or "burn off" targets; weight-loss or body-measurement targets; training through pain, illness or injury; sleep restriction or early-wake challenges that cut sleep; dehydration or sauna-style heat stress; max-effort or one-rep-max tests; back-to-back hard days beyond the plan; "make up" sessions for missed workouts; anything involving medication, supplements or alcohol; anything that needs the phone used while moving in traffic; streak-preservation exercise when sick. This list is my inference. I found no single published checklist.
- **Wording:** one short standing line near training challenges in the wellness style the leaders use ("General fitness guidance, not medical advice. Stop if something hurts, and check with a doctor if you are unsure."), plus the app's existing provider disclosure. Avoid claims of injury prevention.

### Gaps
- Nielsen et al. 2014 (novice runners, weekly distance jumps above 30%) was not retrieved. My unverified recollection is that jumps above 30% over two weeks were linked to more distance-related injuries than jumps under 10%. Check before citing.
- No evidence retrieved on safe progression for strength training, on return-to-training rules after illness (for example the "neck check"), or on minimum rest-day needs from ACSM or similar bodies.
- No research found specifically on fasting challenges, sleep-restriction challenges or weight-loss challenges in apps. The never-ask list rests on adjacent evidence and platform rules.
- The eating-disorder studies are associations. Direction of cause is unresolved.

## 5. Platform, legal and privacy rules

### Takeaway
Apple now requires clear disclosure and explicit permission before personal data goes to a third-party AI (5.1.2(i), revised November 13, 2025), bars HealthKit and fitness data from advertising and data mining, and treats health data as especially sensitive. In the US, an app that syncs with a wearable or Apple Health is likely a "vendor of personal health records" under the FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule, where sharing health data without authorization counts as a breach. In the EU, the AI Act's Article 50(1) duty to tell people they are interacting with an AI system has applied since August 2, 2026.

### Cited Findings

**Apple App Store Review Guidelines (text opened November 13, 2026)**
- [Platform rule] 5.1.2(i): "Unless otherwise permitted by law, you may not use, transmit, or share someone's personal data without first obtaining their permission. You must provide access to information about how and where the data will be used. You must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, including with third-party AI, and obtain explicit permission before doing so." The same paragraph says an app "may not require users to enable system functionalities (such as push notifications, location services, tracking) in order to access functionality". [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- [Commentary] (snippet) The "including with third-party AI" wording was added on November 13, 2025. Before that the rule required consent for sharing but did not name AI. Developer commentary (not Apple text) argues a privacy-policy line alone does not satisfy it and that an in-app consent step is expected. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/13/apples-new-app-review-guidelines-clamp-down-on-apps-sharing-personal-data-with-third-party-ai); [Apple developer news](https://developer.apple.com/news?id=ey6d8onl); [DEV Community](https://dev.to/arshtechpro/apples-guideline-512i-the-ai-data-sharing-rule-that-will-impact-every-ios-developer-1b0p)
- [Platform rule] 5.1.1(i): the privacy policy must identify what data is collected, how, and all uses; confirm that third parties receiving user data give "the same or equal protection"; and explain retention, deletion and how to revoke consent. 5.1.1(ii): apps must "provide the customer with an easily accessible and understandable way to withdraw consent". 5.1.1(iii): collect only data "required to accomplish the relevant task". [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- [Platform rule] 5.1.2(vi): data from HealthKit and similar APIs "may not be used for marketing, advertising or use-based data mining, including by third parties." [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- [Platform rule] 5.1.3(i): apps "may not use or disclose to third parties data gathered in the health, fitness, and medical research context—including from the ... HealthKit API, Motion and Fitness ...—for advertising, marketing, or other use-based data mining purposes other than improving health management, or for the purpose of health research, and then only with permission." Also: "You must disclose the specific health data that you are collecting from the device." 5.1.3(ii): apps "must not write false or inaccurate data into HealthKit" and "may not store personal health information in iCloud." [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- [Platform rule] 1.4.1 and 1.4.5: see section 4. [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- [Platform rule] 4.7 covers "chatbots" and other software "not embedded in the binary": the developer is "responsible for all such software offered in your app". 4.7.1 requires following 5.1 privacy rules including for "sensitive data (such as health ...)", "a method for filtering objectionable material, a mechanism to report content and timely responses to concerns". 4.7.3: no sharing of data or privacy permissions with such software "without explicit user consent in each instance". 1.2 lists "AI content creation apps" among user-generated content cases. [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)

**FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (US)**
- [Platform rule] (opened) The rule covers vendors of personal health records, PHR related entities and their service providers that are not covered by HIPAA. A PHR is an electronic record that "has the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources". The FTC's guidance says a fitness app that can sync with a wearable is likely covered, even if some users never connect one. [FTC compliance guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-ftcs-health-breach-notification-rule-0)
- [Platform rule] (opened) A breach is not only a hack: "Incidents of unauthorized access, including sharing of covered information without an individual's authorization, trigger notification obligations." Notice to individuals is due without unreasonable delay and within 60 calendar days of discovery. For 500 or more people the FTC is notified at the same time, and media notice applies at 500 or more residents of a state. For fewer than 500, the FTC report is due within 60 days after the end of the calendar year. Violators "may be subject to a civil penalty of up to $53,088 per violation." [FTC compliance guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-ftcs-health-breach-notification-rule-0)
- [Commentary] (snippet) The 2024 amendments (finalized April 26, 2024) made explicit that health apps are covered and that a breach includes "an unauthorized disclosure", and require notices to name the third parties that acquired the data. [FTC final rule](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/hbnr_final_rule_04_25.pdf); [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2024/05/ftc-finalizes-hbnr-to-cover-health-app-breaches)

**US state law note**
- [Commentary] (snippet) Washington's My Health My Data Act (in force March 31, 2024 for most businesses) requires prior, express opt-in consent to collect or share "consumer health data", defines that term broadly, and is enforceable by the Attorney General and by a private right of action under the state Consumer Protection Act. Sources disagree on damages. [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2023/04/my-health-my-data-washington-enacts-first-state-comprehensive-health-privacy-law); [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/a-new-paradigm-for-consumer-health-data-privacy-in-washington-state/)

**EU AI Act (differs from the US)**
- [Platform rule] (opened) Article 50(1): "Providers shall ensure that AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons are designed and developed in such a way that the natural persons concerned are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious from the point of view of a natural person who is reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect". Article 50(2): providers of systems generating synthetic text, audio, image or video must ensure outputs "are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated." Article 50(5): the information must be given "in a clear and distinguishable manner at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure." Applies from August 2, 2026. [EU AI Act, Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)
- [Commentary] (snippet) Law-firm alerts from August 2026 say the Digital Omnibus did not delay Article 50(1), (3) or (4). The only Article 50 change reported is a grace period to December 2, 2026 for the 50(2) machine-readable marking duty for systems already on the market. High-risk obligations moved to December 2027 and August 2028. Verify against the Official Journal text. [Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/alerts-technology-dpc-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-now-in-force); [Jones Walker](https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/ai-law-blog/yes-august-2-still-matters-the-eu-approved-a-high-risk-ai-delay-but-most-trans.html); [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/viewpoints/2026/08/102nfqm/you-talkin-to-me-operationalising-the-eu-ai-acts-transparency-obligations)

**Consent and disclosure wording used by comparable apps**
- [Company] Fitbit coach: opt-in preview, and users "are shown a consent prompt for access to their Fitbit data". [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/how-we-are-building-the-personal-health-coach/) (opened)
- [Company] Strava: says the feature "uses generative AI", names the data used ("health and location data", up to "the previous 30 days"), states insights are "only visible to the owner", and documents how to hide it. [Strava support](https://support.strava.com/en-us/articles/15401629-athlete-intelligence-on-strava) (opened)
- [Company] Oura: conversations are "strictly siloed", not sold or shared, and "explicitly never used to train third-party AI models". [Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/how-oura-evaluates-generative-ai-to-earn-trust/) (opened)
- [Company] WHOOP: conversations "will not be accessed without your consent and your data is not stored with 3rd parties", and personal data is not used to train public models. [WHOOP](https://www.whoop.com/au/en/thelocker/introducing-whoop-coach-powered-by-openai) (snippet)

### Inferences
- If the challenge generator sends habits, journal text, goals or training and Health data to a third-party model provider, Apple's 5.1.2(i) requires an explicit, in-app permission step that names the third-party AI and says what is sent and why, before the first call. A privacy-policy sentence alone is unlikely to be enough. The rule-based fallback doubles as the experience for people who decline.
- Data minimization (5.1.1(iii)) argues for sending only the computed facts the challenge needs (counts, baselines, the candidate menu), not raw journal entries or full Health exports. This also shrinks hallucination surface and FTC exposure.
- Health and fitness data used for challenge personalization must never feed marketing, ads or analytics profiles (5.1.2(vi), 5.1.3(i)). Sending it to a model provider to "provide the feature" needs the user's permission and a provider contract that gives equal protection (5.1.1(i)) and no training on the data. The leading apps all state the no-training point in user-facing copy.
- Under the FTC rule, an app that draws on Apple Health, Strava or a wearable plus user-entered data is likely a PHR vendor. Sharing that data with an AI provider beyond what the user authorized could itself be a reportable "breach". The consent record (what was shown, when, what was agreed) is therefore worth storing.
- Comparable disclosure pattern to copy: (1) label the feature as AI-generated at first exposure and persistently near the output, (2) list the data categories used, (3) say who processes it and that it is not used for training or ads, (4) give an off switch and a feedback control, (5) a wellness-not-medical line. In the EU, item 1 is a legal duty under Article 50(1) for whoever is the "provider" of the app's AI system, and the app's existing provider disclosure under the composer already points in this direction.
- Guideline 4.7.1's call for filtering and a report mechanism suggests keeping a "report this challenge" or "offensive" option on generated content, as Strava does.

### Gaps
- Apple's HealthKit documentation page ("Protecting user privacy") did not load. Its extra wording (for example on selling data or disclosing without consent) is not quoted here. The Review Guidelines text above was read directly.
- The last 14,000 characters of the Review Guidelines page (after 5.2.5) were not read. Guideline 4.5.4 on push notifications was not quoted from the page in this session (see section 6 Gaps).
- Whether a small app that wraps a third-party model is a "provider" or "deployer" under the AI Act, and whether the Act applies at all to a US-only app, were not researched. GDPR rules for health data (special category, explicit consent) were not researched.
- Other US state health-data laws (for example Nevada, Connecticut) and California rules were not researched. Washington's applicability to fitness apps is described by law firms as open to interpretation.
- I found no verbatim in-app consent screen text from Fitbit, WHOOP, Oura or Strava, only their public descriptions.

## 6. Notifications and timing for challenges

### Takeaway
Evidence favors few, well-timed, context-aware prompts: effects of activity prompts roughly halve within two weeks and vanish by about four, midday performed best in one large trial, and users start disabling notifications at only a few pushes a week when relevance is low. There is no direct evidence on how often a surprise challenge can appear before it stops feeling special.

### Cited Findings
- [Peer-reviewed] (opened) HeartSteps: at an average of 2.4 suggestions a day, the effect of walking suggestions fell about 3% a day and was undetectable by day 29, and participants found them boring after 2 to 4 weeks. Suggestions were tied to five user-chosen daily moments (morning commute, lunch, mid-afternoon, evening commute, after dinner) and withheld when the person was already active or driving. [Klasnja et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6401341/)
- [Peer-reviewed] (snippet) Bidargaddi et al. 2018 (JMIR mHealth and uHealth): micro-randomized trial, 1,255 users of a commercial well-being app, 89 days, six possible send times (8:30 am, 12:30 pm, 5:30 pm, 6:30 pm, 7:30 pm, 8:30 pm). A tailored push raised the chance of engaging in the next 24 hours by 3.9% overall. The effect was largest at 12:30 pm on weekends (11.8%). The weekday versus weekend difference was not significant (P = .18). The paper cites a background figure of about 50 notifications a day per person across apps. [Bidargaddi et al. 2018](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/11/e10123/)
- [Peer-reviewed, KDD 2020] (snippet) Duolingo's "sleeping, recovering bandit" (Yancey and Settles) picks which reminder template to send, treats a recently used template as temporarily less effective (it "recovers" with rest), and rewards a lesson completed within two hours of the reminder. It is used for millions of daily reminders. The design encodes the idea that repeated wording wears out. [Yancey and Settles 2020](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/yancey.kdd20.pdf)
- [Platform rule] (snippet) Apple's Human Interface Guidelines define four interruption levels (passive, active, time sensitive, critical). Time Sensitive breaks through Focus and scheduled summary and is to be used "only for notifications that are relevant in the moment". A marketing notification must never break through a Focus or scheduled delivery. [Apple HIG: managing notifications](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/managing-notifications)
- [Platform rule] (opened) Guideline 5.1.2(i): an app "may not require users to enable system functionalities (such as push notifications ...)" to access functionality. [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- [Commentary] (snippet, vendor and survey data, some dated 2015 to 2017) A Helplama survey reports the share of users likely to disable notifications at each weekly volume: about 10 to 12% at one push a week, 42% at two to five. Braze reports a 38.3% push opt-out rate among surveyed users' enabled apps (24.5% without one outlier), with frequency plus low relevance as the stated reason. Against that, an Urban Airship benchmark found media-app users who got daily pushes retained at twice the rate of those who got weekly pushes. These are self-reports and vendor data, not experiments. [Business of Apps](https://www.businessofapps.com/marketplace/push-notifications/research/push-notifications-statistics/); [Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/opt-out-of-push-notifications-why-users-do-it); [Airship](https://www.airship.com/newsroom/urban-airships-mobile-app-retention-study-for-key-industry-verticals/)
- [Company] (snippet) Practice in comparable products: Fitbit's coach sends one weekly check-in notification for the plan. Duolingo pairs friends quests at a fixed weekly time (Tuesday afternoon). [Google blog](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/devices/fitbit/personal-health-coach-public-preview/); [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/friends-quests/)

### Inferences
- **Daily sprint:** show it in the app's Today view every day without a push, and send at most one optional push at a time the person picks. Morning suits a day sprint that needs the whole day (fresh-start logic, section 2). A challenge tied to a moment (a walk, a journal entry) is better sent near that moment, and midday is a reasonable default given Bidargaddi. Use the active (default) interruption level, never Time Sensitive.
- **Week sprint:** one notification on Monday morning to offer it, and at most one mid-week progress nudge. That matches Fitbit's weekly check-in cadence.
- **Suppress when unavailable:** skip the push if the challenge is already done, if the person is mid-workout, or if a rest or illness flag is set (HeartSteps availability rules).
- **Rotate wording and type:** do not send the same template two days running (Duolingo's recovering-arm idea, HeartSteps habituation).
- **Surprise challenges:** given the 2 to 4 week habituation window for prompts sent several times a day, a surprise that appears daily will stop being a surprise within weeks. A conservative starting point is no more than about one a week, at irregular intervals, never on consecutive days, and delivered in-app rather than by push. This is my inference, and it should be tuned on acceptance and completion rates.
- **Total budget:** keep challenge-related pushes to a handful a week across all types, since self-reported opt-outs climb steeply between one and five a week when relevance is low.
