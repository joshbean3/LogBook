# Neuroscience and neuropsychology of reward, motivation and feedback, applied to a reward system in a consumer habit and training app (as of 2026)

Reading notes for the report writer:

- Evidence tags used below: [Animal] = non-human recording or manipulation; [Human fMRI] = BOLD imaging, correlational, usually n = 15 to 30; [Human PET] = receptor-binding imaging; [Human direct] = intracranial measurement in patients; [Human drug] = pharmacological manipulation; [Human behavior] = lab or field behavior; [Review] = narrative review or perspective; [Meta] = meta-analysis; [HCI] = human factors study.
- "Verified" means I fetched the paper or its abstract page. "Snippet level" means the fact came from a search summary or a secondary page and should be checked against the paper before it is quoted as a number.
- Nothing here is a clinical claim. Gambling research is cited only as the source of harm patterns to avoid.

## 1. Reward prediction error and dopamine: what the classic and current evidence shows, what it predicts, and where the debates are

### Takeaway
The best-supported account says phasic dopamine reports the difference between the reward received and the reward expected, so a fully predicted reward produces no burst at the moment of reward and the response moves to the earliest reliable cue. That account is well supported in animals and broadly consistent with human imaging and a few direct human recordings, but what dopamine "means" beyond a teaching signal (motivation, effort, value ramps, causal learning) is an open debate, and none of it has been measured for an app's points or animations.

### Cited Findings
- [Animal, theory] The founding paper: Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997, Science 275:1593) identified "dopaminergic neurons in the primate whose fluctuating output apparently signals changes or errors in the predictions of future salient and rewarding events", and tied this to temporal-difference learning theory — [Dayan's copy of the abstract](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~dayan/papers/sdm97.html); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9054347/)
- [Animal, Review, verified] Schultz's 2016 review states the three core facts in plain terms: dopamine neurons "remain at baseline activity for fully predicted rewards"; "The response to the reward itself disappears when the reward is predicted"; "The dopamine response is transferred to the next preceding reward-predicting stimulus"; and "their activity decreases if no, or less than predicted, reward occurs" — [Schultz 2016, Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4826767)
- [Animal, Review, verified] The same review describes a two-part response: "An initial, unselective response component registers any environmental object", which is boosted by novelty and surprise and "subsides in a few tens to hundreds of milliseconds", followed by "a second, selective response component" that "reflects only the reward information". It also argues the signal tracks subjective value: "the dopamine reward prediction error response is in fact a utility prediction error response" — [Schultz 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4826767)
- [Animal, Review] Not all dopamine neurons code value. Bromberg-Martin, Matsumoto and Hikosaka (2010, Neuron 68:815) propose separate populations: "value" neurons (excited by reward, inhibited by aversive events), "salience" neurons (excited by both), and an "alerting" signal to unexpected, potentially important sensory cues carried by both — [Bromberg-Martin et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3032992/)
- [Human fMRI, Meta] Chase et al. (2015) ran an activation likelihood meta-analysis of fMRI studies that used reinforcement learning models and found reward prediction errors represented in ventral striatum and midbrain or thalamus, consistent with the animal work. They also found the maps were sensitive to the model-fitting procedure and the amount of spatial smoothing — [Chase et al. 2015](https://typeset.io/pdf/reinforcement-learning-models-and-their-neural-correlates-an-3u0pkk6dgd.pdf)
- [Human fMRI, Meta] Garrison, Erdeniz and Done (2013) found the striatum was the key region for prediction error, with activity spanning dorsal and ventral striatum, and aversive prediction errors found more widely, including insula and habenula — [Garrison et al. 2013](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/3623/)
- [Human fMRI, Meta] Fouragnan, Retzler and Philiastides (2018, Human Brain Mapping) separated a valence network (positive greater than negative outcomes: ventral striatum, ventromedial prefrontal cortex) from a largely distinct surprise network (anterior cingulate, anterior insula, dorsal striatum) — [Fouragnan et al. 2018](https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/separate-neural-representations-of-prediction-error-valence-and-s/)
- [Human drug + fMRI] Causal evidence in humans: Pessiglione et al. (2006, Nature 442:1042) gave healthy volunteers L-DOPA or haloperidol during instrumental learning for money. The size of the striatal reward prediction error was changed by the drugs, and L-DOPA subjects won more money than haloperidol subjects (but did not lose less). A standard action-value model using the prediction error magnitudes reproduced choices under each drug — [Pessiglione et al. 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2636869)
- [Human direct] Subsecond dopamine has been measured in human striatum only in neurosurgery patients. Kishida et al. (2016, PNAS 113:200) recorded in 17 people with Parkinson's disease during a sequential investment task and found dopamine fluctuations encoded reward prediction errors combined with counterfactual errors (how much better or worse the outcome could have been) — [Kishida et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4711839)
- [Human direct] Sands et al. (2023, Science Advances 9:eadi4927) measured subsecond dopamine in patients during deep brain stimulation surgery on a reward and punishment learning task and concluded dopamine can encode both reward and punishment prediction errors, possibly through separate valence-specific pathways — [Sands et al. preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2023/02/26/2023.02.24.529709.full.pdf)
- [Human PET] Older PET work is weaker than often claimed. Koepp et al. (1998, Nature) reported lower raclopride binding in striatum during a video game played for money, read as dopamine release, strongest in ventral striatum and correlated with performance — [Koepp et al. 1998](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/evidence-for-striatal-dopamine-release-during-a-video-game/). A later review from the same group flagged "the potentially confounding influences of head movement and alterations in rCBF" in that study and no consensus on the best method (snippet level) — [later molecular imaging review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3086965)
- [Review, verified] The motivation debate. Berke (2018, Nature Neuroscience 21:787) argues dopamine "provides a dynamic estimate of whether it is worth expending a limited internal resource, such as energy, attention, or time", that motivational functions "are better described as fast ('phasic'), not slow ('tonic')", and that "dopamine release related to motivation is rapidly and locally sculpted by receptors on dopamine terminals, independently from dopamine cell firing". He warns that "Subtracting away a baseline can make a value signal resemble an RPE signal" and that "Conclusions about dopamine release and RPE coding thus need to be viewed with caution". The evidence cited is mostly rat voltammetry, microdialysis and optogenetics — [Berke 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6358212)
- [Review, verified] Berke also notes dopamine loss does not remove enjoyment: lesioned rats show no "basic deficit in appreciating rewards"; the deficit is in choosing to exert effort to get them — [Berke 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6358212)
- [Animal] Ramps. Howe et al. (2013, Nature 500:575) used voltammetry in rats running mazes and found striatal dopamine that gradually rose as animals approached distant rewards, scaling with distance and reward size, and proposed it "could provide sustained motivational drive" — [Howe et al. 2013](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/91049)
- [Animal] Ramps are conditional. A Namboodiri-lab study (Floeder, Jeong and colleagues, eLife) reports mesolimbic dopamine ramps in mice appear mainly when the interval between trials is short relative to the trial — [Floeder et al., eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/98666)
- [Animal] The newest challenge. Jeong et al. (2022, Science, DOI 10.1126/science.abq6740) measured nucleus accumbens dopamine in mice with a fluorescent sensor and argued that dopamine conveys a retrospective causal learning signal (the ANCCR model: learning what preceded a meaningful event) and not a temporal-difference prediction error — [Jeong et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abq6740)
- [Review, verified] The response. Uchida (Harvard), writing in The Transmitter on 13 September 2024, argues ANCCR does not reproduce derivative-like dopamine signals seen in his group's 2020 teleport experiments ("ANCCR does not appear to possess such a capability"), struggles with omission dips without "somewhat arbitrary and untested assumptions", and was tested only against "relatively simplistic variants" of TD learning. He concedes "no model thus far can explain all the observations" and that ANCCR "can also be a useful model". His answer to whether it replaces the classic model: "no, at least not in its current form" — [Uchida 2024, The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/dopamine/does-a-new-theory-of-dopamine-replace-the-classic-model/)

### Inferences
- A reward moment that is identical every time, at a fully predictable point, should by this theory carry little outcome-time signal after the first few exposures. What is left is the cue that predicts it (the app icon, the habit tile, the tap itself). This is the mechanism behind "cues take over from outcomes". It is a prediction from animal and money-reward work, not a measured result for app animations.
- Partial unpredictability (the reward's size or form is not fully known) keeps a positive prediction error possible. The theory also says a reward that comes in smaller than expected produces a negative error, so any variable element should vary upward from a guaranteed floor and never take away something the person expected.
- The two-component finding suggests novelty and sensory surprise alone produce a brief, non-value "alerting" response. A flashy animation can be salient without being valued. Salience is not the same thing as reward.
- Because the debate between teaching signal, motivational value, and causal learning is unresolved, the app's documents should not say what "dopamine does" at a reward moment. They can say that surprise, timing and progress cues are known to affect learning and motivation.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures a prediction error signal, by any method, for points, badges, streak counters or completion animations in a habit or training app.
- Human direct dopamine recordings come from patients with Parkinson's disease or other conditions during surgery, playing money games. How far they generalize to healthy adults is not established in the sources read.
- I did not find a published direct replication of Koepp et al. 1998, and I did not retrieve the full text of Jeong et al. 2022 or a formal published rebuttal beyond the Uchida perspective.
- Habituation of human striatal responses to repeated identical symbolic rewards over weeks: no source found.

## 2. Wanting versus liking (incentive salience): what the distinction means for a design that should feel good without becoming compulsive

### Takeaway
Animal causal work separates "wanting" (cue-triggered motivation, dopamine-dependent, robust) from "liking" (hedonic impact, generated by small opioid and endocannabinoid "hotspots", not dopamine-dependent). The design risk the theory names is amplifying cue-triggered wanting without any matching gain in liking; the human evidence for the separation is mostly indirect.

### Cited Findings
- [Review, mostly Animal] Berridge and Robinson (2016, American Psychologist 71:670): "'wanting' is generated by large and robust neural systems that include mesolimbic dopamine. By comparison, 'liking' ... is mediated by smaller and fragile neural systems, and is not dependent on dopamine." — [Berridge and Robinson 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5171207)
- [Review] Their incentive-sensitization theory holds that the core of drug addiction is "excessive amplification specifically of psychological 'wanting'", especially wanting triggered by cues, without a necessary rise in liking. They state the theory's scope "is now expanding to include diverse behavioral addictions" (the authors' own claim about scope) — [Berridge and Robinson 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5171207)
- [Animal, Review] Hedonic hotspots are small subregions (nucleus accumbens medial shell, ventral pallidum, and sites in orbitofrontal cortex, insula and brainstem) where opioid and related stimulation increases "liking" reactions to sweetness. These come from rodent microinjection studies — [Berridge lab overview](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/research-overview/neuroscience-of-linking-and-wanting/); [Berridge and Kringelbach 2015, Pleasure systems in the brain](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425246/)
- [Review, Animal] Berke's review agrees on the dissociation from the dopamine side: dopamine-depleted rats still "show signs of enjoying" food placed in the mouth; what they lose is the willingness to work for it — [Berke 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6358212)
- [Review, Animal and Human PET] Uncertainty feeds wanting. Anselme and Robinson (2013, Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience 7:182) argue dopamine is crucial to coding reward uncertainty, cite work in which accumbens dopamine in people with gambling problems was maximal when winning and losing were equally likely, and conclude that "losses contribute to motivate gambling more than gains" — [Anselme and Robinson 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3845016)
- [Human behavior + fMRI] A human example of wanting rising while liking falls: in Clark et al. (2009), near-misses that the player had control over were rated less pleasant than full misses (t39 = -2.75, p = 0.009) and yet increased the desire to keep playing (t39 = 2.66, p = 0.011), n = 40 — [Clark et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2658737)
- [Expert commentary] Masud Husain (Oxford neurologist), writing for a general audience: neuroscientists broadly agree dopamine does not cause pleasure, and "There certainly is no evidence that we experience dopamine 'hits' or 'rushes' every time we repeatedly engage with social media or use our phone apps." — [Husain, Lit Hub](https://lithub.com/a-complex-yet-crucial-chemical-exposing-myths-about-dopamine/)

### Inferences
- A practical test for any reward element: does it make the completed act feel better (liking), or does it mainly pull the person back to the app (cue-triggered wanting)? A reward that sits at the end of a real training session or habit, and is felt as pleasant and deserved, leans toward liking. Notifications, badges on the icon and "your streak is at risk" prompts are cues, and they act on wanting.
- The Clark result is the clearest human warning sign to watch for in design review: an element that people rate as unpleasant but that still raises the urge to continue is working through wanting alone.
- The liking systems are described as small and fragile, so pleasure from a reward moment should be expected to be modest and to fade with repetition. A design should not count on a large hedonic effect.

### Gaps
- Direct human evidence that separates wanting from liking at the level of brain systems is limited in the sources read. Most of it is rodent work with food and drugs.
- No study was found that measures liking and wanting separately for digital rewards in a habit app.
- Whether "behavioral addiction" framing applies to non-gambling apps is contested, and I found no reliable source that settles it. The notes make no claim either way.

## 3. Timing of feedback: discounting, immediacy, and the windows that link an action to its feedback

### Takeaway
Immediate feedback is the better-supported design choice at every timescale: behavior studies show immediate (not delayed) rewards predict persistence in long-term goals; human learning shifts from striatal to hippocampal systems when feedback is delayed by seconds; and for a touch, feedback should arrive within roughly 100 ms (tactile within about 50 ms) to feel caused by the tap. The popular "two brain systems, impulsive versus patient" story (McClure 2004) is contested.

### Cited Findings
- [Human fMRI] McClure, Laibson, Loewenstein and Cohen (2004, Science 306:503): limbic regions tied to the midbrain dopamine system "are preferentially activated by decisions involving immediately available rewards", while lateral prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex "are engaged uniformly by intertemporal choices irrespective of delay"; more fronto-parietal activity went with choosing the later option — [McClure et al. 2004](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/separate-neural-systems-value-immediate-and-delayed-monetary-rewa/)
- [Human fMRI] The critique. Kable and Glimcher (2007, Nature Neuroscience) found that activity in ventral striatum, medial prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate "tracks the revealed subjective value of delayed" monetary rewards, that is, one valuation system for both immediate and delayed rewards, and later stated their results were inconsistent with the separate-systems hypothesis — [Kable and Glimcher 2007](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/44455)
- [Human behavior + fMRI] Kable and Glimcher (2010, the "as soon as possible" effect) reported behavior inconsistent with quasi-hyperbolic (beta-delta) models: people discount relative to the soonest available option, not relative to "now" (snippet level) — [Kable and Glimcher 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2867580)
- [Human behavior] Woolley and Fishbach (2017, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 43:151), five studies, about 449 people (students, gym-goers, museum visitors): "the presence of immediate rewards is a stronger predictor of persistence in goal-related activities than the presence of delayed rewards". This held for New Year's resolutions, single sessions of studying and exercising, and over 1 week and 3 months, even though people said they pursued the goals for delayed benefits. Gym-goers who enjoyed their workouts spent more time exercising. These are mostly correlational and self-report designs (sample figure is snippet level) — [Chicago Booth release](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/implement-immediate-rewards-as-you-pursue-that-long-term-goal); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/achieve-long-term-goals-focus-short-term)
- [Human behavior, experimental] Woolley and Fishbach (2018, JPSP 114:877, DOI 10.1037/pspa0000116), five experiments: an earlier bonus increased intrinsic motivation for the task; the effect ran through a stronger activity-reward association; timing mattered independently of size. In a reading task, an immediate versus delayed bonus led to a 35 percent increase in the number of people who kept reading after the reward was removed, a larger increase than a bigger reward produced (figures from press coverage) — [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/06/180606143709.htm); [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2018/06/its-about-time-immediate-rewards-boost-motivation)
- [Human behavior] A 2025 year-long follow-up (Giurge, Woolley and Fishbach) found intrinsic motivation predicted self-reported resolution success and completion, extrinsic motivation did not, and the effect replicated in China (press coverage) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/what-really-keeps-people-committed-to-long-term-goals-this-study-offers-a-key-insight/)
- [Human fMRI + patients] Foerde and Shohamy (2011, Journal of Neuroscience 31:13157): healthy people showed striatal activation when learning from immediate feedback and hippocampal activation when learning from delayed feedback, and delayed feedback events were better remembered as episodes. A later study reported the same pattern for prediction error responses (greater in striatum when the outcome is immediate, greater in hippocampus when delayed) — [Foerde and Shohamy 2011](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/31/37/13157); [Timescales of learning commentary](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2013.00098/full)
- [Animal, cellular] A biological reason seconds matter: Yagishita et al. (2014, Science 345:1616) found dopamine promoted enlargement of dendritic spines in mouse striatal neurons only when it arrived 0.3 to 2 seconds after the glutamatergic input, strongest at 0.6 s. This is slice physiology in mice — [Yagishita et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4225776)
- [Human behavior, Review] Intentional binding: when a voluntary action causes an outcome, the action and outcome are perceived as closer together in time (Haggard, Clark and Kalogeras 2002, Nature Neuroscience). Moore and Obhi's 2012 review calls the link between binding and the sense of agency "compelling, though not yet fully explained" — [Moore and Obhi 2012](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/6464)
- [Human behavior] Temporal contingency between action and feedback is a cue for the sense of agency, and delay reduces it, but "the quantitative relationship between delay and SoA depends on task settings" (Wen 2019, as cited by later papers) — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.580441); [Predictive Wand paper citing Wen 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11872)
- [HCI, verified abstract] Touchscreen button feedback latency guidelines from Kaaresoja's University of Glasgow thesis (simultaneity perception and perceived quality studies): "visual feedback latency should be between 30 and 85 ms, audio between 20 and 70 ms and tactile between 5 and 50 ms" for single-modality feedback — [Kaaresoja thesis, Latency guidelines for touchscreen virtual button feedback](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/7075)

### Inferences
- Three nested timing layers follow from this evidence. Within about 100 ms of the tap: the acknowledgment (press state, haptic) so the act feels self-caused. Within roughly 0.3 to 2 seconds: the reward moment itself, so it is tied to the act and not to something later. Within the same session: any summary of what was earned. Rewards that only appear on a later visit are more likely to be learned as facts about the app than felt as consequences of the act (inference from Foerde and Shohamy; the Yagishita window is mouse slice data and should be cited only as a plausible mechanism).
- The Woolley and Fishbach work suggests the reward that matters most for persistence is the immediate experience of the activity. A reward system should attach to and amplify the moment of doing the habit or finishing the workout. It should not postpone the payoff to a weekly tally.
- Do not describe the app as "balancing the impulsive brain against the rational brain". The dual-system reading of McClure 2004 is disputed by Kable and Glimcher.

### Gaps
- The exact immediate and delayed intervals in Foerde and Shohamy 2011 could not be retrieved (PubMed blocked the fetch). A commentary mentions a 7 s delay in the group's 2013 study.
- I could not retrieve Wen 2019 itself, so no specific delay threshold for loss of agency is given.
- The specific action-outcome interval used in Haggard et al. 2002 was not confirmed.
- No study was found that tests reward delay (for example 0.2 s versus 2 s versus next day) on habit adherence in an app.

## 4. Schedules and uncertainty in humans: variable versus fixed schedules, uncertainty, near-misses, novelty, and what happens when rewards become expected

### Takeaway
Uncertainty about reward does raise dopamine-related activity in monkeys and engages human striatum, and mild uncertainty about reward size can increase effort in lab tasks, but the strong version ("variable rewards hook the brain") rests on animal schedules, gambling studies and small human samples. Near-misses are the clearest harm pattern: they recruit win circuitry and raise the urge to continue while feeling worse, though whether they actually prolong play is itself disputed.

### Cited Findings
- [Animal] Fiorillo, Tobler and Schultz (2003, Science): in monkeys, the phasic dopamine response to a cue rose monotonically with reward probability, and a separate sustained response grew "until the potential time of reward" and was largest at probability 0.5, that is, at maximal uncertainty. The authors suggested a role in "attention-based learning and risk-taking behavior" — [Fiorillo et al. 2003](https://www.pdn.cam.ac.uk/system/files/documents/2003-fiorillo-science.pdf)
- [Animal, debate] The sustained signal's meaning is disputed. An alternative account says it reflects backpropagating prediction errors and not uncertainty; the original authors defended the uncertainty reading — [Fiorillo, Tobler and Schultz commentary, Behavioral and Brain Functions](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/1744-9081-1-7.pdf)
- [Human fMRI] Preuschoff, Bossaerts and Quartz (2006, Neuron 51:381): in a card gambling task with expected reward and risk (variance) varied independently, activity in dopamine target structures tracked both. The expected-reward signal was immediate and the risk signal was delayed — [Preuschoff et al. 2006](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/118390?ln=fr)
- [Human PET] Zald et al. (2004, Journal of Neuroscience): raclopride PET while healthy adults did card tasks for money. A variable-ratio schedule (25 percent reward rate, outcome unknown in advance) had "a complex and regionally specific influence on dopamine transmission"; a fixed-ratio schedule produced only modest changes versus control. This is not a clean "variable beats fixed" result — [Zald et al. 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6729274); [Vanderbilt release](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/2004/05/07/its-a-gamble-dopamine-levels-tied-to-uncertainty-of-rewards-59664/)
- [Human behavior] The motivating-uncertainty effect. Shen, Fishbach and Hsee (2015, Journal of Consumer Research 41:1301), four studies with real rewards: people "invest more effort, time, and money to qualify for an uncertain reward (e.g., a 50% chance at $2 and a 50% chance at $1) than a certain reward of a higher expected value (e.g., a 100% chance at $2)". Boundary: "This effect arises only when people focus on the process of pursuing a reward, not when they focus on the outcome". Mechanism offered: uncertainty "generates positive experience such as excitement" — [Shen, Fishbach and Hsee 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/679418); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/uncertainty-can-improve-motivation)
- [Human behavior + fMRI, verified] Near-misses. Clark, Lawrence, Astley-Jones and Gray (2009, Neuron 61:481). Behavioral experiment n = 40; fMRI n = 15 (non-problem gamblers). Slot task: wins of £0.50 on 1/6 of trials, near-misses on 2/6, full misses on 3/6. Near-miss versus full-miss activated bilateral ventral striatum (Z = 4.30 and 4.25, FWE-corrected p = 0.005 and 0.006) and right anterior insula (Z = 3.63, p = 0.049), regions that also responded to wins. Near-misses were less pleasant but raised desire to play, only when the participant had chosen the gamble; computer-chosen near-misses reduced desire to play. Insula response to near-misses correlated with a gambling cognitions scale (Z = 3.98, FWE p = 0.018) — [Clark et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2658737)
- [Human fMRI] A follow-up reported that people with gambling problems show stronger midbrain responses to near-misses than casual gamblers — [Society for Neuroscience summary of Chase and Clark 2010](https://www.sfn.org/publications/latest-news/2010/05/04/problem-gamblers-provoked-by-near-misses-to-gamble-more)
- [Human and Animal behavior, contested] Pisklak, Yong and Spetch (2020, Journal of Gambling Studies 36:611) reviewed the literature and ran two experiments (pigeons, then humans). "Both experiments failed to support the near-miss effect hypothesis", and their review found "problems and inconsistencies in the previously cited evidence for a near-miss effect" on persistence. Their human sample was not problem gamblers — [Pisklak et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31522339/). A 2017 systematic review reached a different conclusion, that near misses motivate continued play but have varying effects on emotion and betting — [2017 systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/5663799)
- [Human fMRI] Novelty. Bunzeck and Düzel (2006, Neuron 51:369) found human substantia nigra/VTA responds to stimulus novelty in an oddball task, coding absolute novelty, and suggested this could support learning in novel contexts — [EurekAlert summary](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/580149)
- [Human fMRI, verified] The exploration bonus. Wittmann, Daw, Seymour and Dolan (2008, Neuron 58:967), n = 15: in a four-armed bandit with novel and familiar pictures matched for payoff, people valued novel options more (about £0.41 versus £0.37 initial value, p = 0.01; a bonus of about 4 pence on a £1 prize). Right ventral striatum tracked both the standard prediction error and the extra error from the novelty bonus. Limits stated: small sample, uncorrected whole-brain threshold with small-volume correction, dopamine inferred and not measured, low-stakes safe context — [Wittmann et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2535823)
- [Animal, Review] When rewards become expected: the outcome-time response disappears and moves to the cue, and a smaller-than-expected reward produces a dip (see section 1) — [Schultz 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4826767)
- [Human behavior, field data] Reward rate shapes digital behavior in a way reinforcement learning models describe. Lindström et al. (2021, Nature Communications): over one million posts from over 4,000 people; posting timing fit a model in which people maximize the average rate of social reward (they post sooner when likes are high); an online experiment (n = 176) showed likes causally changed posting. This is about "likes" from other people, not self-tracked points — [Lindström et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-19607-x)

### Inferences
- The defensible version of "variable rewards" for a personal app: keep the fact of the reward certain and contingent on the act, and allow modest, upward-only variation in its form or size. That draws on the Shen, Fishbach and Hsee result (uncertain magnitude can motivate while the person is in the process) without building a gamble.
- The Shen boundary condition matters: the effect shows up when attention is on the process, not when people stop to evaluate the outcome. A multiplier the user calculates in advance is an outcome-focus feature and should not be expected to get this benefit.
- Avoid near-miss constructions: "one more and you would have had it", almost-full meters that reset, a reel or reveal that stops just short. The Clark finding says they raise the urge to continue while feeling bad, and the effect needs a sense of personal control, which a habit app always supplies. That the behavioral effect is disputed (Pisklak) is not a reason to use them. It is a reason not to claim they "work".
- Novelty gives a small, real bonus to exploration in the lab (about 10 percent of initial value in Wittmann). New reward visuals or a fresh short challenge can be expected to draw a little extra interest, and that interest will fade as they become familiar. Novelty should be treated as a short-lived aid.
- Honest framing of streak-warning messages: they are cues that create uncertainty about a loss, which the Anselme and Robinson review ties to wanting. Use sparingly.

### Gaps
- No controlled human study was found comparing fixed versus variable reward schedules on real-world habit adherence or training adherence in an app.
- The classic schedule effects (variable-ratio schedules resist extinction) come from animal operant work; I did not retrieve a modern human replication with non-monetary rewards.
- The Bunzeck and Düzel details (sample size, statistics) were not retrieved from the paper itself.
- Whether the sustained uncertainty signal of Fiorillo 2003 exists as such in humans is not established by the sources read; Preuschoff 2006 is the closest, and it is BOLD in a gambling task.

## 5. Goal pursuit: goal gradient, progress, effort and its valuation, self-efficacy, and loss aversion applied to streak-loss framing

### Takeaway
People speed up as a goal gets closer and respond even to artificial head starts; effort is weighed against reward by a circuit including dorsal anterior cingulate and ventral striatum, yet effort can itself become valued when it is what gets rewarded. The neural case for "losses hurt twice as much" is weaker than its reputation: the best-replicated finding is reduced value-region activity for losses, not a fear response, and intact streaks motivate while broken streaks demotivate.

### Cited Findings
- [Human behavior, field] Goal gradient. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006, Journal of Marketing Research 43:39): café customers bought coffee more often the closer they were to a free one; people rating songs for a reward persisted longer as they approached it; and a 12-stamp card with 2 bonus stamps was completed faster than a plain 10-stamp card (12.7 versus 15.6 days) although both needed 10 purchases ("illusionary goal progress"). Stronger acceleration predicted retention and faster re-engagement — [Kivetz et al. 2006, Columbia](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/going-goal)
- [Animal] A candidate neural correlate of goal proximity is the dopamine ramp in rats approaching distant rewards (Howe et al. 2013), which scaled with distance and reward size. Whether ramps exist in this form in humans pursuing abstract goals is not shown — [Howe et al. 2013](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/91049)
- [Human fMRI] Effort valuation. Croxson et al. (2009, Journal of Neuroscience 29:4531): cues signaled the effort required and the reward on offer. Ventral striatum and midbrain activity tracked net value (expected reward discounted by effort); dorsal anterior cingulate reflected the interaction of reward and effort; posterior orbitofrontal and insular activity reflected reward magnitude only — [Croxson et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2954048)
- [Human drug + PET] Westbrook et al. (2020, Science 367:1362), 50 young adults: willingness to do demanding working-memory tasks for money was greater in people with higher striatal dopamine synthesis capacity; methylphenidate and sulpiride raised cognitive motivation more in those with lower capacity; gaze data suggested dopamine increased the weight on benefits versus costs — [Westbrook et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32193325/)
- [Review] The effort paradox. Inzlicht, Shenhav and Olivola (2018, Trends in Cognitive Sciences): dominant models "assume that effort is inherently costly", yet "Effort adds value to the products of effort, but effort itself also has value", and this value can be accessed during, after, and "in anticipation of such expenditure" — [Inzlicht, Shenhav and Olivola 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/6172040/)
- [Theory, Animal and Human] Learned industriousness. Eisenberger (1992, Psychological Review 99:248): "reinforcement for increased physical or cognitive performance ... conditions rewards value to the sensation of high effort and thereby reduces effort's aversiveness", and this generalizes across behaviors — [Eisenberger 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1594725/)
- [Human behavior, experimental, with replication] Clay, Mlynski, Korb, Goschke and Job (2022, PNAS 119(5)): people rewarded in proportion to effort (not performance) on a cognitive task later chose harder tasks in a transfer phase with no reward, compared with controls whose reward was unrelated to effort (total reward held equal). Five further online experiments with 1,457 participants tested replication and generalization — [Clay et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8812552)
- [Human fMRI] Loss aversion's neural basis. Tom, Fox, Trepel and Poldrack (2007, Science 315:515): in accept-or-reject mixed gambles, value regions (ventromedial prefrontal cortex, ventral striatum) increased with gains and decreased with losses, with a steeper slope for losses ("neural loss aversion"). They did not find amygdala or insula activation increasing with losses — [Tom et al. 2007 as summarized in a later review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4312134); [Tom et al. 2007 PDF](https://stanford.edu/~knutson/bad/tom07.pdf)
- [Human fMRI, multi-team replication, verified] NARPS (Botvinik-Nezer et al. 2020, Nature 582:84): 108 participants did the mixed gambles task; 70 teams analyzed the same data. Share of teams reporting a significant result: negative effect of losses in vmPFC 84.3 percent (equal indifference design) but 32.9 percent (equal range design); positive effect of gains in vmPFC 37.1 and 21.4 percent; gains in ventral striatum 22.9 and 32.9 percent; positive effect of losses in amygdala 5.7 percent in both designs. On average 20 percent of teams reported a result different from the majority. The data descriptor notes the "relatively low power of the Tom et al. study" — [Botvinik-Nezer et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7771346); [NARPS data descriptor](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-019-0113-7)
- [Human behavior, Meta, contested] The size of behavioral loss aversion is disputed. One meta-analysis (Brown, Imai, Vieider and Camerer) pooled 607 estimates from 150 articles and reports a mean coefficient of 1.955 (95% interval 1.82 to 2.10); another (Walasek, Mullett and Stewart) estimates 1.31 (1.10 to 1.53) across 19 datasets (both figures from a secondary summary) — [summary of both meta-analyses](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/loss-aversion/)
- [Human behavior, contested] Gal and Rucker (2018) argued loss aversion has been overgeneralized and that much evidence reflects status quo bias or inertia; Mrkva et al. (2020) replied with large samples showing average loss aversion above 1, with moderators — [Mrkva, Johnson, Gächter and Herrmann](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/preview/3681607/Mrkva-Johnson-Gaechter-Herrmann_2019-11-20.pdf). A 2022 study found "no loss aversion" in five studies (n = 2,001) when gains and losses were presented symmetrically in random order — [Judgment and Decision Making 2022](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/22/220414/jdm220414.html)
- [Human behavior, experimental] Streaks directly. Silverman and Barasch (2023, Journal of Consumer Research 49:1095), seven studies: "intact streaks highlighted via behavioral logs increase consumers' subsequent engagement in that behavior, relative to when broken streaks are highlighted", "independent of actual past behavior". The effect "is amplified when consumers attribute a break in the streak to themselves rather than to external factors, and attenuated when consumers can 'repair' a broken streak" — [Silverman and Barasch 2023](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- [Human fMRI] Failure feedback hurts less when the person chose. Murayama et al. (2015, Cerebral Cortex 25:1241): in a stopwatch game, failure feedback lowered vmPFC activity under forced choice but not under self-determined choice, and that resilience tracked the performance gain from choice — [Murayama et al. 2015](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/36405/)

### Inferences
- A visible, honest progress indicator toward a near goal is supported by the goal-gradient field data. Short challenges (days, not months) keep the person in the steep part of the gradient more of the time. The "bonus stamps" result shows a head start works, but it works by creating a false sense of progress. A personal app that values trust should prefer real progress markers, for example counting what the person has already done this week.
- Reward effort and showing up, not only outcomes. The Clay et al. result (the strongest human evidence here, with large-sample replications) says rewarding effort can make effort itself more attractive later, including without reward. For training, that argues for points or acknowledgment tied to completing the hard session rather than to pace or weight.
- Streak-loss framing: the evidence does not support leaning on fear of loss. The neural "loss as threat" story (amygdala) failed in NARPS; the behavioral coefficient is contested; and the direct streak study shows a broken streak lowers later engagement, most when the person blames themselves. The supported design moves are (a) make the intact streak visible, (b) offer a way to repair, and (c) frame a break as external or neutral (rest day, illness, travel), not as failure.
- Multipliers tied to streak length raise the cost of a break as the streak grows. By the Silverman and Barasch result that also raises the demotivation when it breaks. A multiplier that decays gently or is restored quickly after a break fits the evidence better than one that resets to zero.
- Letting the person choose the challenge may buffer the sting of a missed day (Murayama 2015), though that study is a single lab task.

### Gaps
- Tom et al. 2007's sample size, the behavioral loss-aversion coefficient and the brain-behavior correlations could not be verified (the PDF host returned an error). Do not quote specific numbers from that paper without checking.
- Self-efficacy and mastery: no neuroscience source was retrieved in this pass. Bandura's self-efficacy work is behavioral and belongs to the psychology notes; I found no imaging study that would justify a neural claim about mastery feedback beyond the competence-feedback findings in section 6.
- No human imaging study was found of goal-proximity signals during a multi-day habit streak.
- Progress monitoring as such (for example a meta-analysis of monitoring and goal attainment) was not searched in this pass.
- The two loss-aversion meta-analysis figures come from a secondary summary and should be checked against the primary papers before being quoted.

## 6. Intrinsic motivation in the brain: undermining, competence feedback, choice, curiosity

### Takeaway
There is real but thin imaging evidence that performance-contingent money can reduce later voluntary engagement and the striatal response to success once the money stops (one n = 28 study, not cleanly replicated), and consistent evidence that informational feedback, choice and curiosity engage the same valuation circuitry as money. The behavioral meta-analyses support the direction: expected, tangible, contingent rewards tend to undermine free-choice engagement, while positive competence feedback enhances it.

### Cited Findings
- [Human fMRI, verified] Murayama, Matsumoto, Izuma and Matsumoto (2010, PNAS). n = 28 students, 14 per group. Stopwatch task (stop within 50 ms of 5 s). Reward group got 200 yen per success; controls got a matched amount unrelated to performance. In a free-choice period the reward group played the task less (group effect F(1,26) = 6.59, p = 0.016). Anterior striatum response to success feedback: reward group greater than control in session 1 (t26 = 3.30, p < 0.01); after the reward was removed in session 2 the reward group's response fell to non-significance (paired t13 = 7.35, p < 0.01 for the drop) while the control group's was sustained. Similar pattern in midbrain and, at task cue, right lateral prefrontal cortex. Caveats: whole-brain threshold uncorrected p < 0.001 with small-volume correction; the brain-behavior test was one-tailed (beta = -0.49, p = 0.037); small single-site sample — [Murayama et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3000299)
- [Human fMRI] A larger follow-up with a different design did not reproduce the drop. Albrecht, Abeler, Weber and Falk (2014, Frontiers in Neuroscience), n = 64, monetary and verbal rewards: "we did not find a decrease in activation after reward withdrawal". It also noted the 2010 study was then the only human imaging study of tangible rewards and motivation — [Albrecht et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4166960/)
- [Human behavior, Meta] Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999, Psychological Bulletin), 128 studies: engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36 and -0.28), as did all tangible rewards and all expected rewards; engagement- and completion-contingent rewards also undermined self-reported interest (d = -0.15 and -0.17); "Positive feedback enhanced both free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31)" (figures from the abstract as returned by search) — [Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)
- [Human behavior, Meta, contested] The opposing reading: Cameron, Banko and Pierce (2001) argue negative effects are limited to high-interest tasks when rewards are tangible, expected and loosely tied to performance level — [Cameron, Banko and Pierce 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2731358)
- [Human fMRI] Feedback alone engages the striatum. Tricomi and colleagues found the caudate was more active when people did a perceptual task with feedback than without, and the right caudate distinguished positive from negative feedback, with no money involved; they note "the intrinsic value of feedback may be quite potent to motivated learners" — [Tricomi et al. 2006](https://stanford.edu/%7Ejlmcc/papers/TricomiEtAl06.pdf)
- [Human fMRI, verified] The value of choice. Leotti and Delgado (2011, Psychological Science), n = 18: a cue signaling an upcoming choice (versus no choice, matched for expected monetary value) was liked more (3.9 versus 3.1 on a 5-point scale, t(17) = 2.14, p < .05) and produced more anticipatory activity in right ventral striatum (t(17) = 3.4, p = .004), left ventral striatum (p = .03) and midbrain (p = .005). People chose the path leading to future choice 64 percent of the time. Authors' limits: small sample; could not rule out effects of uncertainty or predictability; orbitofrontal effect not significant — [Leotti and Delgado 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3391581)
- [Human fMRI] Murayama et al. (2015, Cerebral Cortex): choosing one's own stopwatch (a choice irrelevant to difficulty) improved performance; vmPFC activity dropped after failure only under forced choice; striatal responses to success and failure were not changed by choice — [Murayama et al. 2015](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/36405/)
- [Human fMRI + behavior] Curiosity as reward. Kang et al. (2009, Psychological Science 20:963), fMRI n = 19 (sample figure from a secondary account): curiosity about trivia questions correlated with activity in caudate regions tied to anticipated reward; people spent scarce tokens or waiting time to get answers they were more curious about; higher curiosity predicted better recall of surprising answers 1 to 2 weeks later. A commentator notes the nucleus accumbens was not activated and the left caudate is active in many non-reward tasks — [Kang et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/); [Nautilus commentary](https://nautil.us/?p=236809)
- [Human fMRI] Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath (2014, Neuron): in high-curiosity states, anticipatory midbrain and hippocampal activity and their connectivity predicted better memory, including for unrelated material shown during the wait. "Dopaminergic" in the title is an inference from BOLD — [Gruber et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494). A 2021 preprint reports a partial replication (greater VTA activation during answer anticipation and better recall under high curiosity) — [bioRxiv 2021](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.07.15.452391v1.full-text)

### Inferences
- The undermining risk for this app is specific: a reward that is expected, tangible-feeling, and contingent on doing the habit can, when it stops or is devalued, leave the habit feeling less worth doing. Points that are plainly symbolic, that carry information ("that was your fourth run this week"), and that are never withdrawn are closer to "positive competence feedback" (d about +0.3) than to "performance-contingent pay" (d about -0.3 to -0.4). That mapping is an inference; no study tested app points directly.
- Reward moments should say something true about competence or progress. Informational feedback engages the striatum without money, and positive feedback is the one reward type the meta-analysis found to enhance intrinsic motivation.
- Give the person real choices (which challenge, which habit counts, whether a multiplier is on). Choice is valued in itself and may buffer failure. Keep the choices few; the studies used simple two-option choices.
- A small element of "what will it be?" before a reveal draws on the curiosity findings, and curiosity states improved memory for whatever was shown during the wait. This is a mild effect from trivia studies and should be described that way.

### Gaps
- No direct, preregistered replication of Murayama et al. 2010's striatal result was found. The only larger imaging study found did not show the post-withdrawal decrease, though its design differed.
- The exact effect size for expected tangible rewards as a combined category in Deci et al. 1999 was not retrieved.
- No imaging study was found on the undermining effect with non-monetary digital rewards such as points or badges.
- The Kang et al. 2009 sample sizes for the behavioral follow-ups were not confirmed from the paper.

## 7. Multisensory feedback: combining visual, haptic and audio, synchrony windows, and perceived quality

### Takeaway
Touchscreen studies support adding haptic and audio feedback to a visual response: it improves accuracy and perceived quality when the modalities are congruent and tightly timed. The working tolerances from HCI research are tactile within about 5 to 50 ms of the touch, audio within 20 to 70 ms, visual within 30 to 85 ms, with people generally more tolerant when touch leads sound than the reverse.

### Cited Findings
- [HCI, verified abstract] Kaaresoja (University of Glasgow thesis; builds on Kaaresoja, Brewster and Lantz 2014, ACM Transactions on Applied Perception 11(2), "Towards the Temporally Perfect Virtual Button"). Single-modality guidelines: "visual feedback latency should be between 30 and 85 ms, audio between 20 and 70 ms and tactile between 5 and 50 ms." Bimodal guidelines: "visual feedback latency should be 95 and audio 70 ms when the feedback was visual-audio", "visual 100 and tactile 55 ms when the feedback was visual-tactile", and "tactile 25 and audio 100 ms when the feedback was tactile-audio". The guidelines combine simultaneity-perception thresholds and perceived-quality ratings — [Kaaresoja thesis](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/7075)
- [HCI, vendor review] An Immersion review of automotive touch interfaces states haptic latency below about 30 to 50 ms is generally perceived as instantaneous, and satisfaction drops as delay grows. This is a haptics vendor's document and a rule of thumb, not a threshold measurement — [Weddle et al. 2013, Immersion](https://www.immersion.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/UX-Impacts-of-Haptic-Latency-in-Automotive_Weddle_2013.pdf)
- [HCI] Audio-haptic asynchrony on a steering wheel (2023): average detection threshold 123 ms when haptic led audio and 88 ms when audio led haptic; roughly half of participants noticed asynchrony outside about -75 to +100 or 110 ms — [Detection Threshold of Audio Haptic Asynchrony in a Driving Context](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.05451)
- [HCI] Audio-tactile delay with a virtual button (IEEE Transactions on Haptics, 2023): thresholds of 179 ms when audio came first and 451 ms when haptic came first, read as reflecting the everyday experience that touch precedes sound (snippet level) — [Temporal Detection Threshold of Audio-Tactile Delays With Virtual Button](https://aminer.org/pub/6447afd771ac66d2cbd32f55/temporal-detection-threshold-of-audio-tactile-delays-with-virtual-button)
- [HCI] Hoggan, Brewster and Johnston (2008, CHI): adding tactile feedback to a touchscreen keyboard significantly improved finger-based text entry, bringing it close to a physical keyboard, in static and mobile settings — [Hoggan et al. 2008](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/43812). A vendor blog reports accuracies of about 89.6 percent (physical), 80 percent (haptic touchscreen) and 65.8 percent (plain touchscreen); not verified against the paper — [Boréas summary](https://pages.boreas.ca/blog/piezo-haptics/studies-show-haptics-can-improve-typing-accuracy-and-reduce-input-errors-on-smartphone)
- [HCI] Cross-modal congruence. A 2008 study of touchscreen widgets found perceived quality ratings of buttons correlated with how congruent their visual style, sound and tactile feedback were — [Crossmodal congruence: the look, feel and sound of touchscreen widgets](https://www.aminer.org/pub/53e9af0cb7602d9703943c9a/crossmodal-congruence-the-look-feel-and-sound-of-touchscreen-widgets)
- [HCI] Merchel, Altinsoy and colleagues (TU Dresden, 2009 and 2011): touchscreens with tactile or audio-tactile feedback were rated higher in overall quality than no feedback; the frequency content of sound and vibration affects how suitable the pairing feels; and sound biases the perceived intensity of the haptic — [Merchel et al. 2009](https://tu-dresden.de/ing/elektrotechnik/ias/aha/ressourcen/dateien/professur/publikationen/Merchel2009c_-_Audiotactile_feedback_for_touch_screens.pdf); [Altinsoy 2011](https://tu-dresden.de/ing/elektrotechnik/ias/aha/ressourcen/dateien/professur/publikationen/Altinsoy2011d_-_Application_of_Audiotactile_Intensity_Interaction_on_Touch-Screens.pdf)
- [Perception] In visuotactile simultaneity experiments the point of subjective simultaneity tends to be tactile-leading, and the temporal binding window depends on the task (simultaneity versus order judgments give different answers) and widens with spatial congruence — [McMaster thesis on visuotactile integration](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/items/27f1ec9b-b0e0-4e80-835f-c856aeca1a81/full); [The Visuotactile Temporal Binding Window Widens with Spatial Congruency](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/42rmvPB4)

### Inferences
- Order of arrival for a reward moment triggered by a tap: haptic first (target under about 50 ms from touch), sound if any within about 70 ms, visible change beginning within about 85 to 100 ms. If something must lag, let the visual or the sound trail the haptic, not lead it. People tolerate touch-then-sound far better than sound-then-touch.
- For a longer celebratory animation, the haptic pattern should be locked to visible events within the animation (impact frames) to within a few tens of milliseconds. The detection thresholds found range from about 75 ms up, so a 20 to 50 ms tolerance is a conservative target. This applies thresholds measured for button presses and steering wheels to an animation, which is an extrapolation.
- Congruence matters as much as presence: a soft visual with a sharp buzz, or a large visual burst with a faint tick, will read as lower quality. Intensity of sound and haptic should rise and fall together.
- In a Capacitor web view the path from touch to native haptic adds latency that the lab studies did not have. Measuring the actual delay on device matters more than the nominal design value.
- Haptics and sound should be optional and respect system settings. The evidence is for perceived quality and accuracy, not for motivation or adherence.

### Gaps
- No study was found linking haptic or multisensory reward feedback to habit adherence, motivation or striatal response. The evidence is about perceived quality, simultaneity and input accuracy.
- Specific visuotactile simultaneity thresholds (point of subjective simultaneity and just-noticeable difference in ms) from the perception literature were not retrieved.
- The Kaaresoja figures are from the thesis abstract; the individual thresholds behind them were not read.
- No latency study on modern iPhone haptics (Taptic Engine) through a web view was found.

## 8. Translation limits: what comes from animals or from money and food in scanners, what has been shown for symbolic or digital rewards, and which popular claims overreach

### Takeaway
Most of the mechanism evidence is from monkeys and rodents working for juice or food, or from small human samples playing for money in scanners; symbolic rewards (feedback, reputation, likes) do engage human striatum and shape behavior, but no study found here measured brain responses to points, badges, streaks or reward animations in a habit app, and gamification meta-analyses show small to medium, unstable effects on motivation and behavior. "Dopamine hit" and "variable rewards hook the brain" are not supported as stated.

### Cited Findings
- [Translation] The core prediction error findings (no response to fully predicted reward, transfer to cue, omission dip) are from electrophysiology in monkeys receiving juice — [Schultz 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4826767)
- [Translation] Human fMRI measures BOLD in dopamine target regions, not dopamine. Schultz writes that the human imaging signal "reflects the dopamine response" in striatal and frontal terminal areas; Wittmann et al. list as a limitation that the dopaminergic origin is inferred — [Schultz 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4826767); [Wittmann et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2535823)
- [Translation] fMRI findings in this field are sensitive to analysis choices. In NARPS, 70 teams analyzing one dataset disagreed substantially on five of nine hypotheses, and prediction error meta-analysis maps vary with model fitting and smoothing — [Botvinik-Nezer et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7771346); [Chase et al. 2015](https://typeset.io/pdf/reinforcement-learning-models-and-their-neural-correlates-an-3u0pkk6dgd.pdf)
- [Translation] Sample sizes in the classic human studies cited in these notes: Clark 2009 fMRI n = 15; Wittmann 2008 n = 15; Leotti and Delgado 2011 n = 18; Murayama 2010 n = 28 (14 per group); Kishida 2016 n = 17 patients — [Clark](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2658737); [Wittmann](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2535823); [Leotti and Delgado](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3391581); [Murayama](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3000299); [Kishida](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4711839)
- [Human fMRI] What has been shown for non-monetary, symbolic rewards: (a) performance feedback without money engages the caudate — [Tricomi et al. 2006](https://stanford.edu/%7Ejlmcc/papers/TricomiEtAl06.pdf); (b) a good reputation (positive evaluations from supposed strangers) activated striatum overlapping with money, n = 19, read as a "common neural currency" — [Izuma, Saito and Sadato 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18439412/); (c) the opportunity to choose engages ventral striatum — [Leotti and Delgado 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3391581); (d) curiosity about answers engages caudate and midbrain — [Kang et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/); [Gruber et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494)
- [Human behavior] Likes on social media shape posting in a way a reward-rate learning model captures, and an experiment (n = 176) showed the effect is causal. This is social approval from other people — [Lindström et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-19607-x)
- [Meta] Gamification of learning (Sailer and Homner 2020, Educational Psychology Review 32:77): the authors report the effect on cognitive learning outcomes was stable in a subsplit of rigorous studies, while "effects on motivational and behavioral outcomes were less stable"; for behavioral outcomes, game fiction and social interaction were significant moderators. Secondary summaries give pooled effects of g = 0.49 (cognitive), 0.36 (motivational) and 0.25 (behavioral) — [Sailer and Homner 2020](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)
- [Overreach: "dopamine hit"] Dopamine is not the pleasure signal. Berridge and Robinson: liking "is not dependent on dopamine" — [Berridge and Robinson 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5171207). Berke: dopamine-depleted rats still show enjoyment; dopamine tracks whether effort is worth expending — [Berke 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6358212). Husain: "There certainly is no evidence that we experience dopamine 'hits' or 'rushes' every time we repeatedly engage with social media or use our phone apps." — [Husain, Lit Hub](https://lithub.com/a-complex-yet-crucial-chemical-exposing-myths-about-dopamine/)
- [Overreach: "variable rewards hook the brain"] The supporting evidence is: a sustained uncertainty-related dopamine signal in monkeys whose interpretation is disputed — [Fiorillo et al. 2003](https://www.pdn.cam.ac.uk/system/files/documents/2003-fiorillo-science.pdf); [commentary](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/1744-9081-1-7.pdf); a human PET study in which a variable-ratio schedule had "a complex and regionally specific influence" — [Zald et al. 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6729274); and lab behavior showing more effort for uncertain reward magnitude only under process focus — [Shen, Fishbach and Hsee 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/679418). Even the near-miss effect, the best-known "hook", failed to change persistence in a 2020 experimental test — [Pisklak et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31522339/)
- [Overreach: "loss aversion is hardwired fear"] The amygdala loss hypotheses were supported by 5.7 percent of NARPS teams — [Botvinik-Nezer et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7771346)
- [Overreach: "two brain systems fight over now versus later"] Disputed by single-valuation-system findings — [Kable and Glimcher 2007](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/44455)
- [Overreach: "dopamine is settled science"] Leading researchers disagree about whether dopamine signals prediction error, value, or retrospective causal association, and one concedes "no model thus far can explain all the observations" — [Uchida 2024](https://www.thetransmitter.org/dopamine/does-a-new-theory-of-dopamine-replace-the-classic-model/); [Jeong et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abq6740)

### Inferences
- A strength-of-evidence ranking for the app's design choices, from the sources above:
  - Strong, human, behavioral, experimental or replicated: immediate reward increases persistence and intrinsic interest (Woolley and Fishbach 2018); rewarding effort increases later choice of effort (Clay 2022, n > 1,400 in replications); intact streaks motivate and broken ones demotivate, repair helps (Silverman and Barasch 2023); goal-gradient acceleration (Kivetz 2006); positive competence feedback helps and expected contingent tangible rewards can undermine (Deci 1999, contested at the margins); tight multisensory timing and congruence improve perceived quality (Kaaresoja; Hoggan).
  - Moderate, human, small-sample imaging or lab tasks with money: prediction error in striatum; novelty bonus; value of choice; curiosity as reward; near-miss recruitment of win circuitry.
  - Weak for this purpose, animal or cellular: the 0.3 to 2 s plasticity window; dopamine ramps toward goals; the sustained uncertainty signal; hedonic hotspots.
  - Not shown at all: any neural effect of app points, badges, streak counters, multipliers or reward animations; any adherence benefit of variable over fixed rewards in a habit app; any motivational effect of haptics.
- Language the product documents can defend: "feedback arrives right away so the action and its result feel connected"; "progress is shown because people speed up as a goal gets closer"; "rewards mark effort and consistency"; "a missed day can be repaired". Language to avoid: "dopamine hit", "hooks", "rewires", "brain-based", "scientifically proven to build habits", and any statement about what a specific brain chemical does when the user sees an Orb.
- Because this is a personal app for one adult who sets the rules, the usual gamification risks from social comparison and paid rewards are absent. The remaining risks named by the evidence are cue-driven checking (wanting without liking), demotivation after a self-attributed streak break, and the reward becoming the reason for the habit (undermining).

### Gaps
- No imaging, PET or physiological study of points, badges, streaks, multipliers or completion animations in a consumer habit or fitness app was found. This absence is the most important translation limit and should be stated plainly in the report.
- Sailer and Homner's pooled effect sizes are from secondary summaries and should be checked against the paper. Their meta-analysis covers learning contexts, not exercise or habit apps; a meta-analysis specific to gamified physical activity apps was not searched in this pass.
- The "dopamine hit" critiques cited are expert commentary for a general audience (Husain) plus peer-reviewed reviews that make the underlying scientific point (Berridge and Robinson; Berke). I did not find a peer-reviewed paper whose main purpose is to test the phrase as used about apps.
- Long-term habituation to a reward system in an app (months) and whether refreshed novelty counters it: no source found.
- Individual differences (for example novelty seeking, or sensitivity to streak loss) were not covered.
