# Behavioral evidence on streaks, points, rewards and gamification (as of November 2026)

Notes for the report writer. Evidence labels used below: **RCT** (randomized trial), **Lab/online exp** (randomized experiment, usually MTurk or similar, short horizon), **Meta** (meta-analysis), **Field obs** (observational field data), **Natural exp**, **Company report** (not peer reviewed), **Secondary** (figure taken from a press release or summary, not checked against the paper's tables). Where I read the paper's own text, I say "full text read".

## 1. Streaks: what experiments show about streak displays, streak incentives, broken streaks and what softens a break

### Takeaway
Showing an intact streak raises the chance of doing the behavior next time, and showing a broken streak lowers it below showing nothing at all in some studies; the break hurts most when people blame themselves and much less when the streak can be repaired or when slack ("emergency skips") is built in up front. Slack that is framed as a reserve with a small cost beats both a strict every-day goal and an easier goal in a 5-week step field experiment, mostly because people keep going the day after a miss.

### Cited Findings

**Silverman and Barasch (2023), "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", Journal of Consumer Research 49(6):1095-1117. Seven studies plus supplements. Full text of author manuscript read.**
- Definition and core claim: a streak is the same behavior three or more times in a row; highlighting an intact streak in a behavioral log increases later engagement compared with highlighting a broken streak, and the effect depends on how the log represents the behavior, not on what the person actually did. Mechanism: people treat keeping the logged streak as a goal in itself. Source: [abstract, CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [author manuscript, UDSpace](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Pilot survey (N = 100 MTurk): participants named 101 unique apps that show streaks (39% named health and fitness apps); 59% said they had "gone out of their way" to keep a streak; 27% had done something offline purely to keep a logged streak (one did "a quick 7-minute workout in the back of a bar"). Participants did not report feeling overwhelmed or bothered by streak information (means below 2.6 on a 7-point scale). Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Study 1 (**Field obs**, not randomized): 980 university employees in a 30-day, 7,000-steps-a-day challenge with an app showing "days in a row". Controlling for each person's overall success rate, people were more likely to hit the goal on a day when they had an intact streak than after a broken one (contrast b = 0.38, SE .03, p < .001). In separate dummies, an intact streak helped (b = 0.25) and a just-broken streak hurt four times as much (b = -1.01, SE .07, p < .001). The authors state this is correlational. Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Study 2 (**Lab/online exp**, N = 601, exercise): 66.2% chose to continue the target exercise type with an intact logged streak vs 57.9% with a broken one (OR = 1.43, p = .035). Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Study 3 (N = 602, language-learning app paradigm): continuing the same language was 78.3% with an intact streak vs 53.3% with a broken one. Showing the log mattered in both directions: with an intact streak, 92.5% continued when the streak was highlighted vs 64.9% when it was not (OR = 6.63); with a broken streak, 45.2% continued when the break was highlighted vs 60.9% when it was not. So highlighting a break is worse than saying nothing. Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Study 4 (N = 601): intact streak 65.8%, no log 54.7%, broken streak 48.0% (intact vs broken OR = 2.09, p < .001). Study 5 (N = 805): intact 82.6%, no log 77.8%, broken 67.9% (intact vs broken OR = 2.23). The authors note the separate effects of "intact vs no log" and "broken vs no log" were not consistently significant across studies. Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Study 6 (N = 802, attribution): continuing was 53.2% with an intact streak, 42.0% when the break was attributed to an external cause (app's fault), and 28.9% when the break was attributed to the participant (intact vs self-attributed OR = 2.80, p < .001). 45% were willing to watch an ad to maintain a streak and 43% to repair one. Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Study 7 (N = 601, repair): continuing was 93.1% intact, 68.7% broken, 85.2% broken but repairable (repairable vs broken OR = 2.63, p < .001; intact vs repairable still OR = 2.36). Repair recovered about two-thirds of the gap but not all of it; the authors suggest a repaired streak may feel less authentic. Mediation ran through reduced sense of accomplishment/goal failure, not through negative emotion. Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Streak length: in two supplementary studies (3 vs 6 in a row, N = 596; 4 vs 20 in a row, N = 507) the intact-vs-broken effect did not differ by length (interaction ps > .40). The authors say this cannot speak to very long streaks (e.g. 100+). Public vs private logs (Study S4, N = 604): no significant interaction (OR 1.72 public vs 1.52 private). Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Authors' design advice (their inference, partly tested): avoid dwelling on broken streaks, take some of the blame off the user, offer repair, and consider broader definitions of what counts, while noting that broader definitions "may also decrease the value and meaning of maintaining a streak". Silverman has separately suggested apps avoid "you broke your streak" notifications (her suggestion, not a tested result). Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb); [UDaily](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)

**Mehr, Silverman, Sharif, Barasch and Milkman (2025), "The motivating power of streaks: Increasing persistence is as easy as 1, 2, 3", OBHDP 187:104391. Six preregistered studies, combined N = 4,493.**
- "Streak incentives" (pay that rises with each consecutive task completed) led people to complete more work than larger, stable pay per task. Proposed mechanism: consecutive structure raises commitment to the goal of maximizing earnings. These are paid micro-task experiments, not health behaviors, and short horizon. Source: [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v187y2025ics0749597825000032.html); [University of Alberta summary](https://www.ualberta.ca/en/folio/2025/03/hot-streaks-boost-productivity.html)

**Weathers and Poehlman (2024), "Defining, and understanding commitment to, activity streaks", Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science 52(2):531-553. Survey, experimental and field data.**
- An activity streak has fixed performance and time rules, is a perception (people decide whether a series counts), is attributed to the holder's resolve, and has a quantified length. It differs from a habit (automatic), a winning or lucky streak, and a collection (which does not end if you skip). Source: [Clemson News, author summary](https://news.clemson.edu/keeping-a-streak-alive-can-be-strong-motivation-to-stick-with-a-chosen-activity/)
- Commitment to a streak is higher when the activity is identity-relevant, when the person believes it leads to self-improvement, and when the person has a high need for structure. Design levers that raised commitment: framing as a daily task (vs a cumulative total), presenting it as a short-term goal (vs indefinite), and letting the person keep it private (vs shared). Source: [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/joamsc/v52y2024i2d10.1007_s11747-023-00944-4.html)

**Sharif and Shu: emergency reserves (slack with a small cost).**
- Sharif and Shu (2017), Journal of Marketing Research 54(3):495-509: goals with an emergency reserve keep a hard reference point but add slack; people prefer them and persist more. Source: [EconBiz record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/the-benefits-of-emergency-reserves-greater-preference-and-persistence-for-goals-that-have-slack-with-a-cost-sharif-marissa/10011697492)
- Field experiment (**RCT**, full text of working paper read; also Study 1 of the 2021 OBHDP paper): 316 signed up, 273 completed a baseline week and were randomized, 28 dropped. Daily step goal = 120% of own baseline. Four weekly goals: Hard (hit step goal 7 days), Easy (5 days), Reserve-Weekly (7 days with 2 emergency skips per week, no rollover), Reserve-Monthly (7 days with 8 skips across 4 weeks). Days per week goal reached: Hard 2.83, Easy 3.11, Reserve-Weekly 4.00, Reserve-Monthly 3.82 (all Reserve vs non-Reserve contrasts p < .03). Mean daily steps: Easy 6,662, Hard 6,678, Reserve-Weekly 7,754, Reserve-Monthly 7,981. Headline: "up to 20% more steps" and goal reached "up to 40% more days". Source: [working paper PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf)
- Persistence the day after a miss (same study): probability of hitting the goal the day after failing was .37 Hard, .44 Easy, .55 Reserve-Weekly, .48 Reserve-Monthly. When a skip was applied, next-day success was .55/.54 vs .37 for Hard (p ≤ .002). After a miss with no skip left, Reserve participants persisted about the same as others (no backlash). Source: [working paper PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf)
- Moderator: the more flexible monthly pool helped mainly people with higher trait self-control (interaction p = .050); the weekly reserve helped at all levels. Self-reported weight change favored Reserve-Weekly (-3.6 lb vs roughly 0 in other arms; self-report, preliminary). Source: [working paper PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf)
- Sharif and Shu (2021), "Nudging persistence after failure through emergency reserves", OBHDP 163:17-29 (one field study, four lab studies; full text read). After a forced subgoal failure, 73.2% persisted with a Reserve goal vs 63.0% Hard and 65.2% Easy (N = 903; p = .008 and .036). 87.5% of Reserve participants chose to use the reserve. Mechanism: reserve replaces a sense of failure with perceived progress, which raises commitment. Source: [paper PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf); [CHIBE listing](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/nudging-persistence-after-failure-through-emergency-reserves/)
- Timing matters (Study 4a, N = 402): after failing, 84.7% with an emergency point tried the next task vs 60.2% Hard, 64.0% Easy and 66.3% with a free "bonus" point given at the start; only 4.1% of Reserve participants gave up entirely vs 18 to 23% elsewhere. Study 4b (N = 196): applying the reserve right after the failure gave 78.2% persistence vs 54.7% when it was applied before the failure. So a head start is not the same as forgiveness at the moment of the miss. Source: [paper PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf)
- Caveat: a small Utrecht student-thesis replication in dieting found no significant persistence advantage for emergency reserves after subgoal failure (not peer reviewed, small sample). Source: [Utrecht thesis](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/44466)

**"What the hell" effects and goal violation.**
- Soman and Cheema (2004, Journal of Consumer Research): violating a goal, when coded as failure, can make later performance worse than having no goal at all (e.g. people who missed a self-set deadline took longer to finish than people with no deadline). Cochran and Tesser (1996) named the "what the hell" effect: a small lapse on a proximal, inhibitory goal leads to abandoning it. Both are cited by Sharif and Shu and by Silverman and Barasch as the mechanism behind streak-break drop-off. Source: [Sharif and Shu 2021](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf); [PsyBlog summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=13881)
- Zemack-Rugar and colleagues (2014) report that a person's response to an initial slip is a stable tendency across goal domains (Secondary). Source: [Virginia Tech news](https://news.vt.edu/articles/2014/05/050214-pamplin-failureresponse.html)
- The classic dieting version has been challenged: a popular summary notes Tomiyama et al. (2009) found the effect may be weaker than thought outside the lab (Secondary). Source: [PsyBlog summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=13881)

**Getting people back after a miss (megastudy).**
- Milkman et al. (2021), Nature 600:478-483 (**RCT megastudy**, 61,293 members of 24 Hour Fitness, 54 four-week programs). The top performer gave 125 bonus points (about $0.09) for returning after a missed planned workout: +27% weekly gym visits during the program. 45% of interventions raised visits by 9 to 27% during the four weeks, but only 8% produced measurable change after the four weeks ended. Source: [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v600y2021i7889d10.1038_s41586-021-04128-4.html); [Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/wharton-study-best-ways-boost-workout-habits)

**What happens when a streak counter is removed.**
- Moldon, Strohmaier and Wachs (2021, ICSE; **Natural exp**): when GitHub removed streak counters from profiles in May 2016 without notice, many long ongoing streaks were abandoned, weekend activity fell, and single-contribution days became less common. The authors read this as evidence that the counter was driving minimal "keep it alive" contributions. Source: [arXiv 2006.02371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.02371)

**A single missed day and habit formation.**
- Lally et al. (2010, European Journal of Social Psychology; 96 people, 12 weeks, **Field obs**): automaticity plateaued after a mean of 66 days (range 18 to 254); "missing one opportunity did not significantly impact the habit formation process", though very inconsistent performers did not form habits (Secondary: UCL press office). Source: [UCL News](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)

**Company reports (not peer reviewed).**
- Duolingo's growth team reports that a "Streak Wager" raised Day-7 retention by 14% and describes a "Weekend Amulet" that protects streaks over weekends. Silverman and Barasch note Duolingo sells a "streak freeze" for in-app currency. I found no published A/B result for the streak freeze itself; a "21% churn reduction" figure circulates without a primary source. Source: [Duolingo blog](https://making.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals); [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)

**Failure after a stretch target can hurt the most invested.**
- Wang, Lewis, Cryder and Sprigg (2016, Marketing Science; **Field RCT**, 95,532 hotel loyalty members): a bonus-points promotion with a personal stretch goal raised purchases overall, but only about 20% hit the goal; hitting it raised later purchasing and missing it lowered later purchasing, most strongly among top-tier members. Source: [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.2015.0966)

### Inferences
- The cost of a break is asymmetric: in the one field dataset the negative coefficient for "just broke a streak" was about four times the positive coefficient for "streak intact", and in Study 3 a highlighted break did worse than no log. A design that shows "days in a row" should not also announce the break. Showing nothing, or showing what was done, is the safer default after a miss.
- The three softeners with experimental support are (a) external or shared attribution of the miss (28.9% to 42.0%), (b) repair (68.7% to 85.2%), and (c) pre-declared slack with a small cost (next-day success .37 to .55). Slack applied at the moment of failure works better than a head start given in advance.
- A weekly reserve that does not roll over ("7 days, 2 skips a week") is the most robust form: it helped at all self-control levels, whereas a larger flexible pool helped mainly high self-control people.
- Counting "days done" (a cumulative total that cannot be lost) rather than "days in a row" is an untested inference from these papers: it removes the goal-violation moment. Weathers and Poehlman found daily framing raises commitment to a streak, which suggests a pure cumulative count gives up some pull. A reasonable combined design is a consecutive count with built-in slack plus a never-reset total.
- Streak effects appear categorical once a streak exists (3 vs 6 vs 20 made no difference), so there is no evidence that making streaks longer adds motivation, and there is some evidence (GitHub) that long streaks produce token effort to keep the number alive.
- Private streaks produced more commitment than shared ones (Weathers and Poehlman) and the public/private interaction was null in Silverman and Barasch, so a single-user app loses little by having no social streak.
- A small reward for coming back after a miss (megastudy's best arm) is direct support for a "return bonus" rather than a penalty.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for Mehr et al. (2025) per study (percent more tasks completed) were not retrieved; only the direction and N are sourced.
- No randomized field trial was found that compares a consecutive-day streak display against a "days done" total, or that tests streak freezes in a health app. The Duolingo evidence is company-reported.
- No evidence was found on very long streaks (100+ days) and the size of the drop when they break; Silverman and Barasch flag this as open.
- The Silverman and Barasch experiments are single-session online studies with choices between tasks; only Study 1 is real behavior over 30 days and it is correlational.

## 2. Gamification meta-analyses and trials in health, fitness and learning: pooled effects, durability and which elements carry them

### Takeaway
Gamification gives a small-to-medium lift in physical activity (g about 0.4 overall, about 0.2 against a non-gamified active control, roughly +1,600 steps a day in pooled trials) that shrinks to a very small effect (g about 0.15) three to six months after it stops. No meta-analysis can say which element does the work; in the Penn trials, points and levels with loss framing, weekly resets and a social hook worked, and competition lasted longest.

### Cited Findings

**Mazeas, Duclos, Pereira and Chalabaev (2022), JMIR 24(1):e26779. Meta of RCTs; 16 studies, N = 2,407. Full text read via PMC.**
- Pooled effect on physical activity: Hedges g = 0.42 (95% CI 0.14 to 0.69), I² = 74%. Against inactive controls g = 0.58 (0.08 to 1.07); against active controls (non-gamified app or self-monitoring) g = 0.23 (0.05 to 0.41). Source: [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/e26779); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8767479/)
- Steps: mean difference +1,609.56 steps/day (95% CI 372 to 2,847; 7 studies, I² = 86%). MVPA effect not significant (g = 0.31, -0.19 to 0.80; 4 studies). Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8767479/)
- Follow-up: all follow-up data g = 0.09 (-0.07 to 0.26), not significant; in the sensitivity analysis restricted to follow-up 12 to 24 weeks after the intervention ended, g = 0.15 (0.07 to 0.23), I² = 0%. Authors' reading: weaker but present, so not only novelty. Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8767479/)
- Robustness: excluding high or unclear risk-of-bias studies the effect was not significant (g = 0.33, -0.16 to 0.81). Trim-and-fill adjusted g = 0.24 (-0.24 to 0.73), not significant. Median study power 63%. Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8767479/)
- Moderators (all null): age, gender, BMI, intervention duration (β = -.01, p = .74) and number of game mechanics (β = .01, p = .91). Interventions under 12 weeks: g = 0.44 (0.19 to 0.69); 12 weeks or longer: g = 0.41 (-0.19 to 1.01), not significant. Authors suggest short, repeated "doses" may be better than one long run. Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8767479/)
- Elements used across the 18 reviewed trials: badges/rewards 13, teams/leagues 13, levels 9, points 7, leaderboards 7; social incentives in 15 of 18. The review "cannot determine" whether game mechanics, behavior change techniques or social interaction drive the effect. Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8767479/)

**Other pooled estimates.**
- A 2022 meta-analysis of standalone gamified smartphone apps found a small-to-moderate between-group effect on physical activity (SMD 0.34). Source: [PubMed 34893387](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34893387/)
- Nishi et al. (2024, eClinicalMedicine 76:102798; Meta of RCTs of 8+ weeks in adults, gamified vs non-gamified apps): gamification "results in slight improvements" in physical activity and adiposity versus non-gamified versions. Source: [PubMed 39764571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39764571/)
- A 2025 JMIR Serious Games meta-analysis in cardiovascular disease reported a maintenance effect of g = 0.20 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.29; 3 studies, follow-up averaging 2.5 months; prediction interval -0.01 to 0.41). Source: [JMIR Serious Games](https://games.jmir.org/2025/1/e64410/PDF)
- Johnson, Deterding et al. (2016, Internet Interventions 6:89-106; systematic review, 19 papers): 59% reported positive and 41% mixed effects of gamification on health and wellbeing; results clearer for behavior than for cognitive outcomes; evidence quality mostly moderate or lower, with few comparisons against a non-gamified version of the same intervention. Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6096297)

**Learning.**
- Sailer and Homner (2020, Educational Psychology Review 32:77-112; Meta, 38 publications, 40 experiments): small significant effects on cognitive (g = .49), motivational (g = .36) and behavioral (g = .25) learning outcomes (g values from secondary summaries, consistent across them). The cognitive effect was stable in high-rigor studies; motivational and behavioral effects were less stable. For behavioral outcomes, game fiction and combining competition with collaboration were significant moderators. Source: [University of Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)

**Penn trials (Patel and colleagues): points, levels, loss framing.**
- BE FIT (Patel et al. 2017, JAMA Internal Medicine; **RCT**, 200 adults in 94 Framingham families; 12-week intervention, 12-week follow-up). Mechanics: 70 points endowed each week, 10 lost for each day the goal was missed (loss framing); weekly level up or down; five "lifelines" per member for sick days; prize was a coffee mug. Result: step goals met on 53% vs 32% of participant-days; change from baseline +1,661 vs +636 steps/day during the intervention; during follow-up +1,385 vs +798 (still significant, smaller). Source: [Penn Medicine release](https://pennmedicine.org/news/news-releases/2017/october/to-increase-exercise-study-shows-gaming-strategies-and-a-buddy-are-key); [AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/using-gamification-and-social-incentives-to-increase-physical-activity)
- STEP UP (Patel et al. 2019, JAMA Internal Medicine 179(12):1624-1632; **RCT**, N = 602 overweight adults, Deloitte employees in 40 states; 24-week intervention, 12-week follow-up; full text read). Mechanics in all game arms: self-chosen step goal (33%, 40% or 50% above baseline, or custom at least +1,500), signed pledge, 4-week ramp, 70 points each Monday with 10 lost per missed day, five levels starting in the middle (silver), weekly reset of points ("fresh start"), and a reset to silver at weeks 8 and 16 for people in the bottom two levels. Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420/)
- STEP UP results vs control, steps/day: competition +920 (95% CI 513 to 1,328), support +689 (267 to 977), collaboration +637 (258 to 1,017) during weeks 5 to 24; in follow-up competition +569 (142 to 996; p = .009), support +428 (19 to 837; p = .04, which the published abstract and press coverage treat as not significantly different from control, consistent with a multiplicity-adjusted threshold), collaboration +126 (ns). No significant differences among the three game arms during the intervention. No health outcomes measured. Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31498375/)
- BE ACTIVE (Fanaroff, Patel et al. 2024, Circulation; **RCT** in adults at elevated cardiovascular risk with under 7,500 daily steps; 950 completed the 12-month intervention and 6-month follow-up; mean age 67). Versus control over 12 months: gamification +538 steps/day, loss-framed financial incentive +492, both combined +868 (all significant). At 6-month follow-up only the combined arm stayed significantly ahead (+576). Source: [NIH release](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/short-term-incentives-exercise-can-lead-sustained-increases-activity); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/cardiology/20240407/paid-incentives-games-boost-daily-steps-for-people-at-high-risk-for-heart-disease)

**Single-element field evidence.**
- Hamari (2017, Computers in Human Behavior 71:469-478; pre/post field experiment, N = 1,410 before and 1,579 after, one year each): adding badges to a peer-to-peer marketplace raised listings, transactions, comments and total activity. An earlier 2013 study on the same kind of service found that simply adding badges did not raise activity overall; only users who looked at their own badges were more active. Source: [Tampere gamification group](https://webpages.tuni.fi/gamification/2018/09/20/badges-increase-user-activity-a-field-experiment-on-the-effects-of-gamification/)

### Inferences
- A realistic expectation for a points/levels layer on top of plain tracking is the active-control figure, g about 0.2, not the headline 0.4; the app already has tracking, so the marginal gain from gamification is the smaller number.
- The Penn recipe that has replicated three times is not "points" in the abstract. It is: a personal goal set relative to baseline, points given up front that are lost on a miss, a weekly reset so a bad week does not carry over, levels that start mid-ladder, lifelines for sick days, and someone else who sees the result. For a one-person app, the social arm is not available, which matters because competition was the only arm with a robust post-game effect.
- Durability is the weak point everywhere: effects roughly halve or vanish within 3 to 6 months of the game ending, and trials over 12 weeks show no clear benefit. Short, bounded challenges with a rest between them fit the data better than a permanent game layer (the Mazeas authors suggest this; it is not directly tested).
- Number of mechanics did not predict effect, so one or two well-made elements are as defensible as many.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis isolates points, levels or streaks individually; component trials are called for but scarce.
- Almost all trials measure steps; evidence for strength training, running plans or general daily habits (journaling, reading) is thin.
- Exact pooled figures from Nishi et al. 2024 were not retrieved, and the Sailer and Homner g values come from secondary summaries.
- The BE FIT and BE ACTIVE numbers come from press releases and abstracts, not the papers' tables.

## 3. Rewards and motivation: overjustification, cognitive evaluation theory and what self-determination theory implies for apps

### Takeaway
Expected, tangible rewards that are contingent on doing a task reduce later free-choice interest in tasks people already find interesting (d about -0.3 to -0.4), while positive competence feedback raises it (d about +0.3). In gamification experiments, points, levels and leaderboards mostly act as extrinsic incentives that raise the amount of output without changing intrinsic motivation up or down in the short run; badges and progress graphs can support felt competence, and mandatory, comparative setups have backfired over a semester.

### Cited Findings
- Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999), Psychological Bulletin 125(6):627-668. **Meta** of 128 experiments. Free-choice behavior after reward removal: engagement-contingent d = -0.40, completion-contingent d = -0.36, performance-contingent d = -0.28; all tangible and all expected rewards undermined. Self-reported interest: engagement-contingent d = -0.15, completion-contingent d = -0.17. Positive verbal feedback enhanced free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and interest (d = 0.31). Tangible rewards were more harmful for children than for college students; verbal rewards helped children less. Source: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/); [full text copy](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- Context: the 1999 paper was a rebuttal of Cameron and Pierce (1994), who concluded undermining was minimal; the dispute over scope has continued. The undermining studies use tasks that were interesting to begin with and measure behavior after rewards stop. Source: [Deci, Koestner and Ryan 2001](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- Cerasoli, Nicklin and Ford (2014), Psychological Bulletin 140(4):980-1008. **Meta**, k = 183, N = 212,468 across school, work and physical domains. Intrinsic motivation is a medium to strong predictor of performance (ρ = .21 to .45) whether or not incentives are present. It matters less when incentives are directly tied to performance and more when they are only indirectly tied. Intrinsic motivation predicted quality better; incentives predicted quantity better. Source: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24491020/)
- Mekler, Brühlmann, Tuch and Opwis (2017), Computers in Human Behavior 71:525-534. **Online exp** (image-tagging task; points vs levels vs leaderboard vs control). Points, levels and leaderboards increased the quantity of tags but did not affect intrinsic motivation or perceived competence, and did not improve tag quality; the authors conclude that in this context they "functioned as extrinsic incentives, effective only for promoting performance quantity". Their 2013 precursor reached the same "neither make nor break intrinsic motivation" conclusion. Single short session. Source: [paper copy](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf); [2013 precursor record](https://hcds.itu.dk/bibliography/mekler2013points)
- Sailer, Hense, Mayr and Mandl (2017), Computers in Human Behavior 69:371-380. **RCT in an online simulation**. Badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised competence need satisfaction and perceived task meaningfulness; avatars, story and teammates raised relatedness; perceived decision freedom (autonomy) "could not be affected as intended". Conclusion: gamification is not effective per se; specific elements have specific psychological effects. This conflicts in part with Mekler, who found no competence effect. Source: [University of Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109059)
- Hanus and Fox (2015), Computers and Education 80:152-161. Quasi-experiment, 71 students, two course sections, 16 weeks, four measurement points. The section with mandatory badges and a leaderboard showed less motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time than the plain section, and lower final exam scores, which the authors link to lower intrinsic motivation (exam result from a secondary summary). Small sample, not randomized at the individual level. Source: [UCLA resource summary](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf)
- Etkin (2016), "The Hidden Cost of Personal Quantification", Journal of Consumer Research 42(6):967-984. Six **experiments**. Measuring output (steps walked, pages read) increased how much people did but reduced enjoyment, because measurement makes an enjoyable activity feel like work; it also reduced continued engagement once measurement stopped and lowered subjective well-being. The cost disappeared when the activity was framed as a means to a goal the person held (e.g. reading to learn) rather than as fun. Source: [Duke Fuqua summary](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps); [paper PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Etkin-Jordan-11-12-15-Hidden-Cost.pdf)
- Autonomy-supportive language, direct tests: a 2019 JMIR web experiment (N = 526, vegetable intake) crossed autonomy-supportive vs controlling language with choice vs no choice and found no significant effect of language on any outcome; offering choice mattered more, especially for people high in need for autonomy. Source: [PubMed 31670693](https://www.pubmed.gov/31670693/)
- Kinnafick et al. (Journal of Behavioral Medicine; **RCT**, 65 insufficiently active adults, 10 weeks of need-supportive text messages vs neutral): perceived autonomy support and need satisfaction rose, and moderate-intensity activity was higher than control at 4-month follow-up. Source: [Loughborough repository](https://dspace.lboro.ac.uk/dspace-jspui/handle/2134/23467)

### Inferences
- The undermining effect is strongest when (a) the activity was already enjoyable, (b) the reward is expected and tangible, and (c) the outcome is what people do after the reward stops. Symbolic points with no cash value sit between "tangible reward" and "informational feedback"; the gamification experiments suggest they do not measurably damage intrinsic motivation in the short term but also do not build it. What they reliably do is increase quantity, sometimes at the cost of quality (Mekler; GitHub streaks).
- The safest reward form by this literature is informational: it tells the person something true about their competence or progress ("you did what you planned 5 of 7 days"; a graph of their own trend). The riskiest is controlling: required, evaluative, comparative, or framed as something the app will take away.
- Etkin's moderator is directly usable: tie the number to the person's own stated goal (training for a race, building a base), not to the number itself. For a person who already enjoys running, counting it for points risks making it feel like work.
- For things the user finds dull but valuable (a cold-start habit, logging), extrinsic points carry less undermining risk, because there is little intrinsic interest to crowd out. This follows from the theory and from Cerasoli's quantity/quality split; it was not tested directly in the sources retrieved.
- Evidence that wording alone ("should", "must") changes behavior in digital health is weak: the one direct experiment was null. Offering real choice (pick your goal, pick your challenge, opt out) has better support than tone.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis specifically on gamification and basic psychological need satisfaction was retrieved.
- No long-run randomized study was found that measures intrinsic motivation after months of symbolic points in a fitness app and then removes the points. TRIPPA (Section 4) is the closest and uses cash.
- Mekler's and Sailer's effect sizes were not extracted; direction only.

## 4. Reward schedules: variable vs fixed, lotteries, goal gradient, endowed progress, fresh starts, temptation bundling, plans, and evidence for multipliers or bonus periods

### Takeaway
Small, immediate, loss-framed or return-contingent rewards outperform larger delayed ones while they run, but effects usually fade within months of withdrawal; uncertainty can add motivation in short tasks, yet in step trials pure jackpot lotteries failed and only a mixed "frequent small plus occasional larger" lottery worked. Progress framing (goal gradient, a head start, fresh-start resets) is well supported; I found no direct peer-reviewed test of point multipliers or "double points" periods on health behavior.

### Cited Findings

**Framing and schedule of incentives in activity trials.**
- Patel et al. (2016), Annals of Internal Medicine 164:385-394. **RCT**, 281 adult employees, 7,000-step daily goal. Proportion of days goal met: control 0.30, gain incentive ($1.40/day) 0.35, lottery (same expected value) 0.36, loss incentive ($42 given up front, $1.40 removed per missed day) 0.45. Only the loss frame was significantly better than control. Source: [Penn Medicine release](https://www.pennmedicine.org/news/news-releases/2016/february/to-encourage-physical-activity); [CHIBE](https://chibe.upenn.edu/news/loss-incentive-motivates-employees-to-take-more-steps)
- Patel et al. (2018), American Journal of Health Promotion 32(7):1568-1575. **RCT**, 209 adults, daily lotteries. Arms: higher-frequency smaller reward (1 in 4 chance of $5), jackpot (1 in 400 chance of $500), combined (18% chance of $5 plus 1% chance of $50). Goal met on 26% of days in control vs 38% in the combined lottery; only the combined arm beat control (p = .01). The jackpot arm declined significantly over time relative to control (-0.13, p < .001). No differences in follow-up. Source: [CHIBE listing](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/a-randomized-controlled-trial-of-lottery-based-financial-incentives-to-increase-physical-activity-among-overweight-and-obese-adults/)
- Adams et al. (2017), BMC Public Health. **RCT**, N = 96, 4 months, 2x2 (adaptive vs static goals; immediate vs delayed rewards). Immediate small rewards produced +2,762 steps/day from baseline vs +2,016 for delayed (difference 746, p = .009). Source: [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/671a91b2123b4f438a87e367207c93f2)
- Mitchell et al. (2020), British Journal of Sports Medicine 54:1259-1268. **Meta**, 23 studies, N = 6,074. Financial incentives: +607 steps/day during the intervention (95% CI 422 to 792; 12 studies) and +514 (313 to 715; 9 studies) after incentives ended. But by vote count only 4 of 18 studies showed a post-intervention benefit; the authors note the two methods do not align. Incentives were as small as $0.10 to $1.40 a day. Source: [BJSM PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/54/21/1259.full.pdf); [QxMD abstract](https://read.qxmd.com/read/31092399/financial-incentives-for-physical-activity-in-adults-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)
- TRIPPA (Finkelstein et al. 2016, Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology). **RCT**, employees from 13 organizations in Singapore; Fitbit alone, Fitbit plus charity incentive, Fitbit plus cash, or control; incentives for 6 months, follow-up at 12. Cash gave the largest gain at 6 months (about +29 MVPA min/week vs control) but the effect was not sustained 6 months after incentives stopped; the tracker-only and charity arms were ahead of control at 12 months (+37 and +32 min/week) mainly by stemming the control group's decline. About 40% stopped wearing the tracker within 6 months and only 10% still wore it at 12. No improvement in weight, blood pressure or fitness. Press coverage and the linked editorial describe a substantial drop in engagement and activity when cash was removed. Source: [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/788250); [TCTMD](https://www.tctmd.com/news/money-gadgets-incentivizing-healthy-behaviors-questioned-once-again)
- Megastudy (Milkman et al. 2021, see Section 1): micro-rewards worth cents moved gym visits by 9 to 27% for four weeks; only 8% of programs showed lasting change. Source: [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v600y2021i7889d10.1038_s41586-021-04128-4.html)
- Beshears, Lee, Milkman, Mislavsky and Wisdom (2021), Management Science 67(7):4139-4171. **RCT**, 2,508 Google employees. Paying people to exercise in a fixed daily 2-hour window produced fewer lasting gym visits than paying them for exercising at any time; rigid routines did not build stronger habits. Source: [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/unexpected-exercise-advice-for-the-super-busy-ditch-the-rigid-routine); [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v67y2021i7p4139-4171.html)

**Variable or uncertain rewards.**
- Shen, Fishbach and Hsee (2015), Journal of Consumer Research 41(5):1301-1315. Four **experiments** with real rewards. People invested more effort, time and money for an uncertain reward (50% chance of $2, 50% of $1) than for a certain reward of higher expected value ($2 for sure); e.g. more people finished a hard water-drinking task. The effect appears only while people focus on the process of pursuing the reward, not when they focus on the outcome. Short, one-off tasks. Source: [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/679418); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/762247)
- Korinek et al. (2018), Journal of Behavioral Medicine 41(1):74-86. Single-arm system-identification study (small sample of overweight adults) of the Just Walk app: daily step goals and the points on offer varied pseudo-randomly around each person's baseline. Steps rose by an estimated 2,650/day; satisfaction was high and participants "enjoyed receiving variable goals each day". No control group. Source: [journal record](https://thim.mijn.bsl.nl/adaptive-step-goals-and-rewards-a-longitudinal-growth-model-of-d/15066248)

**Progress framing.**
- Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006), Journal of Marketing Research 43:39-58 (**Field exp** and field data). In a "buy 10 coffees, get 1 free" program, time between purchases shortened by about 20% from first to last stamp. A 12-stamp card with 2 stamps pre-filled was completed faster than a plain 10-stamp card (median 10 vs 15 days). After redeeming, purchase speed reset to the slow starting rate ("post-reward resetting"). Source: [Columbia summary](https://business.columbia.edu/insights/chazen-global-insights/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration); [paper PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf)
- Sharif and Shu (2021): a free bonus point at the start did not raise persistence after a failure (66.3% vs 60.2% Hard), while an emergency point applied after the failure did (84.7%). Source: [paper PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf)
- Dai, Milkman and Riis (2014), Management Science 60(10):2563-2582 (**Field obs**: Google searches, university gym records for 11,912 members over 442 days, commitment contracts). Aspirational behavior rises after temporal landmarks. Reported gym-visit increases: about 33% at the start of a week, about 47% at the start of a semester, and smaller rises at the start of a month, a year, and after birthdays (percentages from secondary summaries). Source: [paper PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf); [Coglode summary](https://www.coglode.com/nuggets/fresh-start-effect)
- STEP UP built a weekly fresh start into its points (reset every Monday) and a level reset at weeks 8 and 16. Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420/)

**Temptation bundling and plans.**
- Milkman, Minson and Volpp (2014), Management Science 60(2):283-299. **RCT**, N = 226. Gym-only access to tempting audiobooks raised gym visits by 51% over control, and encouragement to self-restrict by 29% (percentages from a secondary summary, not checked against the paper). Source: [paper PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/TemptationBundling.pdf); [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/health/lead-me-not-into-temptation-for-i-know-the-way)
- Kirgios et al. (2020; Elsevier journal). **Field RCT**, N = 6,792, 4-week program at a gym chain. A free audiobook plus encouragement to bundle raised the likelihood of a weekly gym visit by 10 to 14% and weekly visits by 10 to 12% for up to 17 weeks after the program; explaining the bundling strategy added only a small amount beyond giving the audiobook. Source: [author summary, Rotman Management](https://www.pressreader.com/canada/rotman-management-magazine/20210501/282973927858878)
- Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006): implementation intentions ("if X then I will Y") have a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = 0.65 (as reported in later reviews). For physical activity specifically, Bélanger-Gravel, Godin and Amireault (2013; 26 studies) found 0.31 (95% CI 0.11 to 0.51) post-intervention and 0.24 (0.13 to 0.35) at follow-up; a 2018 PLoS ONE meta of RCTs found 0.25 (0.05 to 0.45) only when plans were reinforced. Source: [PLoS ONE meta, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6235272/); [McMaster summary](https://www.mcmasteroptimalaging.org/full-article/he/meta-analytic-review-effect-implementation-intentions-physical-activity-25322)

**Multipliers and bonus periods.**
- Closest evidence found: (a) streak incentives that pay more per consecutive completion beat larger flat pay in six preregistered experiments (Mehr et al. 2025), which is a multiplier on continuity; (b) a small bonus for returning after a miss was the megastudy's best arm; (c) a stretch-goal bonus-points promotion raised purchasing overall but reduced it among those who tried and failed, especially top-tier members (Wang et al. 2016); (d) a "points plus cash" loyalty promotion raised redemptions but was followed by lower purchase incidence and quantity, a post-promotion dip (Montoya and Flores 2019, Marketing Letters; **Field obs**). Source: [RePEc record, Mehr](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v187y2025ics0749597825000032.html); [INFORMS, Wang](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.2015.0966); [Montoya and Flores PDF](https://www.dii.uchile.cl/~rmontoya/papers/BuyingFreeRewards_MontoyaFlores_ML19.pdf)

### Inferences
- For symbolic points with no cash value, the transferable principles are timing and framing, not amount: reward right away, reward the return after a miss, give a visible head start toward the next threshold, and reset on natural landmarks (Monday, first of the month, start of a training block).
- Loss framing ("you start the week with 70 and lose 10 per miss") is the most effective frame in the Penn trials, but it is the same mechanism that makes a broken streak hurt. In an app that aims not to harm, the lifeline/reserve feature that accompanied loss framing in BE FIT is not optional decoration; it is what makes the loss frame tolerable.
- A multiplier tied to consecutive completions has direct experimental support for raising persistence (Mehr), with two warnings from adjacent evidence: when the multiplier is lost on a miss it compounds the "what the hell" moment (Wang's failure effect was strongest among the most invested), and after any bonus period ends there is likely to be a dip or a reset to baseline speed (Kivetz's post-reward reset; Montoya and Flores). A multiplier that decays gently or is protected by a reserve is the cautious reading; this combination is untested.
- Pure variable-ratio "slot machine" rewards are not supported by the health trials: the jackpot lottery did worse than control over time. Modest uncertainty in the size of a guaranteed reward (Shen et al.) or varied daily goals (Korinek) has some support and was liked by participants.
- Flexibility beats rigid scheduling for lasting behavior (Beshears), which argues for rewarding "did it today" over "did it at 7 am".
- Expect fade. Across incentives, bundling and gamification, the typical pattern is a real effect while the mechanism runs and a smaller or null effect months later. Design for repeated short cycles rather than one permanent mechanic.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed experiment was found that tests point multipliers, "double XP" windows or bonus periods on health or habit behavior. The evidence above is adjacent (pay schemes, loyalty programs).
- No head-to-head trial of variable vs fixed symbolic (non-cash) rewards in a consumer habit app was found.
- Nunes and Drèze (2006, Journal of Consumer Research 32(4):504-512), the endowed progress car-wash study (a 10-stamp card with 2 pre-filled vs an 8-stamp card), is widely reported as roughly 34% vs 19% completion; I could not confirm those figures from a primary source in this session. The parallel Kivetz coffee-card result (10 vs 15 days) is sourced above.
- Exact fresh-start and temptation-bundling percentages are from secondary summaries.

## 5. Goal difficulty and personalized goals: adaptive vs fixed, range goals, goal-setting theory

### Takeaway
Goals set relative to a person's own recent baseline and adjusted over time hold activity better than a fixed target such as 10,000 steps (about +960 steps/day vs fixed in one RCT, a slower decline in another, and more daily activity over 12 months in a third), though fixed goals can produce a bigger initial jump. Range goals ("2 to 4") increase willingness to sign up again compared with a single number, without changing performance.

### Cited Findings
- Locke and Latham (2002), American Psychologist 57(9):705-717 (theory review of about 35 years of work). Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than "do your best": meta-analytic effect sizes range from .42 to .80; goal-difficulty effects range from d = .52 to .82. The relationship holds when people are committed to the goal, have the ability, and get feedback; on complex tasks, learning goals do better than performance goals. Source: [paper copy](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/locke%20pract%20goal%20setting%202002%20am%20psy.pdf)
- Adams et al. (2017), BMC Public Health. **RCT**, N = 96, 4 months, Fitbit. Adaptive vs static step goals. Static goals gave a somewhat larger initial rise (+2,630 vs +2,149 steps/day; difference 482, p = .095), but the adaptive group's steps declined at less than half the rate of the static group across the intervention. Authors recommend adaptive goals. Source: [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/671a91b2123b4f438a87e367207c93f2)
- WalkIT Arizona (McEntee, Adams et al. 2023, IJBNPA 20:97). **RCT**, 512 inactive adults, 12-month 2x2 factorial (adaptive vs static goals of 30 min MVPA on 5+ days; immediate vs delayed reinforcement), plus a follow-up year. Adaptive goals were more effective at increasing the likelihood of any MVPA and daily MVPA minutes, particularly in less walkable neighborhoods. Adaptive plus immediate reinforcement rose by 16.52 MVPA min/day, significantly more than either delayed-reinforcement group. A university summary says adaptive goals helped participants start daily activity about 50% more often than static goals (Secondary). Source: [IJBNPA PDF](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12966-023-01494-2.pdf); [ASU News](https://news.asu.edu/20220120-solutions-study-finds-adaptability-rewards-help-increase-physical-activity)
- Zhou et al. (2018), JMIR mHealth and uHealth 6(1):e28. **RCT**, 64 adults, 10 weeks. Machine-learned personalized daily step goals vs a fixed 10,000-step goal: net difference of 960 steps/day (95% CI 90 to 1,830) in favor of adaptive goals. Both groups declined from run-in; adaptive declined by 390 steps vs 1,350 for fixed. Source: [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5806006/)
- Korinek et al. (2018), Just Walk (single arm, see Section 4): daily goals that varied around the person's baseline, with points on offer, were feasible and well liked; +2,650 steps/day estimated. Source: [journal record](https://thim.mijn.bsl.nl/adaptive-step-goals-and-rewards-a-longitudinal-growth-model-of-d/15066248)
- STEP UP let participants choose their own goal at 33%, 40% or 50% above baseline (or custom), ramped it over 4 weeks, and allowed re-setting at weeks 8 and 16. Sharif and Shu's field study set the daily goal at 120% of the person's baseline week. Source: [PMC, STEP UP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420/); [Sharif and Shu working paper](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf)
- Scott and Nowlis (2013), Journal of Consumer Research 40(3):444-459. Lab and field **experiments**. People with a high-low range goal ("lose 2 to 4 pounds this week") were more likely to re-engage with the goal than people with a single-number goal ("lose 3 pounds"), because the range is both attainable (low end) and challenging (high end), which yields a stronger feeling of accomplishment. In a weight-loss program, range-goal members re-enrolled at higher rates even though average weight loss did not differ. Similar patterns for resisting snacks, puzzles and a shopping game. Source: [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-670766.html); [WashU summary](https://source.washu.edu/2013/06/why-is-it-easier-to-lose-24-pounds-rather-than-3-pounds/)
- Wang et al. (2016): with a stretch target only about 20% reached, failing reduced later engagement (see Section 1). Source: [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.2015.0966)

### Inferences
- The consistent recipe is "a bit above your own recent normal": 120% of baseline (Sharif and Shu), 33 to 50% above baseline reached over a 4-week ramp (STEP UP), or a percentile of the last week or two (Adams). None of the successful trials used a universal number.
- Adaptive goals mostly win by preventing decline, not by producing a bigger peak. That matters for a "keep going" objective.
- Range goals serve retention, not performance. For a habit app the analog is a floor and a stretch ("3 to 5 sessions this week"), where hitting the floor counts as success. Combined with emergency reserves this gives two independent, evidence-backed ways to avoid the all-or-nothing miss.
- Locke and Latham's "specific and difficult" finding is conditional on commitment and feedback, and the consumer evidence shows the cost of failure when a hard target is missed. Letting the user choose among a few difficulty levels (as STEP UP did) supports both commitment and autonomy.

### Gaps
- No trial was found that tests adaptive goals for strength training or for non-exercise daily habits.
- "Just right challenge" as a named construct was not found in the trials retrieved; the adaptive-goal trials are the operational evidence.
- I did not retrieve work on "open goals" (Swann and colleagues) or the Chevance et al. goal-setting studies, so those are not covered.
- Optimal percentile or percentage above baseline has not been compared head to head.

## 6. Harms and limits: compulsive use, anxiety, exercise dependence, eating disorders, crowding out, novelty decay, and ethical guidance

### Takeaway
The best-documented costs are loss of enjoyment from quantification, token "keep the number alive" behavior, fading of gamification benefits over weeks to months, and a rebound when extrinsic incentives are removed; links between trackers and eating-disorder symptoms or compulsive exercise are real as associations but mostly cross-sectional, with randomized studies finding no causal effect in general samples. People with existing eating or exercise pathology, and features that create pressure to hit targets, are the consistent risk factors.

### Cited Findings

**Enjoyment, quantity over quality, crowding out.**
- Measurement raises output but lowers enjoyment and continued engagement, unless the activity is framed as serving the person's own goal (Etkin 2016; six experiments). Source: [Duke Fuqua summary](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps)
- Streak counters produced minimal-effort contributions on GitHub; when counters were removed, single-contribution days and weekend work fell (Moldon et al. 2021; natural experiment). Source: [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.02371)
- 59% of surveyed app users had gone out of their way to keep a streak and 27% had done something offline purely for the log (Silverman and Barasch pilot, N = 100). Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Cash incentives removed after 6 months left no sustained activity gain at 12 months and no health benefit; tracker use fell to 10% by 12 months (TRIPPA RCT). Source: [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/788250)
- Mandatory badges and a leaderboard reduced motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over a 16-week course (Hanus and Fox 2015; quasi-experiment, N = 71). Source: [UCLA resource summary](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf)

**Novelty decay.**
- Koivisto and Hamari (2014), Computers in Human Behavior 35:179-188. Survey of 195 users of the exercise gamification service Fitocracy (**cross-sectional**): perceived enjoyment and usefulness of the gamification decline with time using the service, which the authors read as a novelty effect; ease of use declines with age; women report greater social benefits. Source: [Tampere University](https://researchportal.tuni.fi/en/publications/demographic-differences-in-perceived-benefits-from-gamification/)
- Rodrigues et al. (2022), International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education 19(1). Quasi-experiment, 756 students, 14 weeks, gamified vs non-gamified version of the same system. The gamification effect followed a U shape: it dropped after about four weeks, stayed low for two to six weeks, then partly recovered between weeks six and ten ("familiarization effect"). Overall impact stayed positive. Source: [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6)
- Farzan et al. (2008, CHI, pp. 563-572): a points system for contributing to an IBM internal social network "dramatically increased" contributions on introduction, "followed by a decayed impact" within the first three weeks (as characterized by a later paper from the same group). Follow-up work found a second boost when it was released to new users and that removing the points later reduced participation. Source: [CHI 2008 record](https://scilit.com/publications/8c8b2d7a6b0aa67145628605b72063b7); [IBM Research follow-up](https://research.ibm.com/publications/when-the-experiment-is-over-deploying-an-incentive-system-to-all-the-users)
- In physical activity trials, the pooled gamification effect falls from g = 0.42 to g = 0.15 at 12 to 24 weeks after the intervention, and trials of 12 weeks or longer show no significant pooled effect (Mazeas et al. 2022). In STEP UP two of three arms lost their effect within 12 weeks of the game ending. Source: [PMC, Mazeas](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8767479/); [PMC, STEP UP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420/)
- Megastudy: only 8% of 54 four-week programs had detectable effects after the program ended. Source: [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v600y2021i7889d10.1038_s41586-021-04128-4.html)

**Eating disorders.**
- Simpson and Mazzeo (2017; survey of 493 college students, **cross-sectional**): users of calorie-tracking and fitness-tracking tools reported higher eating-disorder symptomatology. Source: [review in BJPsych Bulletin, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8485346)
- Levinson, Fewell and Brosof (2017; 105 patients at an eating disorder clinic, **cross-sectional**): about 75% used MyFitnessPal and 73% of those users perceived it as contributing to their eating disorder; perceptions correlated with symptoms. Self-perception, not a causal estimate. Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5700836); [Duke Psychiatry summary](https://psychiatry.duke.edu/blog/trouble-tracking)
- Counter-evidence: an experimental study found no causal effect of using MyFitnessPal on eating-disorder symptoms, and a randomized trial in undergraduate women found that introducing calorie self-monitoring had no effect on mental health or health behaviors; a literature summary concludes that tracker users are more likely to have pre-existing symptoms (selection). Source: [review in BJPsych Bulletin, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8485346); [National Center for Health Research summary](https://www.center4research.org/fitness-tracking-apps-eating-disorders/)

**Exercise dependence, compulsion, anxiety.**
- A 2018 survey of 241 adults (IJERPH; **cross-sectional**) classified 8.3% as exercise dependent on the EDS-21 and 13.4% on the Compulsive Exercise Test, and examined preferences for fitness-monitoring technology. Source: [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6313704/)
- A study of 255 endurance athletes (press report; **cross-sectional**) found the highest exercise-addiction risk among those using the most tracking technologies. Source: [Cycling Weekly](https://www.cyclingweekly.com/news/new-research-suggests-strava-technologies-fuelling-addiction-exercise-cyclists-391136)
- In adolescents (2024, International Journal of Adolescence and Youth; **cross-sectional**; outside the adult scope but informative on mechanism), feeling pressure to achieve technology-set exercise goals was associated with higher compulsive exercise, while using technology simply to monitor one's own exercise was associated with positive exercise and wellbeing outcomes. Source: [journal page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02673843.2024.2371397)
- Scudds and Lasikiewicz (2024), Journal of Technology in Behavioral Science: systematic review of wrist-worn trackers in healthy adults (9 studies, only 4 with a suitable control group) concluded the effect of trackers on psychological wellbeing is understudied; it did not establish harm or benefit. Source: [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s41347-024-00442-6)

**Ethical design guidance.**
- HCI work on "attention-capture damaging patterns" lists gamified engagement metrics such as streaks among designs that exploit cognitive and emotional vulnerabilities to maximize engagement (workshop paper; conceptual, no new data). Gray et al. (CHI 2018), "The Dark (Patterns) Side of UX Design", is the standard taxonomy reference. Source: [CEUR workshop paper](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3957/SOCIALIZE-paper07.pdf)
- Silverman and Barasch found that 43 to 46% of participants would watch an advertisement to repair a broken streak and note that firms can "generate revenue" from repair (e.g. paid streak freezes). They frame repair as a welfare gain; the same data show how readily streak loss can be monetized. Source: [author manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)

### Inferences
- The mechanism that separates helpful from harmful tracking in the available evidence is pressure. Monitoring one's own behavior is neutral to positive; targets the system imposes, penalties for missing, and comparison are where compulsion and reduced enjoyment show up. This is consistent with self-determination theory's informational vs controlling distinction, though most harm evidence is cross-sectional.
- For a training app specifically, a daily streak conflicts with sound training: rest days and deload weeks are part of the plan. A streak that counts "did what the plan said today, including rest" or a weekly target with slack avoids rewarding overtraining. No trial tests this; it follows from the token-effort evidence (GitHub, the "7-minute workout in the back of a bar") and the exercise-dependence associations.
- Mechanics that make the risks worse, on this evidence: loss-framed points without lifelines, announcing a break, streaks that can only be repaired by paying or by extra exertion, permanently escalating multipliers, and rewards tied to body weight or calorie numbers.
- Mechanics that reduce risk: private by default, user-chosen goals with an easy way to lower them, pre-declared skips, repair that costs nothing, bounded challenges with an end date, an off switch for the whole reward layer, and feedback that reports what was done rather than what was lost.
- Because novelty fades within roughly 4 to 12 weeks, an honest expectation is that a reward layer buys early momentum; the lasting gains have to come from the habit and from the person's own reasons. Planning refreshes at natural landmarks (new block, new month) matches both the fresh-start evidence and the U-shaped engagement curve.

### Gaps
- No randomized evidence was found on whether streak or point mechanics cause anxiety, exercise dependence or overtraining injuries in adults. The harm literature is survey-based, and the one systematic review found calls the topic understudied.
- I did not locate the Attig and Franke work on dependency on trackers (reduced motivation when not wearing the device) or peer-reviewed studies of streak-related distress in consumer apps (e.g. Duolingo, Snapchat) in this session; these are widely discussed but unsourced here.
- No regulator guidance specific to streaks was retrieved (e.g. FTC or EU positions on engagement mechanics); the dark-pattern sources here are academic and conceptual.
- Evidence on eating-disorder risk concerns calorie and weight tracking far more than activity streaks or points; whether symbolic reward points for training carry similar risk for people with eating disorders is not studied in the sources found.
