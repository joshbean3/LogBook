# How people respond to app screens: evidence review for the Orby redesign (Plan your day, Goals, "What did you learn today?", Fitness)

Evidence grades used on every finding: **[STRONG]** = meta-analysis, large preregistered or large field experiment, or a result replicated by independent teams. **[MIXED]** = real effect in some studies but heterogeneous, moderator-dependent, bias-sensitive, or contradicted by other rigorous work. **[WEAK]** = single small study, qualitative or correlational work, practitioner belief, or vendor claim. Source type is given after the grade (peer-reviewed, vendor, practitioner, press). "Secondary" means I could only confirm the number through a summary, not the paper itself; treat those numbers as needing a check before they are quoted. Research date: 2026-11-12.

## Glanceability: how fast a screen is judged, visual complexity and prototypicality, scan patterns, preattentive attributes, cost of added elements, glanceable displays

### Takeaway
People form a stable appeal judgment of a screen in 17 to 50 ms, and that judgment is driven mostly by low visual complexity and high prototypicality (it looks like what a screen of that kind is expected to look like); complexity matters more than colorfulness. A fast appeal judgment is not the same as comprehension: the evidence for "understood in 5 seconds" is mostly practitioner method, while the academic support for glanceable, single-message, target-relative feedback comes from small but consistent field studies (UbiFit Garden, Gouveia et al.).

### Cited Findings

**First impressions (appeal, not comprehension)**
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed, replicated in direction by Tuch 2012 and Reinecke 2013] Lindgaard et al. (2006, Behaviour and Information Technology 25:115-126) flashed web pages for 50 ms; visual appeal ratings at 50 ms tallied well with ratings after longer viewing. The finding concerns visual appeal only. Lindgaard argued a poor first impression colors later usability and credibility judgments through a halo effect. — [Nature news coverage of Lindgaard 2006](https://www.nature.com/news/2006/060109/full/news060109-13.html); [summary with halo-effect argument](https://old.websiteoptimization.com/speed/tweak/blink)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed, Google/University of Basel] Tuch, Presslaber, Stöcklin, Opwis and Bargas-Avila (2012, Int. J. Human-Computer Studies 70(11):794-811). Study 1: 119 real website screenshots varying in visual complexity (low, medium, high) and prototypicality (low, high), shown for 50, 500 and 1000 ms. Study 2: 17, 33 and 50 ms. Both visual complexity (VC) and prototypicality (PT) affect aesthetic judgments within 17 ms; the PT effect is weaker than VC at the shortest exposures and catches up with longer exposure. Low VC plus high PT was rated most appealing. — [Google Research publication page](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/); [full text PDF](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/38315.pdf); [Google Research blog summary "Users love simple and familiar designs"](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed, CHI 2013] Reinecke et al. rated 450 websites with 548 volunteers after 500 ms exposure. Computed models of perceived visual complexity and colorfulness, plus age and education, explain about half of the variance in appeal ratings. Colorfulness plays a role but "is not nearly as important as the overall visual complexity." — [Reinecke et al. CHI 2013](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2013/reinecke13predicting.shtml)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, abstract-level only] Miniukovich and De Angeli extended automatic complexity and aesthetics metrics to mobile ("Visual impressions of mobile app interfaces", NordiCHI 2014). Their regression models reportedly explained up to 49% of variance in webpage aesthetics but only up to 32% for iPhone app aesthetics (when app genre is included), so the web models transfer to phone screens with less power. — [University of Trento repository listing](https://iris.unitn.it/handle/11572/101623) (figures from a search summary of the listing; full text not checked)

**What guides the eye (preattentive attributes) and the cost of each added element**
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed review, Nature Human Behaviour 2017] Wolfe and Horowitz: attention in visual search is guided by five factors: bottom-up salience, top-down feature guidance, scene structure and meaning, search history, and value. Attention is attracted to items that differ from their surroundings only "if those differences are large enough" and only on a limited set of attributes (color and orientation are named examples); other striking properties are noticed once attended but do not guide attention. Bottom-up salience is "just one of the factors." — [Wolfe and Horowitz 2017 PDF](https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/FiveFactors_Wolfe-Horowitz_2017.pdf)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, CHI 2020] Liu, Gori, Rioul, Beaudouin-Lafon and Guiard, "How Relevant is Hick's Law for HCI?": Hick's law does not justify "less is better" on its own; they argue that modeling visual search and decision making separately is more fruitful than citing Hick's law for interface item counts. — [Liu et al. CHI 2020](https://archivesic.ccsd.cnrs.fr/INFRES/hal-02867301v1)

**Scan patterns and phone glances**
- [WEAK to MIXED, practitioner research (NN/g eye tracking), not peer-reviewed] NN/g reports that eye tracking shows people scan web content in an F-shaped pattern, and published a follow-up on whether it holds on phones. I could confirm only the title-level claim, not the mobile conclusions. — [NN/g, F-Pattern in Reading Digital Content](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/f-pattern-reading-digital-content/)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, Personal and Ubiquitous Computing 2012] Oulasvirta, Rattenbury, Ma and Raita: phone use is dominated by a "checking habit", brief repetitive inspection of dynamic content. The press release says a typical check lasts under 30 seconds and involves a single app. — [Oulasvirta et al. 2012 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2); [Aalto press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/590209)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, MobileHCI 2014] Banovic et al. classify phone sessions by duration and interaction as glance, review and engage sessions. — [ProactiveTasks: the short of mobile device use sessions](https://www.aminer.org/pub/5550491145ce0a409eb73d27/proactivetasks-the-short-of-mobile-device-use-sessions)

**Glanceable displays and feedback**
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, small N] UbiFit Garden (Consolvo et al.): a phone wallpaper garden where flowers stand for types of activity and butterflies for met goals. CHI 2008 three-week field trial with 12 participants focused on experience with sensing. A three-month winter study with 28 participants found that those with the glanceable display maintained their activity level over the holidays, while those without it dropped significantly. — [Consolvo et al. CHI 2008 PDF](https://makeabilitylab.cs.washington.edu/media/publications/Consolvo_ActivitySensingInTheWildAFieldTrialOfUbifitGarden_CHI2008.pdf); [UbiFit project page with the three-month result](https://www.consolvo.org/ubifit); [Flowers or a robot army? UbiComp 2008](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F1409635.1409644)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, small field deployment] Gouveia, Pereira, Karapanos, Munson and Hassenzahl (UbiComp 2016), motivated by evidence that most activity-tracker use is brief glances. They generated 21 concepts and 6 design qualities for glanceable feedback (including being abstract, supporting comparison to targets and norms, and being actionable), built four watch-face prototypes, and found significant differences between them in the wild, "highlighting the surprisingly strong effect glanceable feedback has on individuals' behaviors." An earlier paper from the same group is titled "You have 5 seconds: designing glanceable feedback for physical activity trackers." — [Gouveia et al. UbiComp 2016](https://ktisis.cut.ac.cy/handle/20.500.14279/9337); [You have 5 seconds (2015)](https://ktisis.cut.ac.cy/entities/publication/b363894a-806b-42da-98e6-c9b4bd80c244)
- [WEAK, peer-reviewed press summary] A 2021 LSE-linked study reported that lock-screen checking makes up about a quarter of time on the device, which supports designing for very short visits. — [LSE news](https://www.lse.ac.uk/news/latest-news-from-lse/j-october-21/the-fear-of-missing-out-a-large-amount-of-phone-use-takes-place-on-the-lock-screen)

### Inferences
- The owner's complaint ("my eyes don't immediately understand what's going on") matches the two best-supported levers: lower the visual complexity of each screen and make each screen look like its type (a plan looks like a short list, a goal looks like progress toward a target). Adding color or illustration is a weaker lever than removing elements (Reinecke: colorfulness matters far less than complexity).
- "Visually engaging" and "immediately understood" are compatible only when the engaging element is also the message. UbiFit and Gouveia both made the status itself the picture (garden, watch face) instead of decorating a list. That argues for one glanceable status object per screen (today's plan state, goal progress, this week's training) that reads without text.
- Because guidance by salience only works when one item differs strongly on a preattentive attribute, each screen can have only one thing that is the largest or the only saturated element. Two accent-colored buttons cancel each other. This is a direct argument for one primary action per screen, even though no rigorous A/B evidence for "single CTA" exists (see next section).
- Phone sessions are short checks, so each of the four screens should answer one question in the first screenful: "What do I do next?" (Plan), "Am I on track?" (Goals), "Have I written today?" (Learned), "What is today's session and how is the week going?" (Fitness).
- First-impression findings are about appeal within the first 50 ms, measured on websites. They justify care about the initial look, but they do not prove that a screen judged attractive is understood. A 5-second comprehension test on the redesigned screens would still be needed.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source found for the "5-second test" as a validated comprehension measure; it is a practitioner method. The 17 to 50 ms literature measures appeal, not understanding.
- No rigorous mobile eye-tracking study of scan patterns on app screens (as opposed to web pages) was retrieved. A secondary article attributes to NN/g the figure that 57% of page-viewing time is spent in the first screenful; I could not confirm it against NN/g's own article.
- Rosenholtz's clutter measures (feature congestion) and set-size effects on search time were searched but not retrieved, so I have no sourced number for "cost per added element."
- Gouveia 2016 participant count, duration and per-prototype effects were not confirmed (abstract only). Banovic 2014 glance-duration cutoffs were not confirmed.
- The first-impression studies used desktop website screenshots. Only the Miniukovich work addresses phone apps, and I saw it at abstract level only.

## Next action: single primary call to action, choice overload, Hick's law, defaults, the Fogg behavior model

### Takeaway
There is no rigorous published evidence for the "one primary CTA" rule itself; it rests on attention science and practitioner testing. The supporting behavioral findings are narrower than usually claimed: choice overload averages near zero across studies and appears only under specific conditions (complex options, hard decisions, uncertain preferences, a wish to minimize effort), Hick's law is poorly matched to real screens, and the Fogg model is a useful heuristic with almost no peer-reviewed validation. Defaults are the most robust lever: most people never change them.

### Cited Findings

**Choice overload**
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analysis; flags the original effect as unreliable] Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010, J. Consumer Research 37:409-425): 63 conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments, N = 5,036. "Mean effect size of virtually zero but considerable variance between studies." Several preconditions looked relevant (people with prior preferences or expertise were not harmed by more options), but "no sufficient conditions could be identified." — [Scheibehenne et al. 2010 PDF](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf); [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v37y2010i3p409-425.html)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analysis] Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman (2015, J. Consumer Psychology): 99 observations, N = 7,202. Four moderators predict when more options hurt: choice set complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and an effort-minimizing decision goal. With moderators included, the overall effect of assortment size on overload is significant. — [Kellogg record for Chernev et al. 2015](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual/); [paper listing](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/choice-overload-a-conceptual-review-and-meta-analysis/814702993403281408-7309)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed reanalysis, secondary] A 2018 reanalysis of the Chernev data by McShane and Böckenholt found more complicated patterns: for some outcome measures the effect reverses at low moderator levels, and for others there is no discernible effect. — [as summarized in a 2022 Frontiers in Psychology article on choice overload](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.800528) (attribution through a search summary; check the original)
- [WEAK, press/secondary] A Psychology Today summary gives the Scheibehenne pooled effect as d = 0.02. — [Psychology Today, "Is choice overload a real thing?"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/pop-psych/201602/is-choice-overload-real-thing)

**Hick's law**
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed review, seen at record level only] Proctor and Schneider's review (Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, DOI 10.1080/17470218.2017.1322622) covers the shortcomings of Hick's law: effects of stimulus-response compatibility, practice, very large set sizes and sequential dependencies all break the simple log relation. — [PubMed record](https://www.pubmed.gov/28434379/)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, CHI 2020] Liu et al. conclude that Hick's law "speaks against, not for" the popular "less is better" principle when applied naively, and that it does not describe visual search through a menu or screen. — [How Relevant is Hick's Law for HCI?](https://archivesic.ccsd.cnrs.fr/INFRES/hal-02867301v1)
- [WEAK, practitioner] The Interaction Design Foundation notes the law is most robust for unordered lists where users cannot focus on one part, and that users apply scanning strategies whenever the interface supports them. — [IxDF on the Hick-Hyman law](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/the-hick-hyman-law-an-argument-against-complexity-in-user-interface-design)

**Defaults**
- [MIXED, practitioner research with consistent observations] NN/g's guidance: "assume that users will not change defaults and plan for this in your designs"; search users click the top result far more often than relevance alone explains. — [NN/g, The Danger of Defaults](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/the-danger-of-defaults/)
- [WEAK, practitioner, secondary] Jared Spool's analysis of users' Word settings is widely cited as showing fewer than 5% of users change any default. I could confirm this only through secondary articles. — [UX Collective summary citing Spool](https://uxdesign.cc/default-bias-who-chose-your-settings-d2acde25551b)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, CHI 1991] Mackay studied the customization behavior of 51 users and found many never use customization features at all; the paper frames customization as driven by specific triggers and held back by barriers. — [Mackay 1991, Triggers and Barriers to Customizing Software](https://www.lri.fr/~mackay/pdffiles/CHI91.Triggers.pdf)

**Fogg behavior model (B = MAP)**
- [WEAK, practitioner model] The model (behavior happens when motivation, ability and a prompt coincide) originates in a 2009 Persuasive Technology conference paper. Behavioral-science commentators list "limited peer-reviewed validation" as its main limitation and call it overly simple compared with COM-B, whose capability construct is broader than Fogg's momentary "ability." My search found no rigorous validation study. — [The Behavioral Scientist on B = MAP](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/?p=3058); [comparison of the Fogg model and COM-B](https://thinkingaboutbehavior.substack.com/p/comparing-fogg-behavior-model-and)

**Behavior-change interventions in general are small and hard to predict**
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed megastudy, Nature 2021] Milkman et al.: 61,293 gym members, 53 (per a critique) four-week interventions designed by 30 scientists. About 45% of interventions increased gym visits; critics note only 13 were statistically distinguishable from the other interventions. — [Milkman et al. 2021 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/prediction/2021-milkman.pdf); [critique: megastudy scepticism](https://jasoncollins.blog/posts/megastudy-scepticism)

### Inferences
- The honest reading of the choice literature is: the number of buttons is not the problem by itself. Overload shows up when the person does not already know what they want, the options are hard to compare, and they want to get in and out quickly. Opening Orby to plan a day or log a thought fits that profile (uncertain preference, effort-minimizing goal), so reducing visible options is justified on those screens, but mainly by Chernev's moderators and by visual search, not by Hick's law or the jam study.
- Since most people never change defaults, the default state of each screen is the design. The single pre-selected next step (today's first unplanned slot, the goal that is furthest behind, the empty learning box, today's session) should be decided by the app, with alternatives one tap away rather than laid out side by side.
- Fogg's "make it easy and prompt at the right moment" is reasonable as a checklist but should not be cited as evidence. The better-supported version of the same idea is implementation intentions (a concrete when and where; see next section) and defaults.
- Expect small effects. Even expert-designed nudges in the megastudy worked less than half the time. Redesign choices here should be treated as hypotheses to check against the owner's own use.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or large published experiment was found that isolates "one primary CTA versus several" on app screens. Claims of large conversion lifts from single CTAs come from vendor blogs and were not retrieved or verified.
- Iyengar and Lepper (2000): from memory, the jam study had 24 versus 6 jams with about 3% versus 30% of stoppers purchasing; I did not retrieve a primary source this session. Its status as hard to replicate is supported by the Scheibehenne meta-analysis above.
- Nielsen's 2005 article "The Power of Defaults" and Spool's original "Do users change their settings?" were not retrieved in full text.
- Baymard and NN/g findings on primary versus secondary button distinction were searched; only practitioner guidance without data was found.
- Default-effect meta-analysis (Jachimowicz et al. 2019) was not searched; no effect size for defaults is given here.

## Planning and goals: implementation intentions, goal-setting theory, goal gradient and endowed progress, "top three", planning fallacy, Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina, small wins, fresh starts

### Takeaway
Specific goals, when-and-where plans, and recorded progress monitoring are the best-supported mechanisms in this whole review (meta-analytic d of roughly 0.3 to 0.65, shrinking under bias correction). Visible progress toward a near end point and a head start both speed people up, people resume unfinished tasks (Ovsiankina holds; the Zeigarnik memory effect does not), and writing a plan quiets unfinished goals. The "top three" or most-important-task rule has no direct evidence; it is a reasonable inference from the planning fallacy and proximal-goal findings.

### Cited Findings

**Implementation intentions (if-then, when-where plans)**
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed meta-analysis] Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38:69-119): 94 independent tests (63 reports, over 8,000 participants), medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = 0.65. — [University of Konstanz record](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)
- [STRONG for existence, MIXED for size; peer-reviewed meta-analysis] Sheeran, Listrom and Gollwitzer (2024, European Review of Social Psychology), "The when and how of planning": 642 independent tests; implementation intentions were effective for cognitive, affective and behavioral outcomes, with d between .27 and .66 depending on outcome. — [Sheeran et al. 2024 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)
- [MIXED, secondary summary of the 2024 paper, unverified] Bias-corrected estimates are reported as much lower: trim-and-fill d = 0.35 and robust Bayesian d = 0.15 (95% interval 0.08 to 0.22). — [goalsandprogress.com summary](https://goalsandprogress.com/implementation-intentions-research/) (blog; check against the paper before quoting)

**Goal setting and monitoring**
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed review of meta-analyses] Locke and Latham (2002, American Psychologist 57(9)): specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than vague "do your best" goals; meta-analytic effect sizes reported as d = .42 to .80 (another passage gives .52 to .82), citing their 1990 work. Most of this evidence is from work and lab tasks. — [Locke and Latham 2002 PDF](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgpsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20%282002%29.pdf)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed meta-analysis of RCTs] Epton, Currie and Armitage (2017, J. Consulting and Clinical Psychology): 384 effect sizes, N = 16,523; the unique effect of goal setting on behavior change is d = .34 (CI .28 to .41). Larger when the goal was difficult, set publicly, or a group goal. — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189034/)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed meta-analysis, Psychological Bulletin 2016] Harkin, Webb et al.: 138 studies, N = 19,951. Prompting people to monitor goal progress raised monitoring frequency (d+ = 1.98) and goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, CI 0.32 to 0.48). Effects were larger when progress was physically recorded and when it was reported or made public. — [White Rose repository record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, single classic experiment with children] Bandura and Schunk (1981, JPSP 41:586-598): children given proximal subgoals progressed rapidly, mastered the material and developed self-efficacy and intrinsic interest; distal goals "had no demonstrable effects." — [Bandura and Schunk 1981 PDF](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc552d85141987915dab842_Bandura%20&%20Schunk,%201981.pdf)

**Goal gradient and endowed progress**
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed field experiments, few direct replications found] Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006, J. Marketing Research 43:39-58): people accelerate as they near a reward. Customers given a 12-stamp coffee card with 2 bonus stamps already filled completed the 10 required purchases faster than customers with a plain 10-stamp card ("illusionary goal progress"); process studies rule out sunk cost as the explanation; stronger acceleration predicted retention and faster re-engagement. — [Columbia Business School record](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed field experiment, secondary] Nunes and Drèze (2006, J. Consumer Research 32(4):504-512): car-wash customers got either an 8-stamp card or a 10-stamp card with 2 stamps pre-filled (same 8 purchases needed); the pre-filled group completed at nearly twice the rate. — [secondary summary](https://www.renascence.io/us/behavioral-biases/endowed-progress-effect) (consultancy page; exact figures not verified, see Gaps)

**Unfinished tasks: Zeigarnik, Ovsiankina, and plan-making**
- [STRONG for Ovsiankina, FAILED for Zeigarnik; peer-reviewed meta-analysis] Ghibellini and Meier (2025, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications 12): "We found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but found a general tendency to resume tasks." "The Ovsiankina effect represents a general tendency, whereas the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity." — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed lab experiments, no independent replication found] Masicampo and Baumeister (2011, JPSP 101:667-683): unfulfilled goals caused intrusive thoughts and worse performance on unrelated tasks; letting participants make a specific plan for the goal eliminated these effects, without the goal being completed. — [Masicampo and Baumeister 2011 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed diary studies, correlational] Syrek et al. (2017, J. Occupational Health Psychology 22(2)): unfinished tasks at the end of the week predict worse weekend sleep through rumination. Weigelt and Syrek (2017): 83 employees over 14 weeks; making progress on tasks alleviates the harm. — [Syrek et al. 2017](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/zeigarniks-sleepless-nights-how-unfinished-tasks-at-the-end-of-th/); [Weigelt and Syrek 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jijerp/v14y2017i12p1606-d123681.html)

**Small wins**
- [MIXED, large diary study, mostly reported in a book and HBR, not independently replicated at scale] Amabile and Kramer: nearly 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 people in 26 project teams at 7 companies. Of all positive events, "the single most powerful is progress in meaningful work"; setbacks were the most powerful negative event. Only 5% of surveyed managers ranked progress first as a motivator. Sample was 85% degree-holding professionals. — [Mind Tools summary of The Progress Principle](https://www.mindtools.com/a2s3f5g/the-progress-principle/); [Canadian Government Executive summary](https://canadiangovernmentexecutive.ca/the-motivating-power-of-progress/)

**Fresh starts**
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, large archival data, correlational] Dai, Milkman and Riis (2014, Management Science). Google searches for "diet" rise at the start of a new week (14.4%), month (3.7%), year (82.1%) and after federal holidays (10.2%). Undergraduate gym visits (N = 11,912 members) rise at the start of a new week (33.4%), month (14.4%), year (11.6%) and semester (47.1%), after school breaks (24.3%) and after birthdays (7.5%, subset of 2,076). stickK commitment contracts (42,913 users, 65,845 contracts) rise at the start of a week (62.9%), month (23.6%), year (145.3%), after federal holidays (55.1%) and birthdays (2.6%). — [Dai, Milkman and Riis 2014 PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed field experiment] Beshears, Dai, Milkman and Benartzi (2021, OBHDP 167:72-87): 6,082 university employees. Framing a future date as a fresh start (birthday, first day of spring) increased the choice to raise savings contributions at that date without lowering immediate increases; the New Year framing did not produce a noticeable effect. — [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:167:y:2021:i:c:p:72-87); [note on the New Year null](https://www.napa-net.org/news-info/daily-news/how-fresh-start-framing-can-boost-retirement-savings)

**Planning fallacy and daily capacity**
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed, long-established finding] Buehler, Griffin and Ross (1994, JPSP 67:366-381): students predicted finishing their theses in 33.9 days on average and took 55.5 days; people plan from imagined future steps and ignore how similar past tasks went. — [Buehler et al. 1994 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Plan your day" should produce a small number of specific items, each with a when (and where it helps), because that is the form with the strongest evidence (implementation intentions plus specific goals). Even at the conservative bias-corrected estimate (d about 0.15 to 0.35) it is the best-supported lever available, and it costs one extra field.
- The Goals page should show each goal as a specific target with recorded progress and a near next milestone. Harkin (progress monitoring, larger when recorded) and Bandura and Schunk (proximal subgoals beat distal ones) point the same way: break a distant goal into the next step and show distance to that step, not only to the end.
- Goal-gradient and endowed-progress results justify showing progress as "nearly there" where true, and starting visual progress above zero where there is a real basis (for example counting the plan itself or the first logged step). They do not justify fake progress: both studies involved real rewards, and neither was tested in a self-tracking app.
- Use Ovsiankina, not Zeigarnik: an unfinished item that is visibly resumable ("Continue: 2 of 3 planned") draws people back. Do not rely on people remembering open tasks.
- Planning itself gives relief (Masicampo and Baumeister). The Plan section can honestly present finishing the plan as a completed act with its own reward, separate from finishing the tasks.
- A cap of about three daily priorities is defensible as a guard against the planning fallacy (the thesis took about 64% longer than predicted, 55.5 versus 33.9 days) and as a way to keep goals proximal, but this is an inference. No study tested three versus five versus ten.
- Fresh-start effects support a clean daily and weekly reset (Monday and first-of-month views that start empty and inviting) and argue against carrying yesterday's failures forward visually. The evidence is correlational and the one field experiment had a null for New Year, so treat it as a framing choice, not a proven driver.
- Amabile's finding supports closing the day with a visible record of what moved forward, which also links Plan to the "What did you learn today?" box.

### Gaps
- No direct empirical evidence found for "most important task", "top three" or "1-3-5" rules. They appear to be practitioner conventions.
- Nunes and Drèze's exact figures (from memory: 34% versus 19% redemption, N of about 300 cards) were not confirmed against the paper. No registered replication of endowed progress or of the coffee-card study was found.
- The 2024 Sheeran et al. bias-corrected estimates come from a blog summary; the paper's moderator results (which plan formats work best) were not retrieved.
- Parke et al. (2018) on daily time-management versus contingent planning was searched and not retrieved, so I have no field evidence on daily planning at work.
- I found no megastudy that tested fresh-start framing directly and no failed replication of it; I also found no confirmation.
- Amabile's result was confirmed only through secondary summaries of the book and HBR article.

## Feedback and reward: progress indicators, completion animations and haptics, streaks, gamification, self-determination theory, overjustification

### Takeaway
Progress feedback and streaks reliably change behavior in the short term, but with clear costs: a plain progress bar does not help by default (it helps when early progress feels fast), intact streaks motivate while broken streaks demotivate, and gamification effects on behavior are small (g about 0.25) and unstable in rigorous studies. Points and badges raise output without raising intrinsic motivation, and tangible rewards can undermine it. There is essentially no rigorous public evidence on completion animations or haptics.

### Cited Findings

**Progress indicators**
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed meta-analysis, Google/City University] Villar, Callegaro and Yang (2013, Social Science Computer Review 31:744-762): 32 randomized experiments from 10 sources on progress bars in web surveys. A constant-speed indicator did not significantly reduce drop-off (and increased it in studies with a small incentive). Fast-to-slow indicators (early progress feels quick) reduced drop-off; slow-to-fast increased it. — [Google Research record](https://research.google/pubs/where-am-i-a-meta-analysis-of-experiments-on-the-effects-of-progress-indicators-for-web-surveys/); [City Research Online full text](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/14427/); a reader critique questions a possible coding error in the tables: [NeoAcademic](https://neoacademic.com/2013/11/07/does-gamifying-survey-progress-improve-completion-rate/)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed meta-analysis] Recording progress improves goal attainment (d+ = 0.40; see Harkin et al. 2016 above). — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)

**Streaks**
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, J. Consumer Research 2023, seven studies] Silverman, Barasch et al., "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions": people shown an intact streak did more of the behavior afterwards than people shown a broken one, even when the underlying behavior was identical (in one design all participants had completed the same days, and some were told a software malfunction broke the log). The authors suggest apps should not highlight broken streaks, and note "what counts as a streak is malleable." — [JCR DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029); [press summary](https://phys.org/news/2024-03-streaks.pdf); [press summary 2](https://earth.com/news/streaks-can-be-a-powerful-tool-for-motivation)
- [MIXED, vendor A/B test, primary source, large N but self-reported and not peer-reviewed] Duolingo (blog, 19 Nov 2020): separating the streak from the daily goal, so that one lesson extends the streak, raised Day 14 retention by 3.3%, daily active learners by 1%, the share of daily learners on a streak by 10.5% within 20 days, and the share of new learners on a streak by 19%. A year later, learners on a 7+ day streak were up over 40% and "just over half" of daily learners had a 7+ day streak versus "about a third." Reported downside: fewer learners reached their daily goal. Correlational context: learners who reach a 7-day streak are "2.4 times more likely" to return the next day. — [Duolingo blog, Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- [WEAK, vendor claims, secondary] Duolingo's growth team reports a "streak wager" test with significant gains in Day 1, 7 and 14 retention (Day 7 the largest at +14%), and a PM interview describes over 600 streak experiments and says letting users choose their own streak goal increased retention. — [Duolingo engineering blog](https://making.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals); [Lenny's Newsletter interview](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks) (figures via search summary, not read in full)
- [WEAK, peer-reviewed qualitative] Renfree et al. (2016), "Don't kick the habit": in the Lift app, reminders and streaks supported repetition but created dependence on the app, so the behavior was fragile and often stopped when the app was dropped. — [Northumbria research portal](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/dont-kick-the-habit-the-role-of-dependency-in-habit-formation-app/)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, N = 96] Lally et al. (2010, European J. Social Psychology): time to reach automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days (median 66, from the 39 well-fitted curves); "missing one opportunity did not significantly impact the habit formation process," while very inconsistent performance did prevent habit formation. — [UCL news release](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)
- [FLAG: unsupported claim in circulation] Several blogs cite a "CHI 2020 study" saying streak anxiety is the top reason people quit habit apps and that streak trackers are 63% more likely to quit after one missed day. I found no primary paper behind this claim. Do not use it. — [example of the unsourced claim](https://dev.to/skaraz/why-your-habit-tracker-resets-you-to-zero-and-why-thats-psychologically-damaging-5cho)

**Gamification**
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analysis, Educational Psychology Review 2020] Sailer and Homner: 38 publications, 40 experiments on gamified learning. Pooled effects reported as g = 0.49 (cognitive), g = 0.36 (motivational) and g = 0.25 (behavioral). Only the cognitive effect was stable in the high-rigor subset; motivational and behavioral effects were less stable. For behavioral outcomes, game fiction (a narrative frame) and social interaction were significant moderators. — [University of Augsburg record](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056) (effect sizes via a secondary summary: [rework.com](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/edtech-growth/gamification-in-learning))
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed review, Int. J. Information Management 2019] Koivisto and Hamari reviewed 819 studies: results lean toward positive findings on effectiveness, but the authors call the amount of mixed results remarkable; the field lacks coherent models; points, badges and leaderboards dominate. — [Koivisto and Hamari 2019 PDF](https://trepo.tuni.fi/bitstream/10024/115953/2/the_rise_of_motivational_2019.pdf)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed online experiment] Mekler, Brühlmann, Tuch and Opwis (2017, Computers in Human Behavior 71:525-534): points, levels and leaderboards increased the quantity of output (tags produced) but did not change intrinsic motivation or perceived competence; they worked as extrinsic incentives. — [Mekler et al. 2017 PDF](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf)

**Self-determination theory and overjustification**
- [STRONG for direction, disputed by a rival meta-analysis; peer-reviewed, Psychological Bulletin 1999] Deci, Koestner and Ryan: 128 experiments; tangible rewards "have a substantial undermining effect" on intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks. Cameron and Pierce (1994) had concluded the negative effects were limited and avoidable, and the debate continued in later comments. — [Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999 PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); [2001 follow-up](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analysis, Health Psychology Review 2021] Ntoumanis et al.: 73 experimental studies of SDT-based health interventions; small-to-medium changes in most SDT constructs and in health behaviors at end of intervention and follow-up; small positive changes in physical and psychological health; effects described as modest and heterogeneous. — [University of Birmingham record](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-self-determination-theory-informed-interventio/)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analysis] A separate meta-analysis by Sheeran and colleagues put the average effect of SDT interventions on health behavior at d+ = .23, with publication and small-sample bias detected (effects stayed above zero after correction); autonomous motivation and perceived competence mediated the effect. — [SDT interventions meta-analysis PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/164092/3/SDT%20ABM%202020.pdf)

### Inferences
- Show progress where early steps look large. A goal bar that jumps visibly on the first action (fast-to-slow) is supported; a bar that crawls at the start is worse than no bar. This also fits endowed progress.
- If Orby shows streaks at all, the evidence favors: a low bar for keeping one (Duolingo's one-lesson rule), a streak defined generously (Silverman: what counts is malleable; Lally: one miss does not matter for habit formation), and no display of a broken streak or a reset to zero. A count of total days done, or "5 of the last 7", avoids the broken-streak penalty while keeping the monitoring benefit. This is a design inference; no study compared those formats directly.
- The Duolingo numbers show that streak mechanics move retention by single-digit percentages and can shift effort away from the real goal (fewer learners hit their daily goal). For a one-person app whose purpose is the owner's training and reflection, retention of the app is not the outcome; the risk in Renfree (the habit depends on the app) is the relevant one.
- Points, levels and badges are not well supported for this product: they raise quantity without raising motivation (Mekler), behavioral effects are small and unstable (Sailer and Homner), and tangible-style rewards can undermine interest in activities people already value (Deci et al.). Competence feedback (clear progress, "you did what you planned") is the safer reward, in line with SDT.
- "Rewarding enough to repeat" is best supported by informational feedback: seeing progress recorded (d about 0.4), reaching a near subgoal, and finishing a plan. Completion animation and haptics may add polish but have no evidence base; keep them brief and tied to a real completion.

### Gaps
- No rigorous public study found on completion animations, confetti, sound or haptics and their effect on repeat use. This is practitioner belief.
- Hamari, Koivisto and Sarsa's 2014 review ("Does gamification work?") and Hanus and Fox (2015, classroom badges and leaderboards lowering motivation) were not retrieved; from memory both support the "mixed" grading, but they are unsourced here.
- Deci et al.'s effect sizes (from memory, d about -0.34 for tangible rewards on free-choice behavior) were not confirmed this session.
- Duolingo streak freeze results, the streak-animation result (a secondary article cites a 1.7% lift in Day 7 retention for new learners) and the iOS widget claim ("60% more commitment") could not be traced to a primary source.
- No peer-reviewed quantitative study of streak anxiety or loss aversion in habit apps was found beyond Silverman et al.; the widely quoted CHI 2020 statistics appear to be unsourced.
- No app-specific SDT meta-analysis (for example Villalobos-Zúñiga and Cherubini 2020) was retrieved.

## Reflection and writing prompts: brief daily reflection, expressive writing, "three good things", prompt specificity, keeping a one-line-a-day habit

### Takeaway
The evidence that brief written reflection helps is real but smaller and shakier than its popular reputation: the best-known reflection study is an unreplicated working paper (see Gaps for a co-author integrity caveat I did not verify this session), expressive writing has a very small average effect (r about .075), and "three good things" did no better than a positive placebo in a replication, although gratitude lists remain one of the better-supported happiness practices. Almost nothing rigorous exists on prompt wording or on what sustains a one-line-a-day habit.

### Cited Findings

**Reflection on what was learned**
- [MIXED, working paper, not independently replicated] Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano and Staats, "Learning by Thinking" (SSRN, first posted 2014, revised 2023; earlier titled "Making Experience Count"): two lab experiments plus a field experiment at a large business-process outsourcing company in India. Trainees who spent the last 15 minutes of each training day writing about what they had learned scored about 23% (22.8%) higher on the final assessment than controls who kept working. The effect was mediated by self-efficacy, was strongest early in the learning curve, and adding a step of sharing reflections with others gave no extra benefit. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2414478); [HBS Working Knowledge summary](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/reflecting-on-work-improves-job-performance); [independent review of the paper](https://cpree.uw.edu/a-review-of-learning-by-thinking-how-reflection-aids-performance)
- [WEAK, peer-reviewed HCI field study] Kocielnik et al. (UbiComp/IMWUT 2018), "Reflection Companion": a conversational system sent daily adaptive mini-dialogues about fitness-tracker data. Follow-up prompts worked best when they built on the user's previous response; design choices that made reflection actionable, personal and accountable deepened it. — [Kocielnik et al. 2018 PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/garyhs/docs/kocielnik-UBICOMP2018-reflection.pdf)

**Expressive writing**
- [STRONG that the average effect is very small; peer-reviewed meta-analysis, Psychological Bulletin 2006] Frattaroli: 146 randomized studies of experimental disclosure; average effect r = .075, positive and significant, with wide variability across studies. — [Frattaroli 2006 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/2006-frattaroli.pdf)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analysis, Frontiers in Psychiatry 2023] 31 RCTs: expressive writing had a small significant effect on depression, anxiety and stress symptoms, Hedges' g = -0.12 (95% CI -0.21 to -0.04). — [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1204053/pdf)

**"Three good things" and gratitude**
- [FAILED in part: replication found no advantage over placebo for depression] Mongrain and Anselmo-Matthews (2012, J. Clinical Psychology) replicated Seligman et al. (2005) with an improved control and an added "positive placebo." The positive psychology exercises produced lasting increases in happiness, but so did the positive placebo, and the exercises did not beat control on depression. The authors suggest the benefit comes from a common factor, activating positive self-relevant information. — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24469930/)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed reanalysis, PLoS ONE 2019] When small-sample bias is taken into account, positive psychology interventions have small but significant effects on well-being, with depression effects more variable. — [2019 PLoS ONE reanalysis](https://typeset.io/pdf/meta-analyses-of-positive-psychology-interventions-the-2i41q5zsm5.pdf)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed systematic review, Nature Human Behaviour 2023, itself criticized as unregistered] Folk and Dunn reviewed 494 papers (532 studies) on the five most recommended happiness strategies. Gratitude was among the better-supported: "consistent evidence that people in diverse cultural contexts benefit from making gratitude lists." Almost 95% of experiments on nature, exercise and mindfulness did not meet basic current standards (preregistration, adequate power). — [NPR coverage](https://www.npr.org/2023/07/20/1189035054/rethinking-happiness-gratitude-meditation-mindfulness-exercise); [Scimex summary](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/media-tips-on-being-happy-lack-scientific-evidence)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analysis, J. Happiness Studies 2021] Cregg and Cheavens: gratitude interventions have small effects on depression and anxiety symptoms, and estimates may be inflated by control conditions (such as listing hassles) that push the other way. — [Cregg and Cheavens 2021 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-020-00236-6)
- [WEAK, press, secondhand] A news article on a large well-being meta-analysis reports that no category of intervention had a large effect and names "three good things" as one that showed no benefit. — [Nautilus](https://nautil.us/new-evidence-that-therapy-can-make-you-happier-238241) (not checked against the paper)

**Keeping the habit**
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed] Habit formation takes a median of about 66 days with a range of 18 to 254, depends on repeating the behavior in a consistent context, and survives a single miss (Lally et al. 2010). — [UCL news release](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)
- [WEAK, peer-reviewed qualitative] Habits built on app reminders and streaks tend to depend on the app's cues (Renfree et al. 2016). — [Northumbria research portal](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/dont-kick-the-habit-the-role-of-dependency-in-habit-formation-app/)

### Inferences
- "What did you learn today?" has a plausible evidence base (Di Stefano) for consolidating learning and building self-efficacy, but the effect size should not be promised. The mechanism the authors propose, feeling more capable, suggests the box should feel like recording a gain, not doing homework. That fits Amabile's progress finding.
- Private writing is enough. The sharing condition added nothing in Di Stefano, so there is no evidence-based case for social features around the box.
- The Mongrain result implies the specific exercise matters less than regularly bringing a positive, self-relevant thought to mind. A single consistent question asked every day is defensible, and so is rotating prompts; nothing rigorous says which is better. The Kocielnik finding (prompts that build on the previous answer work best) is the only prompt-design evidence found and is weak.
- For habit strength, tie the box to a fixed daily context (for example right after the day's plan is closed in the evening), keep the response cost at one line, and do not punish a missed day (Lally). The entry should be visible later, since recorded progress is the better-supported reward.
- Expressive-writing benefits, where they exist, come from longer and more emotional writing sessions than a one-line box. Do not justify the box with the expressive-writing literature.

### Gaps
- Di Stefano et al.: I could not confirm journal publication, the field sample size, or the company name. Not searched this session: whether this specific paper is affected by the data-integrity findings against co-author Francesca Gino (from memory, the papers flagged by Data Colada in 2023 did not include this one, and Harvard later revoked her tenure; verify before citing the study prominently). No independent replication was found.
- No randomized study was found that isolates prompt specificity (structured versus open prompts) or prompt rotation and measures adherence. Blog claims that "structured prompts outperform open ones" trace to secondhand reports of Pennebaker's views and unnamed meta-analytic moderators.
- No rigorous evidence found on one-line-a-day journals specifically (retention, benefits or optimal length).
- Baumel et al. (2019) on mental-health app retention (from memory, median 15-day retention of about 4%) was searched and not retrieved.
- Seligman et al. (2005) original effect sizes and the exact preregistered-study counts in Folk and Dunn were not retrieved.

## Aesthetics and motivation: the aesthetic-usability effect, and whether illustration, color and motion help or hurt

### Takeaway
Attractive interfaces are judged easier to use before use, but the causal claim "beautiful is usable" is weak: in a controlled experiment aesthetics did not change perceived usability after use, poor usability lowered perceived beauty, and the meta-analytic effect of aesthetics on actual performance is tiny (g = 0.12). Decorative illustration that is interesting but irrelevant reliably hurts comprehension, while color and shape that carry the content have small positive effects; motion carries real accessibility costs.

### Cited Findings
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, correlational, same-moment ratings] Tractinsky (1997) replicated Kurosu and Kashimura (1995): very high correlations between perceived interface aesthetics and perceived ease of use judged before use, in Israel as in Japan. Tractinsky later (2013) raised the method-bias problem that both ratings were collected at the same time on the same design. — [Ben-Gurion University publication record](https://www.bgu.ac.il/en/researcher/noam-tractinsky/publications/351759634/)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed lab experiment, N = 80; contradicts the popular version] Tuch, Roth, Hornbæk, Opwis and Bargas-Avila (2012, Computers in Human Behavior), "Is beautiful really usable?": four versions of one online shop (aesthetics low or high, usability low or high). "Aesthetics does not affect perceived usability," whereas low usability lowered post-use perceived aesthetics. The authors say the "what is beautiful is usable" notion can be reversed under some conditions. — [Google Research record](https://research.google/pubs/is-beautiful-really-usable-toward-understanding-the-relation-between-usability-aesthetics-and-affect-in-hci/)
- [STRONG that the performance effect is small; peer-reviewed meta-analysis] Thielsch et al. (2019): 25 studies, 101 effects, 3,025 participants. Interface aesthetics has a small significant effect on objective user performance, g = 0.12, with high heterogeneity. — [Thielsch et al. 2019 PDF](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/owms/2019_thielsch_et_al.pdf)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed] First-impression appeal is driven mainly by low visual complexity and high prototypicality, with colorfulness a secondary factor (Tuch et al. 2012; Reinecke et al. 2013; see the Glanceability section). — [Tuch et al. 2012](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/38315.pdf); [Reinecke et al. 2013](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2013/reinecke13predicting.shtml)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed meta-analysis, Educational Psychology Review 2020] Sundararajan and Adesope, "Keep it Coherent": across 58 studies and more than 7,500 learners, interesting but irrelevant additions ("seductive details") lowered learning outcomes. One summary reports the harm is considerable in short lessons (under 5 minutes) and not significant in longer ones. — [Washington State University news](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [WSU repository record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Keep-it-Coherent-A-Meta-Analysis-of/99900601155501842)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analysis, Educational Psychology Review 2021; replication and extension of Brom et al. 2018] Wong and Adesope: "emotional design" (pleasant colors, rounded and face-like shapes applied to the content itself) has small positive effects, reported as g = 0.35 for retention, 0.27 for transfer and 0.29 for comprehension; pleasant colors lower perceived difficulty; effects are stronger for static than animated material. — [WSU repository record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Meta-Analysis-of-Emotional-Designs-in-Multimedia/99900601054701842) (effect sizes via a secondary source: [Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.797236/full))
- [STRONG as a standard; W3C] WCAG 2.1 Success Criterion 2.3.3 (AAA): motion triggered by interaction should be possible to turn off unless essential, because it can cause nausea, dizziness and distraction; the recommended technique is honoring the operating system's reduce-motion setting. — [W3C Understanding SC 2.3.3](https://w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/animation-from-interactions)
- [WEAK to MIXED, practitioner guidance] Small transitions such as a quick fade need no controls; large or looping animation does. GitHub's Primer guidance lists distraction, frustration and difficulty reading among motion's effects and suggests attention-catching animation stop within about five seconds. One accessibility source states over 35% of adults have experienced some vestibular dysfunction by age 40. — [web.dev, Animation and motion](https://web.dev/learn/accessibility/motion); [Primer, Motion and animation](https://primer.style/accessibility/design-guidance/motion-and-animation); [Pope Tech](https://blog.pope.tech/2025/12/08/design-accessible-animation-and-movement/)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, small N] In the glanceable-display studies, an abstract illustrated metaphor (UbiFit's garden) was the feedback itself and was associated with maintained activity. — [UbiFit project page](https://www.consolvo.org/ubifit)

### Inferences
- "Visually engaging, stimulating" should be delivered through illustration, color and shape that encode the state of the screen (the plan filling in, a goal growing, the week's training taking shape). That is where the small positive effects are (emotional design, UbiFit). Illustration that sits next to the content as decoration falls under seductive details, and the harm is largest in very short interactions, which is exactly how app screens are used.
- Do not expect a prettier screen to be easier to use. Fixing clarity raises perceived beauty (Tuch, Roth et al.), not the reverse, so order the work as: structure and next action first, visual richness second.
- Color is a secondary driver of appeal, and guides attention only as a strong contrast on one item. Use the saturated accent for the single next action and for progress; keep everything else quiet. More color overall adds complexity, which is the main negative driver of first impressions.
- Keep motion short, tied to a state change the user caused (completing a task, saving an entry), never looping, and off under reduce-motion. Static emotional design outperformed animated in the learning literature.

### Gaps
- No rigorous study found that tests illustration, color or motion against engagement or repeat use in a lifestyle or habit app. The comprehension evidence comes from multimedia learning research, and the transfer to app screens is an inference.
- Sundararajan and Adesope's pooled effect size was not retrieved (sources also disagree on whether it covered 58 studies or 68 experiments). Wong and Adesope's values come from a secondary citation.
- No meta-analysis of the correlation between aesthetics and perceived usability was retrieved (Hassenzahl's work was searched and not found).
- The 35% vestibular prevalence figure is from a vendor accessibility blog and was not traced to an epidemiological source.

## Customization: letting users hide or choose features (customization versus personalization, IKEA effect, autonomy, defaults)

### Takeaway
Observational research is consistent that few users change defaults, so customization cannot be the fix for a confusing default screen. Choice and self-made things do carry real value (autonomy support has small positive effects; people value what they successfully build themselves), which supports offering a few meaningful choices, such as choosing one's own target, rather than a broad settings surface.

### Cited Findings
- [MIXED, practitioner research, long-standing] Nielsen (NN/g Alertbox on customization): users rarely use customization features, so customization should be reserved for features with substantial benefit and the default design must serve those who never customize. — [NN/g Alertbox: customization](https://www.nngroup.com/alertbox/customization.html); [Coding Horror quoting Nielsen](https://blog.codinghorror.com/the-power-of-defaults/)
- [MIXED, practitioner] "Assume that users will not change defaults and plan for this in your designs." — [NN/g, The Danger of Defaults](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/the-danger-of-defaults/)
- [WEAK, practitioner, secondary] Spool's analysis of Word settings files: fewer than 5% of users had changed any setting. — [UX Collective citing Spool](https://uxdesign.cc/default-bias-who-chose-your-settings-d2acde25551b)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed, CHI 1991, N = 51] Mackay: many users never customize; customization follows specific triggers and is held back by barriers (the paper's framing; the individual triggers and barriers were not checked in full text). — [Mackay 1991 PDF](https://www.lri.fr/~mackay/pdffiles/CHI91.Triggers.pdf)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed; conceptual replication exists] Norton, Mochon and Ariely (2012, J. Consumer Psychology): people value things they assembled themselves more highly (the IKEA effect), but only when they complete the work; when participants built and then destroyed their creations, or failed to finish, the effect disappeared. Sarstedt, Neubert and Barth (2017, J. Marketing Behavior) conceptually replicated it and point to psychological ownership as a mechanism. — [Norton, Mochon and Ariely PDF](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/12136084/norton%20mochon%20ariely%20third%20round%5B1%5D.pdf); [Sarstedt et al. 2017](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/JMB-0039)
- [WEAK, press, secondary] A news article reports a meta-analysis of the IKEA effect covering 55 studies and 5,454 people. — [Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/n-people-who-cant-throw-out-the-crooked-shelf-they-built-themselves-arent-sentimental-fifty-five-studies-covering-5454-people-found-that-building-a-thing-quietly-rewrites/) (not checked against the source)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analyses] Autonomy-supportive (SDT) interventions produce small-to-medium changes in motivation and small changes in health behavior (d+ about .23 in one meta-analysis), mediated by autonomous motivation and perceived competence. — [Ntoumanis et al. 2021](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-self-determination-theory-informed-interventio/); [Sheeran et al. SDT meta-analysis](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/164092/3/SDT%20ABM%202020.pdf)
- [WEAK, vendor claim] Duolingo's retention PM says letting users choose their own streak goal gave a sense of ownership and significantly increased retention. — [Lenny's Newsletter interview](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)
- [MIXED, peer-reviewed meta-analysis] Self-set specific goals matter: goal setting has a unique effect of d = .34 on behavior, and more options are tolerated well by people who already know their preferences (Scheibehenne). — [Epton et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189034/); [Scheibehenne et al. 2010](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf)

### Inferences
- Orby is a one-person app, so the usual "few users change defaults" statistic applies differently: the owner is the only user and is motivated to configure. Even so, Mackay's finding that customization needs a trigger and meets barriers applies to motivated individuals too, and every toggle shown on a main screen adds to the visual complexity the owner is complaining about. Hide-and-show controls belong in settings, not on the four screens.
- The better-supported use of choice is in the content: the person picks their own goals, targets and daily priorities (autonomy, goal setting, IKEA effect when the setup is completed). The layout should be decided by the app.
- The IKEA effect holds only when the building is completed. A long setup that is abandoned halfway gives no ownership benefit, so any "set up your goals" flow should be short enough to finish in one sitting.
- Personalization by the app (reordering by time of day, surfacing the lagging goal) is a way to get a tailored screen without asking for customization. The evidence on personalization versus customization was not retrieved, so this is a reasoned suggestion only.

### Gaps
- Sundar and Marathe (2010, Human Communication Research), "Personalization versus Customization", was searched and not retrieved; from memory it found power users preferred customization and other users preferred personalization, but that is unverified here.
- Page et al. (1996) on word-processor customization (the "92% customize" counter-finding) was searched and not found, so the "few users customize" claim rests on practitioner sources plus Mackay.
- No quantitative study found on whether hiding features in a personal productivity or health app improves use or satisfaction.
- No registered replication of the IKEA effect was found; the reported meta-analysis was seen only in a news article.
