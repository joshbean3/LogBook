## 1. Apple Human Interface Guidelines and WWDC design sessions (2022 to 2026)

### Takeaway

Apple's current position is that a screen is clear when it answers three questions without effort: where am I, what can I do, and where can I go from here. Apple's tools for that are a plain screen title, one colored prominent action, a summary people can read at a glance (the widget and Live Activity rules are the model), empty states that say what will appear and how to make it appear, defaults that need no setup, and show or hide controls placed on the screen they affect.

### Cited Findings

**1.1 The clarity test Apple uses (purpose and next action)**

- [WWDC] The three questions. "That starts with knowing where I am. The app should make that clear right away." "The next question is: 'What can I do?' I shouldn't have to guess. Actions should be clear and easy to understand." "And finally, I ask: 'Where can I go from here?' A clear sense of next steps keeps the flow going and helps me avoid hesitation or second-guessing." — [WWDC25 359, Design foundations from idea to interface](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)
- [WWDC] What failure looks like: a first screen that opens with a menu, a title that "feels more like branding", and content with "nothing to do besides browse". "The screen didn't guide me. I had to work to piece it together." People feel unclear structure "as hesitation, confusion, sometimes even giving up." — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)
- [WWDC] The fix is a toolbar with "a title with the name of the screen, instead of a menu, or branding as before. It sets expectations about the content of the screen", plus "screen-specific actions that people are most likely to want". "Since space is limited, I'd only include what's essential." — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)
- [WWDC] A second set of three questions. "Clear interfaces answer people's questions: What do I pay attention to? What can I interact with? And how do I interact?" "In a simple interface, every element earns its place." — [WWDC26 250, Principles of great design](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/250/)
- [WWDC] Simple is not the same as minimal. "If you bury all your functionality inside a single place, that might make your interface look more minimal, but it doesn't make it simple." "Concise interfaces use plain language. They strip away jargon and speak naturally. They avoid redundancy and get straight to the point." "They reduce the number of steps it takes to get things done." — [WWDC26 250](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/250/)
- [WWDC] Summarize instead of listing. "Identify where there's information you can distill down to its essence. Is there complex data that might be better understood as a graphic? Are there opportunities to summarize information so people can focus on what they care about?" — [WWDC26 250](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/250/)
- [WWDC] Every screen has one stated purpose. "Think about the most important thing someone needs to know at that moment. That's the purpose of your screen." "We know people don't always read the text on screen in order, so make sure your headers and buttons are clear." "You can tell people the purpose of a screen; it's not a secret!" "When you know the purpose of a screen, you can make choices about which ideas you want to keep in, which you can take out, or which ones you can move someplace else." — [WWDC22 10037, Writing for interfaces](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/)
- [WWDC] Anticipate the next step. "When writing for apps, you always have to ask, 'What comes next?' Anticipating the next action or question someone's going to have will help you know what to say." Example given: Workout shows "one large button clearly labeled 'Record Outdoor Walk.'" — [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/)
- [WWDC] Visual anchor. The presenter squints at the screen, finds the eye lands on the wrong element, and fixes it by "making what's most important larger or higher in contrast, so it naturally draws attention first." — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)
- [WWDC] Reduce choice overload by grouping content into a few themes: by time ("Recent files", "continue watching"), by progress ("helps people pick up where they left off", because "people rarely finish everything in one go"), and by patterns (things that belong together). These "help reduce choice overload and make the app feel one step ahead like it understands what we'll need next." — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)
- [WWDC] Prefer a list for structured items. "A List works way better, it's a flexible, highly usable and familiar way to show structured information and facilitates quick scanning." A grid "takes up too much space for just two items" and "doesn't handle longer text well". — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)

**1.2 Button prominence and color for the one primary action** (the "one or two prominent buttons per view" rule is in the earlier pass)

- [HIG] On Liquid Glass, color is reserved. "Apply color sparingly to the Liquid Glass material, and to symbols or text on the material. If you apply color, reserve it for elements that truly benefit from emphasis, such as status indicators or primary actions. To emphasize primary actions, apply color to the background rather than to symbols or text." "Refrain from adding color to the background of multiple controls." — [HIG Color](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color) (change log: December 16, 2025, "Updated guidance for Liquid Glass")
- [HIG] One meaning per color. "Avoid using the same color to mean different things. Use color consistently throughout your interface, especially when you use it to help communicate information like status or interactivity. For example, if you use your brand color to indicate that a borderless button is interactive, using the same or similar color to stylize noninteractive text is confusing." — [HIG Color](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color)
- [HIG] Never color alone. "Avoid relying solely on color to differentiate between objects, indicate interactivity, or communicate essential information." Use "text labels or glyph shapes to identify objects or states." — [HIG Color](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color)
- [WWDC] "Color can be distracting and make an interface feel overwhelming. Aim to use color in ways that create meaning such as portraying hierarchy, groupings, or indicating interaction." Slack is the example: "They use tint for primary actions", badges, new message creation and the selected tab. "Intentional use of color communicates status, feedback, and selection states helping people focus on what's important." — [WWDC26 251, Communicate your brand identity on iOS](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/251/)
- [HIG] Gate the continue action. "If you include a Next or Continue button after a set of text fields, make the button available only after people enter the data you require." — [HIG Entering data](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/entering-data)

**1.3 Empty states and "content unavailable" views**

- [Apple doc] The system component. `ContentUnavailableView` is "an interface, consisting of a label and additional content, that you display when the content of your app is unavailable to users." Use it when content cannot be shown: "a network error, a list without items, a search that returns no results". The simplest form has "a label and some additional content such as a description or a call to action". — [SwiftUI ContentUnavailableView](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/contentunavailableview)
- [HIG] "Provide clear next steps on any blank screens... give them a button or link to do so if possible." (Quoted in the earlier pass.) — [HIG Writing](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing)
- [WWDC] What good and bad empty states say. "Empty states can be times for education or even celebration, like when you complete the last item on your to-do list, but it depends on context." Bad: a favorites screen titled "Nothing strike your fancy?" with "Please come back if you do find something you want to eat", which "doesn't give any useful guidance or indication of how content might show up here." Good: Apple Podcasts says "No Saved Episodes", then "Save episodes you want to listen to later, and they'll show up here." Recap: "create useful empty states by using an appropriate tone and giving helpful guidance." — [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/)
- [HIG] An empty tab stays visible. "Don't disable or hide tab bar buttons, even when their content is unavailable. Having tab bar buttons available in some cases but not others makes your app's interface appear unstable and unpredictable. If a section is empty, explain why its content is unavailable." — [HIG Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars)
- [HIG] Loading is not empty. "Show something as soon as possible. If you make people wait for loading to complete before displaying anything, they can interpret the lack of content as a problem with your app". Show "placeholder text, graphics, or animations as content loads". — [HIG Loading](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/loading)

**1.4 Onboarding by doing, and tips**

- [HIG] "Ideally, people can understand your app or game simply by experiencing it, but if onboarding is necessary, design a flow that's fast, fun, and optional." — [HIG Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding) (change log: June 10, 2024)
- [HIG] "Teach through interactivity. People tend to grasp and retain information better when they can actually perform the task they're learning about instead of just viewing instructional material." — [HIG Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)
- [HIG] "Consider providing a collection of context-specific tips instead of a single onboarding flow." A tip "lets them concentrate on a single action or task before encountering new information." "When you have instructional content that refers to a specific area of the interface, display these instructions near that area." — [HIG Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)
- [HIG] "Postpone nonessential setup flows or customization steps. Provide reasonable default settings so most people can immediately start interacting with your app or game without performing additional configuration." — [HIG Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)
- [HIG] Tip rules with numbers. "Use tips for simple features... If a feature requires more than three actions, it's probably too complicated for a tip." "Keep your tips to one or two sentences and avoid including content that's promotional". "When your app has more than one tip, set the display frequency so tips display at a reasonable cadence, for example, once every 24 hours." "People who've already used a feature won't appreciate viewing a tip that describes it." — [HIG Offering help](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/offering-help)
- [HIG] "Directly relate the help you provide to the precise action or task people are doing right now and make it easy for people to dismiss or avoid the help if they don't need it." "Avoid bloating your help content by explaining how standard components or patterns work." — [HIG Offering help](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/offering-help)
- [WWDC] TipKit behavior worth copying in any stack: show a tip only after an event has happened several times (the demo uses "at least three times"); dismiss it once the person performs the action; stop after a display cap (the demo uses five); never show several at once "because that could be overwhelming and get in the way of what the person is trying to do." "Keep your tips short, instructional, and actionable." — [WWDC23 10229, Make features discoverable with TipKit](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10229/)

**1.5 Text fields and prompts**

- [HIG] Field or text view. "Use a text field to request a small amount of information, such as a name or an email address. To let people input larger amounts of text, use a text view instead." — [HIG Text fields](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/text-fields); a text view is for "text that's long, editable, or in a special format" — [HIG Text views](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/text-views)
- [HIG] Hint plus label. "Show a hint in a text field to help communicate its purpose." "Because placeholder text disappears when people start typing, it can also be useful to include a separate label describing the field to remind people of its purpose." — [HIG Text fields](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/text-fields)
- [HIG] Size signals effort. "To the extent possible, match the size of a text field to the quantity of anticipated text. The size of a text field helps people visually gauge the amount of information to provide." — [HIG Text fields](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/text-fields)
- [HIG] iOS detail: "In general, use the leading end of a text field to indicate a field's purpose and the trailing end to offer additional features". "Display a Clear button in the trailing end of a text field". — [HIG Text fields](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/text-fields)
- [HIG] Prefer choices to typing. "When possible, offer choices instead of requiring text entry. It's usually easier and more efficient to choose from lists of options than to type information, even when a keyboard is conveniently available." "You can also prefill fields with reasonable default values, which can minimize decision making and speed data entry." "Be clear about the data you need." — [HIG Entering data](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/entering-data)

**1.6 Progress indicators, gauges, charts and Activity rings**

- [HIG] "Progress indicator" means a wait, not goal progress. "All progress indicators are transient, appearing only while an operation is ongoing and disappearing after it completes." Prefer determinate. "Avoid vague terms like loading or authenticating because they seldom add value." "Display a progress indicator in a consistent location." — [HIG Progress indicators](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/progress-indicators) (change log: September 12, 2023)
- [HIG] Goal progress is a gauge. "A gauge displays a specific numerical value within a range of values." Two styles: "a standard gauge displays an indicator that shows the current value's location; a gauge that uses the capacity style displays a fill that stops at the value's location on the path." "Write succinct labels that describe the current value and both endpoints of the range." "Consider filling the path with a gradient to help communicate the purpose of the gauge." — [HIG Gauges](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/gauges) (new page September 23, 2022)
- [HIG] macOS level indicator detail that transfers as a pattern: discrete capacity is "a horizontal row of separate, equally sized, rectangular segments. The number of segments matches the total capacity, and the segments fill completely, never partially". "Consider using the continuous style for large ranges." The fill color can change "when the current value reaches certain levels". — [HIG Gauges](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/gauges)
- [HIG] Charts need a headline in words. "Descriptive text titles, subtitles, and annotations help emphasize the most important information in a chart and can highlight actionable takeaways." "Keep a chart simple, letting people choose when they want additional details." "Not every collection of data needs to be displayed in a chart." — [HIG Charting data](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/charting-data)
- [HIG] A novel progress graphic has to be taught once. "If you need to create a chart that presents data in a novel way, help people learn how to interpret the chart. For example, when a Watch pairs with iPhone, Activity introduces the Activity rings by animating them individually, showing people how each ring maps to the move, exercise, and stand metrics." — [HIG Charting data](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/charting-data)
- [HIG] Rings are expected in fitness apps but locked down. "If your app is related to health or fitness, and especially if it contributes information to HealthKit, people generally expect to find Activity rings in your interface." "Use Activity rings only to show Move, Exercise, and Stand information." "Don't use Activity rings for decoration." — [HIG Activity rings](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/activity-rings) (the "never imitate" guardrail is in the earlier pass)
- [HIG] Workouts: "Use a distinct visual appearance to indicate an active workout." "Provide a summary at the end of a session. A summary screen confirms that a workout is finished and displays the recorded information." "Avoid distracting people from a workout with information that's not relevant." — [HIG Workouts](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/workouts)
- [HIG] Status belongs next to the thing. "Consider integrating status feedback into your interface. When status feedback is available near the items it describes, people get important information without having to take action or leave their current context." — [HIG Feedback](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/feedback)
- [HIG] Confirm completion only when it matters. "When it makes sense, confirm that a significant action or task has completed... It's generally best to reserve this type of confirmation for activities that are sufficiently important, because people typically expect their action or task to succeed". — [HIG Feedback](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/feedback)

**1.7 Widgets and Live Activities as the model of a glanceable summary**

- [HIG] One idea per summary. "Choose simple ideas that relate to your app's main purpose. Include timely content and relevant functionality." "Small widgets use their limited space to typically show a single piece of information while larger sizes support additional layers of information and actions." — [HIG Widgets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/widgets) (change log: October 13, 2026)
- [HIG] Density has two failure modes. "Balance information density. Sparse layouts can make the widget seem unnecessary, while overly dense layouts are less glanceable. Create a layout that provides essential information at a glance and allows people to view additional details by taking a longer look. If your layout is too dense, consider improving its clarity by using a larger widget size or replacing text with graphics." — [HIG Widgets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/widgets)
- [HIG] "Display only the information that's directly related to the widget's main purpose." "Prefer dynamic information that changes throughout the day. If a widget's content never appears to change, people may not keep it in a prominent position." — [HIG Widgets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/widgets)
- [HIG] Interaction inside a summary stays small. "Offer simple, relevant functionality and reserve complexity for your app." "Avoid creating app-like layouts in your widgets." The Reminders widget "includes toggles to mark a task as completed." Taps elsewhere "deep link to details and actions that directly relate to the widget's content, and don't make people navigate to the relevant area in the app." — [HIG Widgets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/widgets)
- [HIG] Numbers: text "at 11 points or larger"; update animations "with a duration of up to two seconds". "Use color to enhance a widget's appearance without competing with its content." — [HIG Widgets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/widgets)
- [HIG] A caution for in-app summary cards. "Avoid mirroring your widget's appearance within your app. Including an element in your app that looks like your widget but doesn't behave like it can confuse people." — [HIG Widgets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/widgets)
- [HIG] Live Activities. "A Live Activity lets people track the progress of an activity, event, or task at a glance." "Focus on important information that people need to see at a glance. Your Live Activity doesn't need to display everything." "Use large, heavier-weight text, a medium weight or higher. Use small text sparingly and make sure key information is legible at a glance." Best for "a defined beginning and end" and no more than eight hours. — [HIG Live Activities](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/live-activities) (change log: October 13, 2026)
- [HIG] Height follows content. "When there's less information to show, reduce the height of the Live Activity to only use the space needed for the content. When more information becomes available, increase the height". — [HIG Live Activities](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/live-activities)
- [WWDC] "Think about the information you would find most useful and prioritize the sizing and scale of those elements to be visible at a quick glance." Buttons "should only be used if they're controlling an essential part of the activity itself, as they can use up a lot of space that could otherwise be displaying useful information." — [WWDC23 10194, Design dynamic Live Activities](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10194/)
- [WWDC] A glance budget, stated for Apple Watch. "Remember when designing your widgets to only show what is necessary to ensure glanceability. It helps to remember that people should only be spending around ten seconds max engaging with glanceable information on their Watch." Layouts offered include "an integrated bar gauge" and "an integrated circular graphic"; the Audiobook widget "uses the bar gauge layout to quickly give people a sense of where they are in the book". — [WWDC23 10309, Design widgets for the Smart Stack on Apple Watch](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10309/)
- [WWDC] Relevance decides what is on top. The Smart Stack "intelligently orders the widgets in it so that the most relevant one floats to the top"; Calendar raises an event "occurring in the next hour"; Activity is raised by "the presence of an active Workout". People can also "manually add, remove, and pin their widgets to the top." — [WWDC23 10309](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10309/)

**1.8 SF Symbols and color for meaning**

- [HIG] State through variants. "You could use the slash variant of a symbol to show that an item or action is unavailable, or use the fill variant to indicate selection." Fill gives "more visual emphasis, making it a good choice for iOS tab bars". — [HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols) (change log: July 28, 2025)
- [HIG] Variable color is for a changing quantity. "With variable color, you can represent a characteristic that can change over time, like capacity or strength". "Use variable color to communicate change; don't use it to communicate depth." (Punctuation adjusted.) — [HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols)
- [HIG] Animations have meanings. Bounce "can help communicate that an action occurred or needs to take place." Wiggle can "highlight a change or a call to action that a person might overlook." Replace (up-up) "communicates a change in state that includes a sense of forward progression." Draw On can "convey progress". Pulse and Breathe signal ongoing activity. — [HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols)
- [HIG] Restraint. "Apply symbol animations judiciously. While there's no limit to how many animations you can add to a view, too many animations can overwhelm an interface and distract people." "Make sure that animations serve a clear purpose in communicating a symbol's intent." — [HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols)
- [HIG] Multicolor symbols carry built-in meaning: "the leaf symbol uses green", "trash.slash symbol uses red to signal data loss." — [HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols)
- [WWDC] Familiar symbols make tabs recognizable; the presenter replaces custom icons with SF Symbols because they "are already familiar to people on any Apple platform". — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)

**1.9 Tab bars: naming tabs, a Home tab, hiding or customizing tabs**

- [WWDC] Labels should tell the app's story. Tabs "should be meaningful and descriptive." "They tell a story about what the app can do just by displaying concise labels." In a good example the tabs "are so focused that their functionality is self-evident". "Finally, use clear and concise labels for each tab." — [WWDC22 10001, Explore navigation design for iOS](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10001/)
- [WWDC] Each tab is a decision. "Each extra tab means one more decision for people to make and might present the app as much more complex than what it really is." Ask "What's truly essential? What deserves a tab?" Labels and icons should "help people get a sense of what each tab is for, so they don't have to interact just to find out, or end up skipping it because they're unsure what it leads to." — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)
- [HIG] "Include tab labels to help with navigation... Use single words whenever possible." "It's generally easier to navigate among fewer tabs." "Use a tab bar to support navigation, not to provide actions." — [HIG Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars) (change log: June 8, 2026). The WWDC25 session applies it: Add was removed from the tab bar because "tabs are for navigation, not for taking action" and moved "inside Records where someone is most likely to use it". — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)
- [WWDC] The first tab tends to get overloaded. "The first tab of apps tend to be loaded heaviest with features. Try to create balance in your interface by distributing functionality throughout your tabs." "It can be tempting to add all your functionality into one tab... because it's available at a glance." — [WWDC22 10001](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10001/)
- [WWDC] Apple's warning about Home tabs. "A tab titled Home may seem like an attractive catchall to showcase functionality throughout an app in a single place." "Home becomes the tab where every feature is fighting for real estate, because the tab is trying to solve a problem of discoverability." "When it's your app's functionality, which are the actions people can take to achieve things, the redundancy creates confusion." "Never force someone to change tabs automatically." The session separates content from functionality: "it can make sense to have similar types of content... exist on many tabs, but organized in a different way." — [WWDC22 10001](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10001/)
- [WWDC] How to choose a name (2026). Three criteria: it belongs, it sets the right expectation ("when someone reads a name, they're already predicting what they'll find"), and it works everywhere. "Sometimes the most obvious word is the right one because it's already doing the job." Example: "Balance" beats "Spending Power" and "Current Funds". Clever names carry "a learning curve... That friction makes choosing harder." Test: "Take the names and drop them into the sentences you might read or say to a friend." Name by what it does for the person, not the technology. "For a feature like this, a verb works well because we're putting the person using it in control." "Names build on each other, and over time, they become the language of your app." — [WWDC26 290, Craft clear names for features and labels in your app](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/290/)
- [HIG] Customizing tabs is described for iPadOS. "Let people customize the tab bar. In apps with a lot of sections that people might want to access, it can be useful to let people select items that they use frequently and add them to the tab bar, or remove items that they use less frequently... aim for a default list of five or fewer". — [HIG Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars)
- [WWDC] "People expect to hide non-essential tabs or to rearrange groups in the sidebar. Customizations to the order and visibility are automatically persisted." Core tabs can be locked: "the most important tabs in the app, like 'Watch Now' or 'Library' are fixed and always available." Tabs can also be hidden by default (`defaultVisibility`). — [WWDC24 10147, Elevate your tab and sidebar experience in iPadOS](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10147/)

**1.10 Settings and customization (edit mode for summaries)**

- [HIG] Defaults first. "Aim to provide default settings that give the best experience to the largest number of people." "When you choose appropriate default settings, people may not have to make any adjustments before they can start enjoying your app". — [HIG Settings](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/settings) (change log: June 10, 2024)
- [HIG] Few settings. "Minimize the number of settings you offer. Although people appreciate having control over an app or game, too many settings can make the experience feel less approachable, while also making it hard to find a particular setting." — [HIG Settings](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/settings)
- [HIG] Where a show or hide control lives. "When possible, prefer letting people modify task-specific options without going to your settings area. For example, if people can adjust things like showing or hiding parts of the current view, reordering a collection of items, or filtering a list, make these options available in the screens they affect, where they're discoverable and convenient. Putting this type of option in a separate settings area disconnects it from its context, requiring people to suspend their task to make adjustments, and often hiding the results until people resume the task." — [HIG Settings](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/settings)
- [HIG] What does belong in Settings. "Put general, infrequently changed settings in your custom settings area. People must suspend what they're doing to open an app's or game's settings area, so you want to include options that people don't need to change all the time." — [HIG Settings](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/settings)
- [HIG] Toggles. "Use a toggle to help people choose between two opposing values that affect the state of content or a view." "Clearly identify the setting, view, or content the toggle affects." — [HIG Toggles](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/toggles)
- [WWDC] Apple's 2026 principle of flexibility supports rearranging. "Sometimes, the best option is to let people personalize your experience to suit their own preferences. It's really hard to nail down a single layout that works perfectly for every person, so offer people the flexibility to rearrange them, to support their personal workflow." — [WWDC26 250](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/250/)
- [Apple support] Apple's own summary screens use an in-place edit mode. Health: "Tap Summary, then tap Edit next to Pinned"; reorder by dragging. — [iPhone User Guide, View your health data](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/view-your-health-data-iphe3d379c32/ios). Fitness: "Personalize the Summary tab in the Fitness app to see what matters to you like your Activity and workout details, Awards, and more." — [Apple Fitness, App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/apple-fitness/id1208224953)
- [Practitioner] How the Fitness edit mode behaves, from how-to articles, not from Apple: an "Edit Summary" control at the bottom of the Summary page; cards jiggle; a minus badge removes a card, an Add button brings cards back, drag reorders; the Activity rings card at the top "can't be moved or deleted". — [iDrop News](https://www.idropnews.com/how-to/how-to-customize-your-iphones-fitness-summary/227225), [iPhone in Canada](https://www.iphoneincanada.ca/2025/01/02/customize-your-fitness-app-summary-tab/)
- [HIG] Restore state. "Restore the previous state when your app restarts so people can continue where they left off." — [HIG Launching](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/launching)

**1.11 Liquid Glass era hierarchy**

- Covered in the earlier pass (hierarchy "through layout and grouping", no glass in the content layer). New here: the color rule in 1.2, and the brand session's advice to "move color into the content area of your app, into the scroll view" so that glass controls pick it up. — [WWDC26 251](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/251/)

### Inferences

- A redesigned Orby screen passes Apple's bar when a person can answer "where am I, what can I do, where can I go from here" from the title, one colored action and the first card, without reading body text. That is a usable acceptance test for all four screens.
- "Plan your day" should behave like a medium widget inside the app: one purpose, a few rows, one toggle or tap per row, a deep link for detail. It should not look like an actual Orby widget, because Apple warns against mirroring a widget in the app.
- For Goals and Fitness progress, Apple's vocabulary is gauge (capacity style, with the current value and both endpoints labeled) plus a one-line text headline. A segmented (discrete) capacity bar suits small whole-number targets such as "2 of 4 runs"; a continuous fill suits miles.
- "What did you learn today?" is more than "a small amount of information", so by Apple's definition it is a text view, sized to hint at a sentence or two, with the question kept visible as a label above the field and a hint inside it.
- Apple's Settings guidance splits the new setting in two. "Choose what the home screen shows" is a show, hide and reorder option, so it belongs on the home screen itself as an edit mode (the Health "Edit next to Pinned" and Fitness "Edit Summary" model), with Settings offering at most a link to it. "Hide the Fitness area entirely" is general and rarely changed, so a Settings toggle fits.
- The HIG rule "don't disable or hide tab bar buttons" is about the app changing tabs on its own when content is missing. A tab that the person has chosen to turn off is closer to the iPadOS customization guidance (persisted, predictable, core tabs locked). Apple gives no iPhone-specific rule for user-hidden tabs, so this is a reading, not a quote.
- The WWDC22 Home tab warning applies directly to "Plan your day": it may summarize content from other tabs, but it should not duplicate their actions, and a tap on it should not silently switch tabs.
- By the WWDC26 naming test, "Fitness" is the stronger tab name than "Plan": it is the obvious word, it predicts the content, and it does not collide with "Plan your day" on the home screen. Two different things called "plan" breaks "names build on each other".

### Gaps

- The HIG has no dedicated "Empty states" page. Apple's guidance is spread across Writing, Tab bars, `ContentUnavailableView` and the 2022 writing session. No Apple source gives a size, an illustration rule or a button count for empty states.
- No Apple source found states a number of tabs for iPhone on the HIG page (the earlier pass notes the UIKit limit of five).
- Apple publishes no design rationale or usage data for "Edit Summary" in Fitness or "Pinned" in Health. The behavior details come from third-party how-to articles.
- No Apple guidance was found for a chip component or for "suggested replies" under a text field on iPhone (outside the generative AI and keyboard pages, which were not read in depth).
- No WWDC 2026 session specifically about progress, streaks or habit design was found in the design catalog; the 2026 design sessions read were 250, 251 and 290.

## 2. Google Material 3 guidance on the same points

### Takeaway

Material gives the primary action its own floating component (the FAB) and a five-step button ladder, where Apple uses one prominent in-content or toolbar button and keeps actions out of the tab bar. Material's older empty state rule (image plus a tagline that does not look tappable) is more passive than Apple's and everyone else's current advice to include a next step.

### Cited Findings

- [Android doc] FAB definition. "A Floating Action Button (FAB) is a high-emphasis button that lets the user perform a primary action in an application. It promotes a single, focused action that is the most common pathway a user might take and is typically found anchored to the bottom right of the screen." Use cases listed: create new item, add new contact, center location. Four types: FAB, small, large, extended (icon plus text). — [Android Developers, Floating action button](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/fab)
- [Material] "A floating action button (FAB) represents the primary action of a screen." "A FAB performs the primary or the most common action on a screen." Sizes in the docs' figures: 56 dp default, 96 dp large; "Medium FAB (most recommended)"; the 40 dp small FAB is marked deprecated. — [material-components-android, FloatingActionButton.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/components/FloatingActionButton.md)
- [Material] The FAB "represents the most important action on a screen. It puts key actions within reach." "Extended FABs help people take primary actions"; they are wider "to accommodate a text label and larger target area" and "are the only FABs that can be used without an icon." — [material-web, fab.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-web/blob/main/docs/components/fab.md)
- [Android doc] Button ladder. Filled: "High-emphasis buttons. These are for primary actions in an application, such as 'submit' and 'save.'" Filled tonal: "also for primary or significant actions". Elevated: similar to tonal. Outlined: "Medium-emphasis buttons, containing actions that are important but not primary." Text: "Low-emphasis buttons, ideal for less critical actions such as navigational links, or secondary functions like 'Learn More' or 'View details.'" — [Android Developers, Button](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/button)
- [Material] Cards. "Cards contain content and actions about a single subject." Three types: elevated, filled, outlined. "Each provides the same legibility and functionality, so the type you use depends on style alone." Anatomy: container, headline, subhead, supporting text, image, buttons. — [material-components-android, Card.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/components/Card.md)
- [Material] Progress. "Progress indicators inform users about the status of ongoing processes, such as loading an app or submitting a form." They "may be determinate to show progress, or indeterminate for an unspecified amount of progress." Two types, linear and circular. — [material-web, progress.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-web/blob/main/docs/components/progress.md)
- [Android doc] Chips as prompts. Four types. Assist: "Guides the user during a task." Suggestion: "Provides recommendations to the user based on their recent activity or input. Typically appear beneath an input field to prompt user actions." Filter and Input cover refining and showing entered items. "Suggestion chips present dynamically generated hints." — [Android Developers, Chip](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/chip)
- [Material] Empty states (Material Design 1, about 2014 to 2017). "Empty states occur when an item's content can't be shown." "Although these states aren't typical, they should be designed to prevent user confusion." "The most basic empty state displays a non-interactive image and a text tagline." The image "is subtle and neutral with respect to the background"; the tagline "has a positive tone" and "conveys the purpose of the app without appearing to be actionable". The "don't" example: a bright image and a tagline "worded like a call to action, which may encourage users to touch either the image or the tagline". — [Material Design 1, Empty states](https://m1.material.io/patterns/empty-states.html)
- [Material] Alternatives to a truly empty screen. Starter content: "The most compelling way for new users to learn and get excited about your app is by using it." "Use content that has broad appeal and demonstrates primary features"; "Give users the ability to delete and replace this content". Educational content: "Help users understand what they'll be able to do on this screen once it has content"; "Make it possible to dismiss or skip this content"; "Keep it brief". — [Material Design 1, Empty states](https://m1.material.io/patterns/empty-states.html)

**Where Apple and Google differ**

| Point | Apple (iPhone) | Google (Material) |
|---|---|---|
| Home of the primary action | A prominent button in the content or one primary toolbar item on the trailing side; never in the tab bar ([HIG Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars), [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)) | A floating button over the content, bottom right ([Android Developers, FAB](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/fab)) |
| Emphasis levels | Prominent, tinted, neutral, plain (earlier pass) | Filled, tonal, elevated, outlined, text ([Android Developers, Button](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/button)) |
| How color marks the action | Color on the button background, not the label; not on several controls ([HIG Color](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color)) | Filled container; FAB color variants surface, primary, secondary, tertiary ([material-web fab.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-web/blob/main/docs/components/fab.md)) |
| Empty state | Title, explanation, and a button or link where possible ([HIG Writing](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing), [ContentUnavailableView](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/contentunavailableview)) | M1: neutral image and a tagline that must not look actionable; or starter or educational content ([M1 Empty states](https://m1.material.io/patterns/empty-states.html)) |
| "Progress indicator" | A transient wait indicator; goal progress is a gauge, chart or ring ([HIG Progress indicators](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/progress-indicators), [HIG Gauges](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/gauges)) | Status of an ongoing process such as loading ([material-web progress.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-web/blob/main/docs/components/progress.md)) |
| Suggested inputs | "Offer choices instead of requiring text entry" as a principle; no chip component page found ([HIG Entering data](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/entering-data)) | Named components: assist and suggestion chips ([Android Developers, Chip](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/chip)) |
| Cards | No card component; prefers lists and rows for text (earlier pass) | Card is a core component for "a single subject" ([Card.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/components/Card.md)) |

### Inferences

- On iPhone, the Material FAB translates to Orby's one Sky button placed in the hero card, or a single toolbar action. A floating button over a scrolling list is a Google idiom and would also compete with Orby's floating dock.
- Material's "single subject" test for a card is a useful check for Goals: one goal per card or row, with its progress and its one action, and nothing unrelated.
- Material's starter content idea (real, deletable examples in place of an empty screen) fits Goals and the Learned box better than an illustration, because it shows the shape of a good entry.
- Material's old "tagline must not look actionable" rule exists to prevent a fake button. The safe reading for Orby is to make the empty state's action a real, standard button and keep any illustration plainly decorative.

### Gaps

- `m3.material.io` pages (FAB, buttons, cards, progress indicators, chips guidelines) could not be read directly. M3 do and don't captions, the exact "one FAB per screen" wording and current dp specifications are unverified here. The quotes above come from Google's developer docs and component repositories.
- No Material 3 empty states page was found or verified. The M1 page is old; treat its "not actionable" rule as historical.
- No Material guidance was found on letting people hide features or edit a home screen.

## 3. Nielsen Norman Group, Baymard, GOV.UK and similar on tests, recognition, labels, empty states, progress, discoverability, customization and fewer options

### Takeaway

The research firms and GOV.UK agree with Apple and add numbers: visible, plainly labeled options get used far more than hidden ones; weak visual cues for tappable things cost about a fifth more time; a single clear call to action beats several; almost nobody changes settings, so the default is the design; and an empty area should state status, teach in place and offer one direct way to start.

### Cited Findings

**3.1 Recognition over recall, and visible options**

- [NN/g] "The difference between recognition and recall is the number of cues." "Recognition is easier than recall because it involves more cues". Design rule: make "information and interface functions visible and easily accessible". Menus are recognition; command lines and gestures are recall. Showing history helps "because users don't need to remember interesting information." Contextual tips beat tutorials because "tutorials have a lot of information, but they are not rehearsed much." — [NN/g, Memory Recognition and Recall in User Interfaces](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/recognition-and-recall/) (Raluca Budiu; page dated January 15, 2024 when read)
- [NN/g, study] Hidden navigation is used less. 179 participants, 6 live sites, phones and desktops. On mobile, navigation was used in 57 percent of tasks when hidden and 86 percent when partly visible; on desktop 27 percent hidden against 48 percent visible. Content discoverability fell "more than 20%" with hidden navigation. Task difficulty was rated 21 percent higher. On mobile people were 15 percent slower. Recommendation: "If your site has 4 or fewer top-level navigation links, display them as visible links." — [NN/g, Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation Hurt UX Metrics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/) (Pernice and Budiu, June 26, 2016)
- [NN/g, study] Weak signals for tappable things cost time. Eyetracking, 71 users, 9 page pairs. People "spent 22% more time" on pages with weak signifiers (flat text links, ghost buttons) and made 25 percent more fixations, both significant at p < 0.05. In one pair, 86 percent reached the target with strong signifiers against 50 percent with weak ones. Flat designs are safer with "visual simplicity, external consistency, clear visual hierarchy, and contrast." Desktop web, not apps. — [NN/g, Flat UI Elements Attract Less Attention and Cause Uncertainty](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/) (Kate Moran, September 3, 2017)
- [NN/g] Icons need words. "A text label must be present alongside an icon to clarify its meaning in that particular context." "Icon labels should be visible at all times, without any interaction from the user." Only a few icons (home, print, search) are near universal. Rule of thumb: "if it takes you more than 5 seconds to think of an appropriate icon for something", an icon is unlikely to communicate it. — [NN/g, Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/) (Aurora Harley, July 27, 2014)

**3.2 Information scent and labels (plain words for tab names)**

- [NN/g] Information scent is "the user's imperfect estimate of the value that the source will deliver". Labels should be "a succinct yet accurate description". "Link names should be clear and self-explanatory." "Jargon, branded terms, or simply too sophisticated words may end up ignored". Generic labels such as "More" have "low information scent". "It's always good to be as specific as possible with the link names". No statistics in the article. — [NN/g, Information Scent](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/) (Raluca Budiu, February 2, 2020)
- [NN/g] "Menus are not the place to get cute with made-up words, internal jargon, or abstract high-level categorization." Make labels easy to scan by "front-loading key terms." "Failing to indicate the current location is probably the single most common mistake we see on website menus." — [NN/g, Menu-Design Checklist: 17 UX Guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/menu-design/) (Page Laubheimer, June 7, 2024)
- [NN/g] "Tab labels should usually be 1-2 words." "Short labels are more scannable; if you need longer labels, it's a sign that the choices are too complicated for tabs." "Use plain language rather than made-up marketing terms." "The fewer tabs, the better." "Use at least two selection indicators to enhance the visual salience of the selected tab." "All caps negatively impacts legibility." — [NN/g, Tabs, Used Right](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tabs-used-right/) (Evan Sunwall, August 2, 2024)
- [GOV.UK] "We should use the same language and the same design patterns wherever possible." (Principle 9, Be consistent, not uniform.) — [Government Design Principles](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles) (last updated April 2, 2025)

**3.3 Empty-state design**

- [NN/g] Three guidelines. (1) Communicate system status: "Totally empty states cause confusion about how and whether the system is working." (2) Provide learning cues, which NN/g calls "pull revelations" because they appear only where the person is looking: "In most cases, this approach is generally more successful than forced tutorials shown to the user at initial use." Example: "Star your favorites to list them here." (3) Provide direct pathways: "Provide direct pathways (i.e., links) to getting started with key tasks related to populating the empty state." Summary: "Do not default to totally empty states." "Don't let empty-state design be an afterthought". The article is about complex web applications. — [NN/g, Designing Empty States in Complex Applications: 3 Guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/) (Kate Kaplan, September 19, 2021)
- [Design system] Polaris. Empty states should "orient merchants by clearly explaining the benefit and utility of a product or feature", "be encouraging and never make merchants feel unsuccessful or guilty because they haven't used a product or feature", and "use only one primary call-to-action button". Title: "Be action-oriented"; do "Create orders and send invoices", don't "Orders and invoices". Button: "use the {verb}+{noun} format"; do "Create order", don't "New order". — [Shopify Polaris, Empty state (repository source)](https://github.com/Shopify/polaris/blob/main/polaris.shopify.com/content/components/layout-and-structure/empty-state.mdx) (the legacy Polaris site now redirects to shopify.dev)
- [Design system] Atlassian. "Include an action or link to help people understand what to do next." Don't "include too many call to action buttons on one page." Only the heading is required. Tone depends on cause: a first-time blank slate may be "inspirational, motivating, or educational"; an all-done state "could have a more celebratory tone and illustration." "Try to limit the number of words in the message as much as possible." Illustrations are optional and never negative. — [Atlassian Design System, Empty state usage](https://atlassian.design/components/empty-state/usage)
- [Design system] Carbon. Three types: no data, user action, error. The no data goal: "User understands what will be available on the page when data has been added or is available." Title: "A short and concise explanation." "Where possible, write this as a positive statement." "Keep words to a minimum so they are fast to read and act upon." "Don't cover multiple options in one empty state." "As a general rule, don't lead the user into a dead end." When several empty states show at once, "we recommend using a tertiary button for the call to action" so there are not several primary buttons. In small tiles, if space is tight, "use just text." "Empty states should replace the element that would ordinarily show." Starter content is "pre-built content that allows new users to get started with an app quickly." — [IBM Carbon, Empty states pattern](https://carbondesignsystem.com/patterns/empty-states-pattern/)

**3.4 Onboarding and help**

- [NN/g] "Avoid creating app onboarding whenever possible and instead spend your resources making the UI more usable." "Users should be able to learn the interface by using it." "Test the app with no onboarding before investing money into adding extra screens." "Instructional onboarding should not be used to supplement poor design." If used, it "should be brief, optional, and should only highlight the minimum that users need to know." "We don't recommend deck-of-cards onboarding." Interactive walkthroughs "enable users to learn by doing". "Visual-design customization, such as selecting a color scheme, doesn't belong in onboarding." "Content customization can create a relevant experience and is more likely to be appropriate for initial app onboarding." — [NN/g, Mobile-App Onboarding](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-app-onboarding/) (Alita Kendrick, June 21, 2020)

**3.5 Text entry prompts**

- [NN/g] "User testing continually shows that placeholders in form fields often hurt usability more than help it." Among seven problems: "Disappearing placeholder text strains users' short-term memory"; "users' eyes are drawn to empty fields", so a field that already shows text is noticed less; placeholder text can be mistaken for an answer already filled in. "The best solution is to have clear, visible labels that are placed outside empty form fields." Hints "should also be persistent and placed outside of the field." — [NN/g, Placeholders in Form Fields Are Harmful](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/form-design-placeholders/) (Katie Sherwin, May 11, 2014; reviewed 2018)
- [Baymard] "In every usability test we've conducted inline labels have suffered from major usability problems." The main loss is context: once the label is gone, "the only context for the field is the user's own input." Exception: a single standalone field, or a very common one or two field form, may use an inline label; "three or more fields" always need separate labels. Placeholder text suits "brief descriptions" such as a format example. — [Baymard, Mobile Form Usability: Never Use Inline Labels](https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-forms-avoid-inline-labels) (Jamie Holst, June 4, 2013)
- [GOV.UK] "Start by asking one question per page". "Asking just one question per question page helps users understand what you're asking them to do". The question can be the page heading by using the field's label as the heading. "Make sure to only ask for a piece of information once". — [GOV.UK Design System, Question pages](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages/)

**3.6 Visual indicators of progress and status**

- [NN/g] Heuristic 1. "Visibility of system status: How well the state of the system is conveyed to its users." "Only by knowing what the current system status is can you change it". "A lack of information often equates to a lack of control". "The predictability of the interaction creates trust". Tips include changing a button's look or adding a checkmark after a tap, and showing how much more is needed to reach a threshold (the free shipping example). — [NN/g, Visibility of System Status](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visibility-system-status/) (Aurora Harley, June 3, 2018)
- [NN/g] Wait indicators, with thresholds: "Use a progress indicator for any action that takes longer than about 1.0 second." Looped animation for 2 to 10 seconds; percent-done for "10 or more seconds". A cited University of Nebraska-Lincoln study found people shown a moving bar "were willing to wait on average 3 times longer". — [NN/g, Progress Indicators Make a Slow System Less Insufferable](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/) (Katie Sherwin, October 26, 2014). This applies to Orby's assistant replies and syncing, not to goal progress.
- [Practitioner, with primary sources] Goal-gradient effect. "The tendency to approach a goal increases with proximity to the goal." Takeaway: "Provide a clear indication of progress in order to motivate users to complete tasks." Origin: Clark Hull's hypothesis (1932) and rat runway study (1934). Further reading lists Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, "The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected". — [Laws of UX, Goal-Gradient Effect](https://lawsofux.com/goal-gradient-effect/)
- [Peer-reviewed] Endowed progress. People "provided with artificial advancement toward a goal" show "greater persistence toward reaching the goal." The abstract's example is "converting a task requiring eight steps into a task requiring 10 steps but with two steps already complete", which raises the likelihood of completion and shortens completion time. The effect depends on the reason given for the head start. Nunes and Drèze, Journal of Consumer Research 32(4), March 2006, pages 504 to 512. — [The Endowed Progress Effect: How Artificial Advancement Increases Effort](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/32/4/504/1787425)
- [Practitioner] Zeigarnik effect. "People remember uncompleted or interrupted tasks better than completed tasks." Origin: Bluma Zeigarnik, 1920s. — [Laws of UX, Zeigarnik Effect](https://lawsofux.com/zeigarnik-effect/)
- [GOV.UK] A checklist shows progress through per-task status, with no separate progress bar. "Group related actions into tasks" and "show the status of the tasks." Task names "start with verbs". "Start with the smallest number of different statuses you think might work." Completed is quiet: "black text with no background colour." Cannot start yet is "grey text" and the row "should not be linked." "Where possible, allow users to complete tasks in any order." Use statuses that are "adjectives rather than verbs." "Only use a complete multiple tasks page for longer transactions". — [GOV.UK Design System, Complete multiple tasks](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/complete-multiple-tasks/)
- [GOV.UK] Step counters are not always worth it. "A number of GOV.UK services have removed this style of progress indicator without any negative effects"; the Carer's Allowance team removed a 12-step indicator with "no effect on completion rates or times". — [GOV.UK Design System, Question pages](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages/)

**3.7 Feature discoverability and progressive disclosure**

- [NN/g] "It must be obvious how users progress from the primary to the secondary disclosure levels", and the progression "should have strong information scent." Use "frequency-of-use statistics" to decide what goes first. (The two-level limit is in the earlier pass.) — [NN/g, Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- [NN/g] Feature promotion works in context: "highlight features while the user is in the app, through contextual help". — [NN/g, Mobile-App Onboarding](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-app-onboarding/)

**3.8 Customization, defaults and how few people change settings**

- [Practitioner, study] "Less than 5% of the users we surveyed had changed any settings at all." The survey covered 150+ settings in Microsoft Word, from "several hundred" submitted config files. People assumed the defaults were deliberate: "Microsoft must know what they are doing." Spool's closing question: "Are the defaults the optimal choice?" The exact sample size and study year are not given. — [Jared Spool, Do users change their settings?](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/) (UIE, September 14, 2011)
- [NN/g] "Customization is done by the user." "Personalization is done by the system being used." Customization's cost: "many users don't know what they actually need," and "most users are not interested in doing the work required to tweak the user interface." "Personalization and customization should enhance an already good experience, rather than try to fix a poor one." The designer should "prioritize the ideas and create a tight base-level design". "Customization works well under the assumption that users know best what their goals and needs are." No percentages in this article. — [NN/g, Customization vs. Personalization in the User Experience](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/customization-personalization/) (Amy Schade, July 10, 2016)
- [NN/g] Defaults steer behavior. In a search study the top result drew 42 percent of clicks and the second 8 percent; with the two swapped, the top position still drew 34 percent and the second 12 percent. For forms: "pre-populate fields with the most common value". "By educating and guiding users, default values help reduce errors." "It's therefore important to select helpful defaults." — [NN/g, The Power of Defaults](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-power-of-defaults/) (Jakob Nielsen, September 25, 2005)

**3.9 Minimizing options and calls to action**

- [GOV.UK] "Avoid using multiple default buttons on a single page. Having more than one main call to action reduces their impact, and makes it harder for users to know what to do next." "Pages with too many calls to action make it hard for users to know what to do next." "Write button text in sentence case, describing the action it performs." — [GOV.UK Design System, Button](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/)
- [GOV.UK] "Do less." "Do the hard work to make it simple": "Making something simple to use is much harder." "Start with user needs": "If you don't know what the user needs are, you won't build the right thing." — [Government Design Principles](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles)
- [NN/g] "As the number of choices increases, so does the effort required to collect information and make good decisions." Observation: a drink dispenser with 100+ flavors took some people over a minute against under 10 seconds for a plain one; time on task "bloated by over 500%". "Keeping the number of options at a reasonable level allows people to make decisions more easily and complete tasks faster." An observation, not a controlled study. — [NN/g, Simplicity Wins over Abundance of Choice](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/simplicity-vs-choice/) (Hoa Loranger, November 22, 2015)
- [Practitioner] Hick's law. "The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices." Takeaways: "Avoid overwhelming users by highlighting recommended options." "Break complex tasks into smaller steps". "Be careful not to simplify to the point of abstraction." Origin: Hick and Hyman, 1952. — [Laws of UX, Hick's Law](https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/)
- [Practitioner] Von Restorff effect. "When multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs from the rest is most likely to be remembered." "Make important information or key actions visually distinctive." "Use restraint when placing emphasis on visual elements to avoid them competing with one another". Origin: Hedwig von Restorff, 1933. — [Laws of UX, Von Restorff Effect](https://lawsofux.com/von-restorff-effect/)

### Inferences

- The owner's wish for a next move that is obvious "in a visually engaging, stimulating way" and the "not overwhelmed with buttons" constraint are the same requirement seen from two sides: the isolation effect only works when exactly one element differs, so engagement comes from making the single action vivid and everything else quiet, not from adding more vivid things.
- Because under 5 percent of people change settings in the one study available, the default home screen and the default tab set are what nearly every person will see. The hide and reorder setting should be treated as a courtesy for a few, and the default should be redesigned as if the setting did not exist. Customization must not be the fix for a cluttered home.
- The same data argues against asking people to configure the home screen during onboarding. NN/g allows content customization at first run only when brief; Apple says postpone nonessential customization.
- For the "What did you learn today?" box, the evidence supports this arrangement: the question stays visible above the field as the label (GOV.UK question as heading, NN/g, Apple), a short example may sit inside as a hint (Baymard's single-field exception), and no essential instruction lives only in the placeholder.
- For Goals and "Plan your day", the GOV.UK checklist gives a tested vocabulary: verb-first row names, the fewest statuses that work, done rows quiet instead of loud, any order allowed. A separate progress bar is optional when the per-row status already shows progress.
- Endowed progress suggests starting a ring, bar or checklist with honest progress already showing (for example, today's first item counted as soon as the day is opened or a goal is created) and saying why. The paper warns the reason for the head start matters, so it should not be fake.
- Empty states for a habit should carry all three NN/g jobs in one small block: status ("No goals yet"), a learning cue in place ("Goals you add show up here with a weekly target"), and one direct verb-plus-noun action ("Add goal"). Polaris and Carbon both cap it at one primary action.
- Tab labels: one word, plain, no brand terms. NN/g's "made-up marketing terms" warning and Apple's 2026 naming session point the same way as the inference in section 1 on "Fitness" versus "Plan".

### Gaps

- NN/g's own five-second test material is a 3-minute video ([5-Second Usability Test, Katie Sherwin, April 20, 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/5-second-usability-test/)); its page gives only a one-line summary, so no NN/g procedure or threshold could be quoted.
- NN/g's article on the Zeigarnik effect in UX returned 404 at the URL tried; the effect is cited from Laws of UX only.
- The widely quoted car wash numbers for endowed progress (34 percent against 19 percent redemption) were not visible on the journal's abstract page and are not verified here. The Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006) abstract page returned 403, so its coffee card figures are also unverified.
- The only quantified source on how few people change settings is Spool's Word survey, reported in 2011 without an exact sample size or date. No mobile app equivalent was found.
- NN/g's hidden navigation and flat UI studies are web studies (2016, 2017). No comparable public study on native iPhone apps was found.
- No Baymard research specific to lifestyle app home screens, dashboards or empty states was found in the public articles; Baymard's public work is mostly e-commerce.
- GOV.UK guidance is written for one-time government transactions, not daily-use apps. Its "one thing per page" and task list patterns transfer as principles, not as evidence about habit apps.

## 4. Named, reusable patterns

### Takeaway

Eight patterns cover the four Orby screens and the new setting. Each has support in at least one official source, though several of the names are practitioner shorthand and not terms the sources use.

### Cited Findings

**4.1 Hero plus one action.** One dominant element states the screen's purpose and current state; one colored button sits with it; everything else is quiet.
- Apple: "the most important item on the screen is always the most obvious one" — [WWDC26 250](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/250/); make the important thing "larger or higher in contrast" — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/); color on the primary action's background only, and not on several controls — [HIG Color](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color); "one large button clearly labeled 'Record Outdoor Walk'" — [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/).
- GOV.UK: more than one main call to action "reduces their impact" — [Button](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/).
- Google: the FAB "promotes a single, focused action that is the most common pathway" — [Android Developers, FAB](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/fab).
- Do: verb-first label that still makes sense read alone ("if you only read the button labels, you would still understand what you were choosing" — [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/)). Do not: two filled buttons, or a hero that is decoration or branding.

**4.2 "Next best action" card.** A single card at the top that changes with context and offers the one thing most worth doing now. The name is practitioner and marketing shorthand; no source read uses it.
- Apple's supporting ideas: "What comes next?" — [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/); grouping by progress so people "pick up where they left off" and the app feels "one step ahead" — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/); relevance ordering in the Smart Stack — [WWDC23 10309](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10309/); "Prefer dynamic information that changes throughout the day" — [HIG Widgets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/widgets).
- NN/g: personalization "can work well if users do not know exactly what they need" — [Customization vs. Personalization](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/customization-personalization/); recommended options reduce decision time — [Laws of UX, Hick's Law](https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/).
- Do not: repeat another tab's actions on the home screen or jump tabs on tap — [WWDC22 10001](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10001/).

**4.3 Checklist with progress.** A short list of verb-first rows, each with a visible state, plus a count or bar.
- GOV.UK statuses and naming rules — [Complete multiple tasks](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/complete-multiple-tasks/).
- Apple: Reminders widget "toggles to mark a task as completed" without opening the app — [HIG Widgets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/widgets); discrete capacity segments that "fill completely, never partially" — [HIG Gauges](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/gauges); status "near the items it describes" — [HIG Feedback](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/feedback).
- Motivation: goal gradient — [Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/goal-gradient-effect/); endowed progress — [Nunes and Drèze 2006](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/32/4/504/1787425).
- Do: keep completed rows quiet (GOV.UK). Do not: add a step counter that duplicates the row states (GOV.UK removed them without harm — [Question pages](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages/)).

**4.4 Guided daily ritual (one thing per step).** A short linear flow that asks one thing at a time. NN/g calls the linear variant staged disclosure.
- GOV.UK: "Start by asking one question per page" — [Question pages](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages/).
- NN/g: "Staged disclosure is a variant in which users step through a linear sequence of options" — [Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/).
- Apple: gate Continue until the required answer exists — [HIG Entering data](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/entering-data); "define the purpose of the entire flow, as well as each screen within it" — [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/); a modal suits "a self-contained task" where the person "has enough information in that view to make decisions and complete a task" — [WWDC22 10001](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10001/).
- Do not: use it for things people do in any order, or when one screen would hold it all.

**4.5 Inline add field.** A field that is always present where new items go, so adding needs no navigation.
- Apple: size the field to the expected text; keep a visible label because the placeholder disappears; purpose at the leading end, extras at the trailing end — [HIG Text fields](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/text-fields).
- NN/g and Baymard: label outside the field; inline label acceptable only for a single standalone field — [NN/g placeholders](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/form-design-placeholders/), [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-forms-avoid-inline-labels).
- NN/g: "users' eyes are drawn to empty fields" — [NN/g placeholders](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/form-design-placeholders/).
- The term "inline add" is practitioner shorthand; no source read names the pattern.

**4.6 Suggested chips.** Two to four tappable starters next to a field, so the person can recognize an answer instead of composing one.
- Google names it: suggestion chips "typically appear beneath an input field to prompt user actions"; assist chips guide "during a task" — [Android Developers, Chip](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/components/chip).
- Apple supports the principle: "offer choices instead of requiring text entry" and prefill sensible defaults — [HIG Entering data](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/entering-data).
- NN/g: recognition needs fewer cues than recall — [Recognition and Recall](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/recognition-and-recall/).
- No source read gives a maximum number of chips.

**4.7 Completion state.** What the area shows once today's thing is done: a quiet confirmation, the result, and the next small step or nothing.
- Apple: empty or finished states "can be times for education or even celebration, like when you complete the last item on your to-do list" — [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/); confirm only significant completions — [HIG Feedback](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/feedback); a workout "summary screen confirms that a workout is finished" — [HIG Workouts](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/workouts); symbol Bounce and Replace to mark the change, used "judiciously" — [HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols).
- Atlassian: an all-done state "could have a more celebratory tone and illustration" — [Empty state usage](https://atlassian.design/components/empty-state/usage).
- GOV.UK: completed status is plain black text — [Complete multiple tasks](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/complete-multiple-tasks/).
- NN/g: change the control's look or add a checkmark so the tap is confirmed — [Visibility of System Status](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visibility-system-status/).

**4.8 Modular home with an edit mode.** A home made of sections that people can hide, show and reorder in place, with sensible defaults and the core section locked.
- Apple: put show, hide and reorder controls "in the screens they affect" — [HIG Settings](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/settings); Health "Edit next to Pinned" — [iPhone User Guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/view-your-health-data-iphe3d379c32/ios); Fitness "Personalize the Summary tab" — [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/apple-fitness/id1208224953); lock essential items, persist the rest — [WWDC24 10147](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10147/); "offer people the flexibility to rearrange" — [WWDC26 250](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/250/).
- Limits: "Minimize the number of settings you offer" — [HIG Settings](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/settings); under 5 percent change settings — [Spool](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/); customization should not "fix a poor" design — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/customization-personalization/).
- Fitness edit mode details (jiggle, minus badge, Add, fixed rings card) are from third-party articles — [iDrop News](https://www.idropnews.com/how-to/how-to-customize-your-iphones-fitness-summary/227225).

**4.9 Two supporting patterns**
- Empty state as first step: status line, one teaching sentence, one verb-plus-noun button, or starter content. — [NN/g empty states](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/), [Polaris](https://github.com/Shopify/polaris/blob/main/polaris.shopify.com/content/components/layout-and-structure/empty-state.mdx), [Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/patterns/empty-states-pattern/), [M1 Empty states](https://m1.material.io/patterns/empty-states.html), [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/)
- Contextual tip: one or two sentences, next to the control, shown after the person has had a chance to find the feature, gone once used. — [HIG Offering help](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/offering-help), [WWDC23 10229](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10229/)

### Inferences

- Mapping to Orby. "Plan your day": patterns 4.2 and 4.3 (a contextual lead row, then a short checklist), with 4.7 once the day is planned. Goals: 4.1 for the current goal with a gauge, 4.3 for the rest, 4.9 for the empty case. "What did you learn today?": 4.5 with 4.6 for starters and 4.7 after saving. Fitness: 4.1 (today's session and one action) over a glanceable week summary built to widget rules. New setting: 4.8, plus one Settings toggle for hiding Fitness.
- Patterns 4.2 and 4.4 pull against each other on the home screen. Apple's Home tab warning and the "not overwhelmed" brief both favor a single contextual card over a multi-step ritual shown inline; a ritual, if wanted, fits a sheet opened from that card.
- A completion state that is quiet most days and celebratory on milestones matches all four sources in 4.7 and keeps celebration meaningful.

### Gaps

- "Next best action", "inline add" and "guided daily ritual" are not terms used by Apple, Google, NN/g or GOV.UK in the pages read. The descriptions are assembled from adjacent guidance.
- No source read gives evidence that any of these patterns improves habit retention in a lifestyle app. The support is guidance and general psychology, not app outcome data.
- No numeric limits were found for chips per field, rows in a checklist card, or sections on a customizable home.

## 5. Concrete tests a team can run on a screenshot

### Takeaway

Six quick tests cover most of what the sources say about clarity: squint, five seconds, first click, the three questions, a count of actions and labels, and a read-only-the-headers pass. Only some come with published thresholds; the rest need a team-set pass mark.

### Cited Findings

**5.1 Squint test (where does the eye land).** Blur or squint at the screenshot and note the first thing that stands out; it should be the screen's most important item.
- [WWDC] "When I squint, my eyes go straight down to the first collection, because it's visually heavier and colorful. Missing half of the content, and sense of place. What's missing here is visual hierarchy." — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)
- Threshold: none published. Apple's pass condition is qualitative: "the most important item on the screen is always the most obvious one" — [WWDC26 250](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/250/). (NN/g's version of the test is in the earlier pass.)

**5.2 Five-second test (is the purpose understood).**
- [Practitioner] Procedure from the method's best-known write-up: give a focused task; tell the person the page will show for five seconds; show it; hide it; ask them to "write down everything they remember about the page"; then ask two questions, "What is the most important information on this page?" and how they would do the task. Pass looks like: "If the page is understandable, users will easily recall the critical content." — [Christine Perfetti, 5-Second Tests: Measuring Your Site's Content Pages](https://articles.centercentre.com/five_second_test/) (UIE, September 11, 2007)
- [Practitioner] Fit: "the technique is best when we use it on pages designed with a single primary purpose." "Home pages and major navigation pages don't yield as valuable results, because they often serve many different tasks." — [Perfetti](https://articles.centercentre.com/five_second_test/)
- [NN/g] NN/g describes it as "a simple usability technique to help designers gauge the audience's first impressions of a webpage." — [NN/g video, 5-Second Usability Test](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/5-second-usability-test/) (Katie Sherwin, April 20, 2018)
- Threshold: none published by either source (no pass percentage, no participant count).

**5.3 First-click test (is the next action obvious).** Show the screenshot with a task ("You want to log today's run. Where do you tap?") and record the first tap, the time to tap, and confidence.
- [Practitioner, study] The origin claim, from Bob Bailey and colleagues across 12 studies in 2007 to 2009: when the first click was on the right path "87% eventually succeed"; when it was not, "only 46% eventually succeed." — [MeasuringU, Getting The First Click Right](https://measuringu.com/first-click/) (Jeff Sauro, October 19, 2011); [MeasuringU, The Importance of the First Choice in Website Navigation](https://measuringu.com/?p=193) (Sauro, December 10, 2013, updated June 2017)
- [Practitioner, study] The replication is weaker on live sites: 5 websites, 21 tasks, 750 users, average 78 percent success after a correct first click against 70 percent after a wrong one, which "didn't match the much larger 2 to 1 ratio Bailey reported." In tree tests (8 studies, 137 tasks, over 1,000 users) it was 80 percent against 14 percent. — [MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/?p=193)
- [Practitioner] How to run: write tasks as problems to solve; define the correct targets first; record the click; "measure time to make the click"; ask confidence on a 7-point scale, where "a few 3's and 4's... are the signposts of navigation problems"; ask difficulty on a 7-point scale and probe anything "less than a 5"; compare old and new designs with task order counterbalanced. One example found significant differences "with just 13 users". — [MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/first-click/)
- Threshold: no published pass rate for the share of correct first taps. The sources give the diagnostic cues above.

**5.4 The three questions test (Apple's own review).** Hand someone the screenshot and ask: Where am I? What can I do? Where can I go from here?
- [WWDC] "When those questions are easy to answer, the app feels inviting and fluid. That's usually the sign of a solid foundation." — [WWDC25 359](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/359/)
- [WWDC] Variant: "What do I pay attention to? What can I interact with? And how do I interact?" — [WWDC26 250](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/250/)
- [WWDC] Variant for copy: state "the most important thing someone needs to know at that moment" in one sentence; that is the screen's purpose, and it should be "right at the top". — [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/)
- Threshold: qualitative ("easy to answer").

**5.5 Counting tests (with the thresholds the sources give)**

| Count on the screenshot | Threshold | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Prominent (filled, colored) buttons | One or two per view (Apple); avoid more than one main call to action per page (GOV.UK) | [HIG Buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons), [GOV.UK Button](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/) |
| Controls with a colored background | One: "refrain from adding color to the background of multiple controls" | [HIG Color](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color) |
| Primary buttons in an empty state | One; one main action per empty state | [Polaris](https://github.com/Shopify/polaris/blob/main/polaris.shopify.com/content/components/layout-and-structure/empty-state.mdx), [Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/patterns/empty-states-pattern/) |
| Words in a tab label | One where possible (Apple); 1 to 2 (NN/g) | [HIG Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars), [NN/g Tabs](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tabs-used-right/) |
| Top-level destinations that can stay visible | 4 or fewer: show them all | [NN/g Hamburger Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/) |
| Customizable tab default list | Five or fewer (iPadOS guidance) | [HIG Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars) |

| Placeholder-only fields (no visible label) | Zero, except a single standalone field | [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/form-design-placeholders/), [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-forms-avoid-inline-labels) |

**5.6 Reading tests for labels and copy**
- [WWDC] Headers and buttons only. Cover the body text and read only the title and the buttons; the purpose and the next step should still be clear. Basis: "People don't always read the text on screen in order, so make sure your headers and buttons are clear", and for buttons, "if you only read the button labels, you would still understand what you were choosing." — [WWDC22 10037](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/)
- [WWDC] Say it in a sentence. Put a feature or tab name into "the sentences you might read or say to a friend"; "a name that reads and sounds natural, that's a name worth exploring further." Then check the three criteria: belongs, sets the right expectation, works everywhere. — [WWDC26 290](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/290/)
- [WWDC] Tabs only. Read the tab bar with the content hidden: do the labels "tell a story about what the app can do"? — [WWDC22 10001](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10001/)
- [NN/g] Icon check. Show an icon without its label and "ask people what they expect the icons to stand for"; for memorability, ask the same people again after a couple of weeks. — [NN/g Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)
- [Design system] Button label check: verb plus noun ("Create order", not "New order"). — [Polaris](https://github.com/Shopify/polaris/blob/main/polaris.shopify.com/content/components/layout-and-structure/empty-state.mdx)
- [NN/g] Empty state check: does the empty area state its status, teach what will appear, and offer one direct way to start? — [NN/g empty states](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/)
- [NN/g] No-onboarding check: "test the app with no onboarding before investing money into adding extra screens." — [NN/g Mobile-App Onboarding](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-app-onboarding/)
- [NN/g] Defaults check: review the screen in its default state, since that is what nearly everyone sees. — [Spool](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/), [NN/g Power of Defaults](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-power-of-defaults/)

### Inferences

- A practical order for each Orby screen: squint (designer alone, seconds), headers-and-buttons read, the counting table, then five-second and first-click with two or three people who have not seen the app. The first three need only a screenshot.
- Perfetti's caveat matters for the home screen: a five-second test suits single-purpose screens (Goals, the Learned box, Fitness) better than a multi-purpose home. For "Plan your day", test the section as its own crop with its own task.
- Since no source gives a pass mark for first-click or five-second tests, the team should set one before testing. Given the replication data, first-click success is best treated as a comparison between the old and new design on the same task, with time to first tap and confidence as tie-breakers, and not as an absolute score.
- The earlier standard uses a "3-second test". None of the sources read here names a three-second version; five seconds is the documented duration.
- The counting table can be turned into a short checklist for the brand-guardian review, since every row is checkable on a screenshot.

### Gaps

- No source gives a numeric pass threshold for the squint test, the five-second test or first-click correctness, or a recommended participant count for five-second tests.
- Bailey and Wolfson's original first-click report was not read directly; its numbers are quoted through MeasuringU, which attributes them to Bailey and 12 studies. Vendor pages repeat the 87 and 46 percent figures without the original.
- No published test was found for "is this screen visually engaging" as distinct from "is it clear". The closest evidence is the first-impression research in the earlier pass.
- No source gives a maximum count of tappable elements per screen. "Counting actions" has thresholds only for prominent buttons, tabs, tips and empty-state actions.
