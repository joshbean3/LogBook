# Show the plan first, ask afterwards

Great onboarding for health, fitness, habit and productivity apps comes down to one rule. **Every screen should either collect an answer that visibly changes what the user gets, or deliver a piece of value. Nothing should take something from the user (an account, a permission, money, a commitment) before it has given them something.** The evidence backing this is uneven. Rigorous studies show that explanatory tutorial decks add nothing, and that people who read them rate tasks as harder. They show that visible effort and personal relevance raise how much people value what comes out, that when-and-where plans help a little (d ≈ 0.15–0.35 after bias correction), and that habits take about two months to form. Most famous onboarding numbers are company anecdotes or vendor marketing. That includes Duolingo's +20% DAU from delaying sign-up, Calm's 3x retention from a reminder prompt, and the idea that long quizzes convert. The most careful published experiment, Headspace with Irrational Labs, carries the warning that matters most. A personalisation quiz doubled course starts but did not change how often people meditated. Retention is the real problem: health and fitness apps keep roughly 3–8% of installers at day 30, while a habit takes a median of 66 days to form. That makes the first morning back after setup the moment that decides whether Logbook works. Logbook's flow already follows most best practice. Its race-goal preview moves as you answer, "Writing your plan" shows real work, the user turns down nudges on Logbook's own screen before iOS asks, and setup ends on a first week rather than a blank dashboard. The changes that matter most are these. Make the AI-data choice an explicit opt-in to meet App Review 5.1.2(i). Get the 8-page tour out of the way of the payoff. Add a first real action and a deliberate path back on day 1. Ask for Apple Health inside the flow, at the moment it pre-fills something. Instrument the funnel without logging health values before adding a paywall.

## Most installers leave by day 30, and onboarding moves starts more than habits

The retention baseline is low and the benchmarks disagree. Adjust-based figures put health and fitness at about **27% day 1, 13% day 7 and 8% day 30**, while an AppsFlyer-based figure puts day 30 nearer **3%** ([Business of Apps](https://businessofapps.com/data/health-fitness-app-benchmarks); [Sonar summarising Adjust](https://trysonar.app/blog/app-user-retention-benchmarks-and-what-moves-it.md)). Both reached the researchers only through secondary summaries. Peer-reviewed panel data on 93 mental-health apps sits at the bottom of that range, with **median day-30 retention of 3.3%** ([Baumel et al. 2019, JMIR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6785720)). A meta-analysis of chronic-disease apps found **43% pooled dropout** even inside studies, with heterogeneity above 99% ([Meyerowitz-Katz et al. 2020](https://www.jmir.org/2020/9/e20283)). The 2–3x spread comes from vendor client mix, paid versus organic installs, and N-day versus unbounded retention definitions ([Amplitude docs](https://amplitude.com/docs/en/analytics/charts/retention-analysis/retention-analysis-interpret)). The practical lesson is to pick one definition and track your own trend, not to chase a cross-vendor number. Amplitude's rule that a 7% day-7 return rate puts a product in the top quarter, and "correlates strongly" with three-month retention, is vendor-published, and its correlation figures were never shown ([Amplitude](https://www.amplitude.com/blog/7-percent-retention-rule)). No peer-reviewed study was found showing how well day 1 or day 7 predicts day 90 in consumer apps.

That baseline collides with how long habits take. In Lally's 84-day study, the median time to reach automaticity was **66 days, ranging from 18 to a projected 254**, and **missing a single day did not derail formation** ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); [UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)). Lally herself now warns that 66 days is not a universal rule ([University of Surrey](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)). The gap between a 30-day retention cliff and a two-month habit curve is the central design problem. Onboarding cannot form the habit. It can only get the user to come back often enough for the habit to start.

The best-documented onboarding experiment sharpens the point. Irrational Labs, Headspace and Purchasely tested five onboarding variants on new trial users. A short "perceived fit" quiz raised course starts from **31% to 63%**. The quiz alone, recommending the same Basics course, added 7.6 points, and a when-where-how precommitment raised app opens 7.5%. **No variant significantly changed active meditation days**, and the precommitment effect faded within 2–3 days ([Irrational Labs](https://irrationallabs.com/case-studies/headspace-doubled-course-starts/)). The post-mortem explains why. Returning users landed on a generic Today page and couldn't find their course, because a one-tap "return to course" modal had been cut. Content didn't match what users expected. Matching over-weighted sleep content. The lesson for Logbook is concrete: a beautiful setup that hands off to a Today screen with no obvious next action wastes its own work.

Famous activation thresholds deserve the same scepticism. Facebook's "7 friends in 10 days" and Slack's "2,000 messages" reach us second-hand, with conflicting details. Both are correlational cut-offs mined from users who were already engaged ([Caroline Frost](https://carolinefrost.github.io/2021/11/01/how-to-build-an-activation-metric); [Pragmatic Data Scientists](https://medium.com/pragmatic-data-scientists/why-is-aha-moment-wrong-ed96ae493a01)). The defensible method has two steps. First, find the early behaviour that separates retained users from churned ones in your own data. Then test whether nudging people toward it causes retention. Most companies skip the second step. No rigorous evidence supports any specific deadline in seconds or minutes for reaching value. Claims like "users decide in N seconds" could not be traced to a method.

The table below grades the claims that most often drive onboarding decisions.

| Claim | Best available evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Explanatory tutorial decks don't help | NN/g between-subjects test, n=70: no gain in success or speed; readers rated ease lower (p=0.047) ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-tutorials/)) | Rigorous but small; immediate usability, not retention |
| Visible effort raises perceived value | Labour illusion, 5 experiments ([Buell & Norton 2011](https://core.ac.uk/download/28952418.pdf)) | Peer-reviewed; not tested in app onboarding |
| A head start raises completion | Car-wash cards: 34% vs 19% redemption ([Nunes & Drèze summary](https://www.coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect)) | Peer-reviewed field study; needs a credible reason for the head start |
| When-and-where plans help | 642 tests; d ≈ 0.35 (trim-and-fill) to 0.15 (robust Bayesian) after bias correction ([Konstanz](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/69905)) | Meta-analytic; small effect |

| Delaying sign-up lifts usage | Duolingo executive: ~20% DAU ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker/)) | Company-reported, no sample size or interval |
| A reminder prompt after first value triples retention | Calm: ~40% opt-in, ~3x retention ([Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/blog/calm-increased-retention-amplitude)) | Vendor case study, ~2016, self-selection only partly addressed |
| Long personalised quizzes convert | Teardowns of Noom, Flo, Runna; no public A/B with a method | Anecdote, confounded by paid acquisition |

| Celebration motion and haptics lift retention | None found; haptics improve subjective ratings, not speed ([York MobileHCI 2013](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/mhci2013d.html)) | No evidence for the retention claim |

## Length pays only when every answer visibly builds the plan

The best flows fall into two camps. "Value first" apps reach core value in about 8 to 12 screens. Duolingo asks "7 quick questions", runs a lesson, and only then asks you to "save your progress" ([ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/articles/duolingo-onboarding-design/); [vc.ru 2026](https://vc.ru/design/3017207-onboarding-duolingo-analiz-ux-i-temnyh-patternov)). Calm puts a breathing exercise in the first minute ([Amplitude 2016](https://amplitude.com/blog/2016/12/06/4-steps-to-measure-user-onboarding)). "Long quiz, then reveal, then paywall" apps run **30 to 110+ screens**. Noom runs 77 to 113, with a projected goal date that updates as you answer ([ScreensDesign: Noom](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/noom-weight-loss-food-tracker); [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/web-to-app-onboarding-funnel)). Runna shows about 30 screens before the plan, then a plan summary and estimated race times ([Revyl Atlas](https://revyl-landing.pages.dev/atlas/runna/report/)). Bevel runs 34 steps ([ScreensDesign: Bevel](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/bevel-health-performance)). What the long flows share is not length itself. Each one pays for its length with a **visible artefact**: a date, a plan, race times, a calorie budget or a hatched pet. Rigorous evidence is consistent with that reading. It does not show that long onboarding is bad. It shows that front-loaded instruction adds nothing and that gates before value cost users. Answering questions and watching real work be done plausibly raises the value of the result through the labour illusion and the IKEA effect, though neither has been tested in app onboarding ([Buell & Norton](https://core.ac.uk/download/28952418.pdf); [Norton, Mochon & Ariely 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002)). The "long quizzes convert" story is confounded. Those flows end in a personalised paywall shown to motivated paid-acquisition audiences. One critic argues a tedious flow simply filters out unmotivated users, and the app then gets credit for motivation people already had ([The Behavioral Scientist](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/fabulous-app-product-critique-onboarding)).

### Which questions earn their place

Questions are worth asking when the answer visibly changes the product, or when it captures a specific when, where and how. Demographic questions with no visible use are the ones to cut. Headspace's quiz raised starts through perceived fit even though the personalised course was no better than the default ([Irrational Labs](https://irrationallabs.com/case-studies/headspace-doubled-course-starts/)). That shows perceived fit is a real lever, and also shows it is not enough on its own. Fabulous's customised onboarding reportedly doubled return visits, but that figure comes from a Firebase case study ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.cn/downloads/Firebase_Fabulous_Case_Study.pdf?hl=it)). The same flow draws heavy criticism for intrusive lifestyle questions asked without saying why, a fingerprint "commitment contract" and loaded notification copy ([The Behavioral Scientist](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/fabulous-app-product-critique-onboarding)). Behavioural levers should be treated as hypotheses with small real-world effects. Implementation intentions shrink from d=0.65 to 0.15–0.35 once publication bias is corrected ([Konstanz](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/69905)). The well-known "sign at the top" honesty finding was **retracted in 2021 for fabricated data** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8463784)). Endowed progress works only when the head start looks earned ([Coglode](https://www.coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect)). Fresh-start timing is well grounded: people are more likely to start new behaviour after temporal landmarks such as a new week or month ([Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)). That makes a plan that starts on a Monday a small, cheap advantage.

For habits specifically, the peer-reviewed evidence argues for **anchoring each habit to an existing routine** rather than to a clock alarm. A review of 115 habit apps found they rely on tracking and reminders. In a four-week study, reminders supported repetition but **hindered automaticity**, while event-based cues built it ([Stawarz et al. 2015](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1468224/)). Streaks are powerful but fragile. Across seven studies, an intact streak raised later engagement and a broken one lowered it. The harm grew when users blamed themselves and shrank when the streak could be repaired ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)). Duolingo's company tests point the same way: separating the daily goal from the streak added 3.3% day-14 retention ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)).

### Doing beats watching, and tours belong after the payoff

The strongest controlled evidence on tutorials is NN/g's between-subjects test of deck-style tutorials in four iOS apps. Task success was 91% for readers versus 94% for skippers, a non-significant difference. Readers rated tasks **harder (4.92 vs 5.49, p=0.047)** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-tutorials/)). Learning science adds that producing or retrieving information beats reading it: the generation effect is about d=0.40 ([Bertsch et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441); [Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)). That evidence comes from verbal-learning experiments, so transfer to UI learning is plausible but unproven. Apple says the same in its own terms. Onboarding should be "fast, fun, and optional", should "teach through interactivity", should consider "a collection of context-specific tips instead of a single onboarding flow", and a skipped tutorial should not come back but should be easy to find in settings ([HIG: Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)). The best flows have largely replaced carousels with learning by doing. Things creates a "Meet Things" project you learn from by completing its to-dos, and needs no account ([Cultured Code](https://culturedcode.com/things/support/articles/2803553/)). Duolingo teaches through a lesson, and Finch through a chat with the pet ([Lazyweb: Finch](https://lazyweb.com/canvas/flows/finch/onboarding)). Subscription apps such as Runna move their guides *after* the plan, with a run-types explainer and coach videos ([ScreensDesign: Runna](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/runna-running-training-plans)). Doist's lesson from its redesign: "Don't let your users start from a blank page" ([Speaker Deck](https://speakerdeck.com/fmerian/how-todoist-redesigned-its-user-onboarding)). Bevel shows the failure mode, where 34 steps end on a completely blank dashboard ([ScreensDesign: Bevel](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/bevel-health-performance)).

## Apple's rules put hard limits on permissions, accounts and AI consent

Apple's guidance is most specific where onboarding meets private data. On timing, the HIG says to request permission "only when your app clearly needs access" and ideally "when people actually use an app feature that requires access". A request belongs inside onboarding only if the app cannot function without it ([HIG: Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy); [HIG: Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)). A custom pre-permission screen is allowed, with conditions. It must have **one button, titled "Continue" or "Next" rather than "Allow"**, that always opens the system alert. It must offer no close or cancel route. Incentives, imitation alerts, pictures of the alert and annotations are named as patterns that get apps rejected ([HIG: Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)). The research notes suggest a compliant workaround (their inference, not Apple text): let the user decline on a feature-choice screen *before* any priming. "No" then never reaches the system alert, so the one-shot iOS prompt is kept for a better moment.

### Notifications: ask in context and offer real choice

Apple's own example for a task app is to request notification authorization "after the person schedules a first task". It also notes that the system prompt appears only once ([Apple: Asking permission](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/asking-permission-to-use-notifications)). **Provisional authorization** can be requested at first launch without a prompt. It delivers quietly to Notification Center with Keep and Turn Off buttons. Capacitor's local-notifications plugin documents no provisional option, so using it would take a small native call ([Capacitor](https://capacitorjs.com/docs/apis/local-notifications)). Industry datasets put iOS push opt-in at about **56% across all apps and 54% for health and fitness** in Pushwoosh's 600-app 2024 data, and at about 49% (iPhone median) in Airship's 2025 report. The category figures reached the researchers via search summaries ([Pushwoosh](https://www.pushwoosh.com/blog/push-notification-benchmarks/); [Airship](https://www.airship.com/blog/your-guide-to-airships-mobile-app-push-notification-benchmarks-for-2026/)). No method-backed data shows how much priming lifts opt-in, or which session is best to ask in. Calm's evidence is the best available. A reminder prompt shown right after the first meditation drew about 40% opt-in, and those users retained about 3x better, matching users who found reminders on their own. Calm read that as evidence of a causal effect ([Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/blog/calm-increased-retention-amplitude)). It remains a vendor case study. App Review 4.5.4 bars requiring push to use the app. The HIG warns against putting sensitive health details in notification text ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); [HIG: Notifications](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/notifications)).

### Apple Health: ask when it does work, and expect silence on denial

The HealthKit HIG asks for data "only when you need it". Its example is asking for weight when someone logs their weight, "not immediately after your app launches". UI copy must say "Apple Health", never "HealthKit". Apps must not build screens that replicate the permission sheet or "affect the flow of health data" ([HIG: HealthKit](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/healthkit)). The API behaviour shapes the UX. **An app cannot tell whether the user denied read access**: denied and empty look identical, and users can also grant only a limited recent window ([Apple: Authorizing access](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/authorizing-access-to-health-data)). Background delivery needs a dedicated entitlement and observer queries registered natively at launch. Step counts are delivered at most hourly ([Apple: enableBackgroundDelivery](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkhealthstore/enablebackgrounddelivery(for:frequency:withcompletion:))). That means a JS-only registration in a Capacitor app would miss wake-ups. Streaks shows the right value exchange: Health access makes tasks complete themselves ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/streaks/id963034692)). No published dataset reports Health permission grant rates, partly because Apple hides read status.

### Third-party AI and health data now need explicit consent

App Review 5.1.2(i) now reads: "You must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, **including with third-party AI**, and obtain explicit permission before doing so" ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Secondary reporting dates the AI wording to 13 November 2025 and says a privacy-policy line alone does not satisfy it ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/de/article/news-apple-app-review-guidelines-ai-data-sharing/); [DEV Community](https://dev.to/arshtechpro/apples-guideline-512i-the-ai-data-sharing-rule-that-will-impact-every-ios-developer-1b0p)). Consent must be withdrawable, and data collected for one purpose may not be repurposed without further consent (5.1.1(ii), 5.1.2(ii)). Health data carries extra limits. It may be disclosed to third parties only to improve health management, and you must disclose the specific health data collected (5.1.3(i)). Apple's HealthKit documentation adds that even with permission, a third party must "also provide a health or fitness service to the user" ([Apple: Protecting user privacy](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/protecting-user-privacy)). Apple publishes no example of a compliant AI consent screen. Whether a pre-enabled switch counts as "explicit permission" is untested. It is the weakest reading of the rule.

### Accounts: delay them, make them easy, make them deletable

The HIG is blunt on accounts: "Delay sign-in for as long as possible. People often abandon apps when they're forced to sign in before they can do anything useful." It also says to explain the benefit on the sign-in screen ([HIG: Managing accounts](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/managing-accounts)). Guideline 5.1.1(v) says apps without significant account-based features should work without a login. It also requires in-app account deletion wherever accounts can be created. Asking for a name must stay optional (5.1.1(x)) ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Apple steers developers toward passkeys, Sign in with Apple and Password AutoFill rather than bare passwords ([HIG: Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)). Offering Google login would require an equivalent privacy-preserving option, which no longer has to be Sign in with Apple ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2024/01/27/sign-in-with-apple-rules-app-store/)). In the teardowns, account-first flows (Runna, Strava, Oura) are the ones where the account is needed immediately for a device or for sync. Elsewhere, deferral backed by "save your progress" is the safer default. The only quantified support is still Duolingo's company-reported result ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker/)). App Tracking Transparency does not apply to an app with no cross-app ad tracking, and showing its prompt anyway costs trust for nothing.

### Launch and splash

Apple says the launch screen "isn't an opportunity for artistic expression". It should be nearly identical to the first screen, carry no text, and show no logos unless they are a fixed part of that first screen. Any splash belongs at the start of onboarding, "just long enough" to absorb at a glance. On relaunch, the app should restore the previous state ([HIG: Launching](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/launching); [HIG: Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)). For web-wrapped apps, guideline 4.2's "beyond a repackaged website" bar is the main review risk. Native Health, notifications and haptics are the strongest evidence of an app-like product ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Capacitor's documented best practice is `launchAutoHide: false` with `hide()` called on first paint ([Capacitor: Splash Screen](https://capacitorjs.com/docs/apis/splash-screen)). Logbook's config already does this.

## Delight is polish, accessibility is required, and the paywall is decided on day 0

The evidence for motion and haptics is thin. Controlled studies find that haptic confirmation improves subjective ratings more than speed or accuracy. It helps most when the finger hides the target ([CHI 2019 reading](https://teaching.vda.univie.ac.at/hci/20s/materials/Lit-Tests/4TouchscreenHaptics.pdf)). Nearly half of one text-entry sample preferred **no haptic or audio feedback at all** ([York University](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/mhci2013d.html)). Claims that celebration screens or micro-interactions lift retention trace back to vendors, most visibly a haptics licensor ([Immersion](https://immersion.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/tactile-feedback-in-mobile-consumer-attitudes-about.pdf)). Teardowns show that character and celebration carry tone in the most-loved flows, such as Finch's hatch sunburst and Duolingo's Duo, but they cannot measure the effect ([ScreensDesign: Finch](https://screensdesign.com/articles/finch-app-onboarding-design/)). Apple's rules are the firmer guide. Use system haptics "according to their documented meanings", avoid overuse, and **make haptics optional** ([HIG: Playing haptics](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics)). The best-grounded "delight" is animation that shows real work, because that draws on the labour illusion rather than on charm.

### Onboarding is an audited "common task" for accessibility

Apple's Accessibility Nutrition Labels, announced May 2025, let apps declare support for VoiceOver, Voice Control, Larger Text (200%+), Dark Interface, Differentiate Without Color Alone, Sufficient Contrast, Reduced Motion, Captions and Audio Descriptions. To claim a feature, "users must be able to complete all of the common tasks" with it, and **"first launch experience, login, purchase, and settings" are named common tasks** ([App Store Connect Help](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-accessibility/overview-of-accessibility-nutrition-labels)). Labels are voluntary now, but Apple says they will be required for submissions "over time". The HIG sets the specifics: text that enlarges to at least 200%, 4.5:1 contrast for body text in both appearances, 44×44 pt targets, and fades instead of x/y/z motion under Reduce Motion ([HIG: Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). In a web view, Dynamic Type does not come for free. Custom gesture controls such as wheels and draggable timelines need accessible equivalents.

### Paywall timing follows day-0 behaviour, and honesty converts

RevenueCat's 2026 dataset covers 115k+ apps. In it, **82.1% of health and fitness trial starts happen on install day** (78% for productivity), and about half of all paid conversions also happen on day 0 ([RevenueCat SOSA 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)). The same report has an internal inconsistency: another section says one third of conversions happen on day zero. Median health and fitness figures are 6.9% download-to-trial, **37.7% trial-to-paid** and 2.9% download-to-paid by day 35. Trials of 5–9 days convert at 37.4%, against 25.5% for trials of 4 days or less, and 3-day trials see 55% of cancellations on day 0. Hard paywalls reach 10.7% download-to-paid by day 35 versus 2.1% for freemium, with nearly identical year-one retention of yearly subscribers (27% vs 28%) ([RevenueCat 2026 summary](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026)). That gap is an app-level, observational comparison confounded by acquisition channel. It is not a prediction for any single app. Hard paywalls also carry higher refund rates (5.8% vs 3.4%), and health and fitness has the second-highest refunds of any category ([SOSA 2025](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2025)). It also has the lowest first-renewal retention (30.3%) ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/health-fitness-app-subscription-benchmarks/)). In onboarding teardowns, the first paywall sits at a median step of 11, and category leaders put it about three-quarters of the way through. Only 22 paywalled flows were compared, so this is directional ([Lazyweb](https://experiments.lazyweb.com/research/do-category-leaders-paywall-earlier.md)). **No public dataset randomises paywall placement** (end of onboarding vs after a first workout vs day 3), and none reports day-30 user retention by paywall timing. That is the biggest gap in this area.

Rules and the one published ethical test point the same way. Apple requires describing "what the user will get for the price" before asking (3.1.2(c)). Practitioners report rejections when the trial is shown more prominently than the billed price ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/841626)). They also report rejections since mid-January 2026 for free-trial **toggles**, though Apple has announced no formal policy ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/rip-toggle-paywall)). Paid features may not depend on granting data access (5.1.1(ii)), and the HIG prefers letting people experience the app before a purchase prompt ([HIG: Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)). Blinkist tested a transparent timeline showing today, a reminder before the trial ends, and the charge date. It raised trial sign-ups 23%, **cut complaints 55%**, and lifted push opt-in from 6% to 74% because of the promised reminder ([UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/how-solving-our-biggest-customer-complaint-at-blinkist-led-to-a-23-increase-in-conversion-b60ad514134b)). That is company-reported with no sample size, but it is the clearest case of honesty and conversion lining up. The FTC's federal click-to-cancel rule was vacated in July 2025 ([Gunderson](https://www.gunder.com/en/news-insights/insights/client-insight-eighth-circuit-vacates-ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule)). The EU's online withdrawal-button requirement has applied since 19 June 2026, and how it interacts with App Store billing is unresolved ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/5/eu-consumer-law-new-withdrawal-button-requirements-for-online-contracts)).

## A small app can only measure big swings, and must keep health data out of analytics

Kohavi's guidance is that A/B tests need "at least thousands of active users, preferably tens of thousands". Detecting a 5% change takes around 200,000 users, and underpowered tests produce exaggerated winners ([GrowthBook on Kohavi](https://www.growthbook.io/blog/lessons-learned-from-ronny-kohavi-and-luke-sonnet-running-trustworthy-experiments)). Duolingo's rule of thumb was 100,000 DAU, at most three arms, and a 20–30% bar for new products ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker/)). The standard sample-size approximation (n ≈ 16·p(1−p)/δ² per arm) gives rough numbers. Detecting a lift in day-7 retention from 15% to 16.5% needs about **9,000 installs per arm**. Detecting a step completion rate moving from 10% to 12% needs about 3,600. An app with a few thousand installs a month can therefore test only large changes, one at a time, run over whole weeks. Below that volume, before-and-after cohort comparisons, a funnel and qualitative work are the honest tools. Blinkist's winning hypothesis came from mining complaints, and Headspace's null result was explained by watching where users got lost ([Growth.Design](https://growth.design/case-studies/trial-paywall-challenge); [Irrational Labs](https://irrationallabs.com/case-studies/headspace-doubled-course-starts/)). When tests do run, they need set rules. Decide the primary metric and guardrails before launch. Don't peek unless the engine is sequential. Check for sample-ratio mismatch. For paywall tests, wait for trials to expire and renew, or use predicted LTV ([Statsig](https://statsig.com/blog/sequential-testing-explained); [RevenueCat docs](https://revenuecat.com/docs/experiments-results-v1)). Trial starts should never be the deciding metric, because short-trial volume comes with day-0 cancellations. Flo shows why the investment pays off: it says onboarding drives **40% of its revenue**, and it rebuilt onboarding as server-driven so it can iterate quickly ([Flo engineering](https://medium.com/flo-health/mobile-onboarding-evolution-part-1-cfc9702835ce)).

Privacy constrains what can be logged. The FTC's revised Health Breach Notification Rule (2024) treats an unauthorised *disclosure* to third-party SDKs as a breach. GoodRx paid $1.5M in the first enforcement, and Flo was earlier sanctioned for sharing fertility data with Facebook and Google ([Akerman](https://www.akerman.com/en/perspectives/hrx-ftcs-enforcement-action-against-goodrx-breathes-new-life-into-decade-old-regulation.html); [PolicyMed](https://policymed.com/2023/03/goodrx-fined-in-first-violation-of-the-health-breach-notification-rule.html)). Apple bars using HealthKit data for advertising or data mining ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/protecting-user-privacy)). The workable pattern has three parts. Log step IDs, completions, skips, permission booleans and coarse buckets. Never log health values, free-text answers or injury details. Prefer first-party or self-hosted analytics.

## What to change in Logbook, in priority order

Logbook's current flow already does what the evidence supports. The race goal produces a live training-block shape read off the real plan builder, the same pattern as Noom's moving date and Runna's race times, but honest because it cannot promise a number the plan won't keep. "Writing your plan" waits only until the plan is actually saved, which is the defensible form of the labour illusion. The progress bar starts with the account segment filled, a head start with a real reason behind it. "You usually train: before work / after work" and the wake/leave timeline are implementation-intention questions, the type with meta-analytic support. The nudge step lets users decline on Logbook's own screen, which keeps the one-shot iOS prompt unused, and its framing matches Apple's "after scheduling" example. The name is optional, as 5.1.1(x) requires. Setup ends on a real first week and Today's first-run face rather than Bevel's blank dashboard. Replay setup lives in Settings, where the HIG says skipped onboarding should be found. Account deletion already exists in Settings, and the Capacitor splash already hides on first paint. The recommendations below build on that base. They are ordered by risk first, then by expected effect on whether a new user comes back on day 1 and day 7.

| Priority | Change | Why | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P0** | Make the AI-reading choice an explicit, unselected opt-in, not a switch that defaults to on | 5.1.2(i) requires "explicit permission" before personal data reaches third-party AI; a pre-enabled switch is the weakest reading of that | Apple rule; interpretation of "explicit" untested |
| **P0** | Before Apple Health data can reach the assistant, ask again and name the health data types | 5.1.2(ii) bars repurposing without new consent; 5.1.3(i) requires naming specific health data; HealthKit's third-party condition needs a legal read | Apple rule |
| **P1** | Move the 8-page tour after the payoff, or replace it with first-encounter tips and seeded examples | Tutorial decks don't improve task success and lower perceived ease; it currently sits between the last answer and the reveal | Rigorous (NN/g) plus HIG |
| **P1** | End onboarding with one real action on Today, then a success beat | Doing beats reading; gives a measurable activation event | Learning science, HIG, teardowns |
| **P1** | Design the day-1 return path: tomorrow's Today opens on the plan's first item, and the nudge lands there | Headspace's starts doubled but behaviour didn't, because users couldn't find their course | Partner experiment |
| **P1** | Instrument the funnel with privacy-safe, first-party events | You cannot improve, or safely add a paywall to, a funnel you can't see | Method guidance; FTC HBNR |
| **P1** | Run an accessibility pass on the whole flow as a "common task" | Accessibility Nutrition Label criteria name first launch and login | Apple program rule |
| **P2** | Offer Apple Health in context: "Fill from Apple Health" at weekly miles, or a payoff card that backfills recent runs | HIG says ask when the feature needs it; visible payoff as in Streaks | HIG; teardown pattern |
| **P2** | Tie starter habits to morning-routine anchors, and keep the nudge as fading scaffolding | Event cues build automaticity; clock reminders can hinder it | Peer-reviewed, small |
| **P2** | Move toward an account-later flow ("Save your plan"); meanwhile add passkeys or Sign in with Apple | HIG: delay sign-in; Duolingo's company-reported lift | HIG plus company anecdote |
| **P2** | Re-offer the nudge once, in context, to users who chose "No nudges" | Their system prompt is still unused; Calm's after-first-value timing | Vendor case study |
| **P3** | If a paywall comes: soft, after the first-week reveal, timeline-style, 7-day trial, no toggle | Day-0 trial concentration; 3.1.2 rejections; Blinkist test | Large dataset plus rules |
| **P3** | Add a haptics toggle and keep the native launch screen free of branding | HIG: haptics optional; launch screen guidance | Apple guidance |

### P0: Fix the AI consent before the next App Review submission

The privacy page is in the right place, before the account and before any data moves. Its one choice should become an explicit decision rather than a default. Show two equally weighted actions, such as "Let the assistant read my training" and "Not now", with neither preselected. Name the AI provider, list what is sent and what is not (the page already does this), and confirm in one line that the choice can be changed in Settings. The safer reading of "obtain explicit permission" is an affirmative act. Apple has published no compliant example, so this is a judgment, but the cost of the change is one tap. Users who choose "Not now" should meet a contextual re-ask the first time they use the ask bar, at the moment the value is obvious. That fits Apple's ask-in-context guidance and should recover most of the opt-ins a default would have captured. When Apple Health ships, its samples must not quietly join the assistant's context under the original training-data consent. Ask again, name the specific types (workouts, distance, heart rate), and have someone check the HealthKit condition that a third party must "also provide a health or fitness service to the user". The question is whether that condition is met when an AI provider acts as Logbook's processor.

### P1: Clear the path from the last answer to the payoff, then give the user something to do

The 8-page tour is well made: optional, looping, Reduce Motion-aware, with numbered steps. But it is still a deck of passive demos, the format NN/g found adds nothing and makes tasks feel harder. It also sits at the most expensive point in the flow, between the moment the user finishes answering and the moment they see the plan their answers built. Move it after "Open Today", offered once as a dismissible "See how Logbook works" card. Better still, break it into first-encounter tips: the first time the user opens Habits, Goals, Journal or the ask bar, show that page's demo in place. Seed Today with real example content in the spirit of Things' tutorial project, such as a starter to-do that teaches swipe-to-complete. Keep the full tour reachable from Settings as "How Logbook works", separate from Replay setup.

Then end onboarding with one real action, not just a view. On Today's first-run face, ask the user to do one small thing the plan just created: light the first habit, confirm tomorrow's wake time, or tick a seeded to-do. Mark it with the success haptic and a brief celebration. That turns a symbolic win (the plan reveal) into a behavioural one. It also creates the first candidate activation event: completed one Today action on day 0. Design the next morning just as deliberately. Headspace's quiz doubled starts and changed nothing downstream because returning users couldn't find their course. Logbook's equivalent risk is a day-1 Today that doesn't clearly lead with the first session of the new block. The morning nudge should deep-link to that item. Its text should never name health specifics, and it should give a generic preview for hidden notifications.

Instrumentation belongs in P1 because every later decision depends on it. Log events by step ID: viewed, completed and skipped for each question and each tour page, the AI choice, the nudge choice, plan saved, the first Today action, and returns on days 1, 7 and 30. Log nothing that is a health value or free text, and keep events first-party. Then run the activation analysis described earlier. Compare 30-day retention for users who did and didn't light a habit on day 0, log a run in week 1, or keep their morning plan on three mornings. Only after that, test whether nudging the strongest behaviour moves retention. At Logbook's likely volume, expect before-and-after cohorts and a handful of moderated sessions, not significance-tested A/B results.

The accessibility pass should treat the whole flow as a declared "common task". Three custom controls need checking in particular. The race-day wheel needs an adjustable VoiceOver control and a typed-date fallback. The wake/leave timeline needs stepper equivalents to dragging. Habit lights must not rely on colour alone. Web-view text must scale to at least 200% without truncation, and VoiceOver focus should move to each new step's heading. The value pager and tour need pause controls if they autoplay.

### P2: Bring Apple Health, habits and the account to the moment they pay off

Apple Health currently sits in Settings, which is compliant but means the weekly-miles question relies on guesswork. Offer "Fill from Apple Health" on that step, asking only for workouts and distance, with a purpose string that states the benefit in one active sentence. Alternatively, add a payoff card that backfills recent runs into the first week. Because read denial is invisible, write the empty state neutrally ("No runs found yet. You can change access in Settings › Health"). Don't show a connected/denied badge, and never build per-type toggles. Starter habits should attach to the morning routine chips the user just picked ("after coffee"), with the nudge framed as scaffolding that fades. Streaks need grace days or repair, never self-blame copy. Users who chose "No nudges" still have an unused system prompt. Offer the nudge once more after a few mornings of use, then never again.

Account creation is the largest remaining gap from Apple's "delay sign-in" guidance. The value pager softens it, and the privacy-first ordering is principled. The end state is the one the earlier design notes deferred: an anonymous, local-first session with "Save your plan" after the reveal, which also lines up with Duolingo's company-reported result. Until then, cut the cost of the account step. Add passkeys or Sign in with Apple beside email and password, and put a one-line benefit on the sign-in view ("Your plan, habits and journal, synced and backed up").

### P3: If a paywall arrives, put it after the reveal and make it honest

Logbook has no paywall, and nothing here argues for adding one to improve onboarding. If one comes, the day-0 concentration of trial starts argues for a soft offer straight after the first-week reveal, before full use, with a second placement at a natural value moment such as first use of the coach. Use a timeline layout (today, a reminder two days before the trial ends, the charge date) and a 7-day trial. Show the billed annual or monthly price at least as prominently as "free", use separate plan tiles rather than a trial toggle, and include a visible "Not now", Restore and the Terms and Privacy links. Never tie any paid feature to granting Apple Health. Judge it on trial-to-paid, refunds, day-0 cancellations and the day-7 retention of *all* installs, never on trial starts alone. Finally, two small Apple guidance items. Add a haptics on/off setting if none exists. Keep the native launch screen a plain background that matches first paint, and let the animated orb-and-wordmark splash play only on first launch. Signed-in users should go straight back to the screen they left.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the question from "how long should onboarding be?" to "what does each screen give back, and what happens tomorrow morning?" Length, motion and personalisation all prove weaker levers than the industry claims. The most rigorous results are mostly negative: decks don't teach, quizzes raise starts without changing behaviour, and behavioural effects shrink under scrutiny. What survives is a short list of mechanisms: real work made visible, specific when-and-where plans, habits anchored to existing routines, value before any ask, and a clear path back on day 1. Logbook's flow already uses most of them well. Its remaining risks are mostly structural: a default-on consent under a new App Review rule, a tour placed between the user and their plan, and a hand-off that ends on a view rather than an action.

The biggest unknown cannot be read from anyone else's data: which day-0 or week-1 behaviour actually predicts that a Logbook user is still training with the app in month two. No vendor benchmark answers that, and the famous thresholds were never causal. The most valuable next step is not another redesign. It is a privacy-safe funnel and a cohort analysis that tells Logbook what its own activation event is, followed by a single test of whether onboarding can push users toward it.
