# Calm to-do apps win by deciding placement

The iPhone to-do apps that reviewers praise most win on two things. They make capture fast, and they keep the daily list calm. Feature count is not what separates them. Things 3 is the reference for both: one draggable add button that files a task by where you drop it, a start date ("When") kept apart from a deadline so Today stays short, unfinished items carried forward with no red overdue count, and a screen that is mostly white space with color used only for meaning. Todoist is the reference for structured speed: one line of typed marks, where any wrongly read word goes back to plain text with a tap. Apple Reminders now leads on capture from outside the app. The day planners (Sunsama, Akiflow, Amie) look good on the desktop and are the most criticized apps on the iPhone. The evidence is weaker than the marketing. No source measures taps or seconds to capture for any app, accessibility reviews barely exist, the most-cited reviews of Things date from 2017, and the research on points and streaks does not show they raise completion. Orby already has the strongest structural ideas in the category: a when-style day that surfaces a to-do on its date, silent carry-forward, a hand-ordered top three, drag to Tomorrow, and a reward layer the person can switch off. What it lacks is the plumbing every rival treats as basic: repeating to-dos, an optional alert time, a way to undo a misread mark, and capture without opening the app. In priority order, Orby should make its quick-add marks tappable and undoable, add repeating to-dos with one live copy at a time, add an optional reminder time with Done and Tomorrow actions in the notification, turn day-old leftovers into one gentle offer instead of a growing pile, and then build a Control Center, Action Button and Lock Screen path into the add field. It should leave its poster Today, its Orbs and its drag model alone, and skip Todoist-style filters, red overdue lists, time-blocking timelines and default nagging.

## Eleven apps, two families, and only one family works well on the phone

The eleven apps sort into **list managers** (Things, Todoist, TickTick, Apple Reminders, Microsoft To Do, Superlist), which organize by container plus date-based smart lists, and **day planners** (Sunsama, Structured, Akiflow, Any.do's Plan my Day, Amie), which organize around the day itself, usually a timeline with a backlog behind it. The single most useful organizing idea in the category belongs to Things. Its When picker sets the day you intend to start, and a to-do moves into Today when that date arrives. A separate deadline marks when it must be done, and **"tasks with a deadline remain active in the Anytime list"** instead of crowding Today ([Cultured Code](https://culturedcode.com/things/support/articles/2803579/)). Todoist by contrast shows only dated tasks in Today and Upcoming and puts P1 tasks "at the top of your Today view in red" ([Todoist](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/plan-your-day-with-the-todoist-today-view-UVUXaiSs)). Its own daily guidance shrinks the whole system to one rule: mark "the three most important tasks you need to complete today" as P1. Things carries more concepts (six fixed lists, When versus Deadline, areas, projects, headings, tags, checklists of up to 100 items), but the lists are fixed and named in plain words, and Zapier still calls the hierarchy "surprisingly intuitive" ([Zapier](https://zapier.com/blog/best-todo-list-apps/)). A 2017 review praised the loose, human wording of Today, This Evening and Someday as a strength in itself ([Brooks Review](https://brooksreview.net/2017/08/things-3)).

Praise and complaint follow the families. The list managers draw praise for calm polish and fast entry. Things is "gorgeous and great to use" and never cluttered ([Zapier](https://zapier.com/blog/best-todo-list-apps/)). Microsoft To Do is "a beautiful, simple task management tool" with **4.7 stars from about 257,000 ratings** ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/the-successor-to-wunderlist-is-here-microsoft-to-do/); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/ae/app/microsoft-to-do/id1212616790)). TickTick sits near **4.9 from about 45,000 ratings** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/ticktick-to-do-list-calendar/id626144601)). The day planners collect the worst iPhone verdicts. A February 2026 App Store review of Sunsama says the "mobile app is near unusable for task editing/addition" ([Usecarly](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/sunsama-review/)). Akiflow's mobile app "is just a companion" ([Dhruvir Zala](https://dhruvirzala.com/akiflow-review/)). Amie is called beautiful by "almost everyone" yet holds **3.1 stars from about 40 ratings**, with complaints about speed and Apple Calendar sync ([Ellie](https://ellieplanner.com/comparisons/amie-calendar-review); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/amie-todos-calendar/id1548277133)), and one comparison site says it has since pivoted toward AI notetaking, which no maker source confirms ([efficient.app](https://efficient.app/compare/akiflow-vs-amie)). Superlist earns design praise but is described as buggy ([efficient.app](https://efficient.app/compare/sunsama-vs-superlist)). The lesson is that feel depends on speed and reliability as much as on visuals. A pretty app that is slow at adding and editing on the phone loses.

A word on what this evidence can carry. Maker help pages are reliable on how a feature behaves and say nothing about whether it works. Independent reviews carry the qualitative consensus, but the deepest Things review is from 2017 ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/things-3-beauty-and-delight-in-a-task-manager/)). User comments are anecdotes. Marketing claims, such as Akiflow pulling tasks "in from thousands of apps" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/akiflow-ai-planner-calendar/id1621279084)) or Todoist's "turn your scattered thoughts into clear tasks" ([Todoist](https://www.todoist.com/help/todoist/product-updates/turn-your-scattered-thoughts-into-clear-tasks-ramble-jan-21-2026-HhmP8ue8R)), arrive with no usage data. Widely repeated statistics fail on inspection. UX Magazine's "Stanford 2024 Friction Index Study" could not be found ([UX Magazine](https://uxmag.com/articles/friction-science-why-users-drop-off)), and the "41% of to-do items are never completed" figure could not be traced to any source. Where makers working independently have landed on the same design, this report treats that convergence as the strongest available signal.

## Capture is won by placement and undoable marks, not by forms

Two capture models dominate. Things' **Magic Plus** sits in the lower right. A tap adds a to-do at the top of the current list. A drag drops it at an exact spot in Today, under a heading, onto a day in Upcoming (which sets its start date), or onto the Inbox target. Only a name is needed to save ([Cultured Code](https://culturedcode.com/things/features/); [The Sweet Setup](https://thesweetsetup.com/a-guide-to-capturing-tasks-in-things-3-for-ipad-and-iphone/)). MacStories called it "one of the most clever methods of task entry I've seen", and also said entry in Things "may not be the quickest available, especially when creating tasks in bulk" ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/things-3-beauty-and-delight-in-a-task-manager/)). Todoist's **Quick Add** is the speed benchmark: one box that reads dates and repeats in plain words, `#project /section`, `p1` to `p3`, `!reminder` and `{deadline}`. Todoist has now copied the drag as "Dynamic Add" ([Todoist](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/use-task-quick-add-in-todoist-va4Lhpzz)). That borrowing is telling: the two leaders converged on a floating, draggable add button in thumb reach.

Inline parsing raises one hazard that both parsers solve the same way. In Todoist, a word wrongly read as a date goes back to plain text when you **"click or tap the word"**. In TickTick you press Delete or tap the recognized time, and smart parsing can be turned off ([Todoist](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/use-task-quick-add-in-todoist-va4Lhpzz); [TickTick](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782422935240704)). TickTick users still find its parser "sluggish" next to Todoist's for patterns like "3rd Wednesday of every month" ([r/ticktick](https://axebps-redlib.hf.space/r/ticktick/comments/1im1vkt/smart_date_parsing_help)). Things avoids the hazard entirely. It parses only inside its When field, where Jump Start accepts fragments such as "tom", "Sat" and "Wed 8pm" ([Cultured Code](https://culturedcode.com/things/features/)). That protects titles like "Call May" or "Read 1984" at the cost of an extra step. Orby's quick add follows the Todoist pattern with a lighter grammar (`!` to `!!!`, `p1` to `p3`, today, tomorrow, a weekday, later, someday, `#List` at the end of a line). It already prints what it understood under the field ("P2 · NOV 13 · WORK"). It has no way to reverse a single mark, and because it reads only the last words of the line, "Finish the essay on friday" saves as "Finish the essay on". That trailing preposition is a small, real defect, and it is exactly the case the tap-to-undo convention exists for.

The biggest capture shift of 2025 and 2026 happened outside the apps. Apple's iOS 26 Reminders gained a New Reminder control for **Control Center, the Lock Screen and the Action Button**: an interactive snippet with a keyboard and list, date and flag fields. MacStories called it "fantastic", and notes it uses private APIs that Things and Todoist cannot match ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/stories/ios-and-ipados-26-the-macstories-review/16)). Todoist's **Ramble** voice capture went to every user on January 21, 2026, in 38 languages, and can be bound to the Action Button or placed as a Lock Screen widget. Its documented limits show the trade: it cannot yet file into a project or add labels or reminders ([Todoist](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/use-ramble-to-add-tasks-by-voice-P1Raq7vVF); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/01/21/todoists-app-now-lets-you-add-tasks-to-your-to-do-list-by-speaking-to-its-ai/)). Things lists Siri and Shortcuts, widgets, Control Center and Lock Screen buttons, Home Screen Quick Actions and Mail to Things ([Cultured Code](https://culturedcode.com/things/features/)), and Siri remains reviewers' favorite way into Reminders ([Zapier](https://zapier.com/blog/best-iphone-ipad-to-do-list-apps/)). No source measured taps or seconds from a locked phone to a saved task for any app. By reading the documented flows, the in-app path is about three taps plus typing, and a system control removes the app launch.

On where new items land, Things and Todoist default to an **Inbox**, while Microsoft To Do, Any.do, Sunsama, Akiflow and Structured are built around the day ([Things URL scheme](https://culturedcode.com/things/support/articles/2803573/); [Zapier](https://zapier.com/blog/best-iphone-ipad-to-do-list-apps/)). No study compares the two. The relevant research is indirect but useful. Masicampo and Baumeister found that making a specific plan for an unfinished goal removed its intrusive thoughts, while noting it did not ([JPSP 2011 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_918752704)). That argues for giving every task a when eventually, not necessarily at capture. Orby defaults to Today and lands a to-do on the day whose page you add from. For a list whose job is to name one next move, the Today default is right only because the poster shows three stops and folds the rest.

## Calm daily lists carry yesterday forward without a red count

Daily planning in the category runs from passive to guided. Things' Today fills itself as start dates arrive. Microsoft To Do's My Day "resets every night, so you have a blank slate", and unfinished tasks come back the next day as suggestions behind a light-bulb button ([Microsoft](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/ToDo/my-day-and-suggestions)). Sunsama began as a multi-step morning ritual with an evening shutdown and has become an agent, Autoplan, that drafts the day for review. The maker says **"planning usually takes 2–4 minutes"** ([Sunsama](https://help.sunsama.com/docs/daily-planning)), which is a maker claim, not a measurement. That shift from a ritual you perform to a draft you approve reads as an admission that the full ritual was a barrier. Microsoft's users point at the same failure from the other side: one says they "cant remember to do that on a daily basis" ([Microsoft Tech Community](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/tag/Completion%20sound)). Users also ask to plan My Day the night before ([Microsoft Tech Community](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/discussions/to-do/feature-request-add-to-my-day-tomorrow/1930251)). A ritual sticks when the default is already filled in and the moment to act arrives on its own.

What happens to yesterday's leftovers separates calm apps from anxious ones. Todoist parks them in a red **Overdue** section with a bulk Reschedule button and warns, "Don't let overdue tasks build up from one day to the next" ([Todoist](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/plan-your-day-with-the-todoist-today-view-UVUXaiSs)). A Todoist-hosted essay describes the result: as overdue tasks pile up, people "avoid their task lists until they snowballed out of control" ([Todoist Inspiration](https://todoist.com/inspiration/gtd-tips)). Things users describe old to-dos simply staying in Today with no overdue state ([Griply board](https://griply.featurebase.app/p/date-rollover-for-overdue-tasks-not-for-deadlines)). Sunsama rolls everything over at midnight and is the only app with a documented way to end a pileup on its own: tasks that roll **"more than four days"** go to an Archive, adjustable from 1 to 14 days ([Sunsama](https://help.sunsama.com/docs/archive)). Even that draws complaints from users who "have to pull them out the archive every few days" ([Sunsama roadmap](https://roadmap.sunsama.com/improvements/p/set-task-rollover-days-by-channel)). Orby already behaves like Things: a Today to-do stays in Today until it is done, moved or deleted, and a later one surfaces on its date. The research says what to add. Rescheduling a leftover to a specific day is itself a plan in Masicampo and Baumeister's sense. And a 2025 meta-analysis found no general memory advantage for unfinished tasks, only a reliable tendency to resume them ([Ghibellini and Meier](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)). So leftovers should come back as offers to resume, not as counts that nag.

Every app supports recurrence and some kind of subtask. The design question is how a repeat interacts with rollover. Things and Sunsama both avoid stacking duplicate copies of a missed repeat: Sunsama rolls a recurring task "until they collide with the next instance" ([Sunsama](https://help.sunsama.com/docs/task-rollover-and-recurring-tasks-the-basics)), and users report that a Things repeat left in Today moves on instead of becoming overdue ([Medium](https://medium.com/the-extratextual/things-3-e8f2f704b662)). Things' own long-standing slowness on repeating tasks was reportedly addressed in a 2026 update ([iDrop News](https://www.idropnews.com/news/things-3-repeating-tasks-update-2026/267466/)). Subtasks range from Things' checklists to Any.do's AI-suggested steps, which Zapier warns could encourage procrastination ([Zapier](https://zapier.com/blog/best-todo-list-apps/)). Calendar integration splits three ways. Things shows events beside Today without blocking time ([Morgen](https://www.morgen.so/blog-posts/todoist-vs-things-3)). Todoist (paid), TickTick, Structured and Any.do let you drop a task onto an hour. Sunsama fits tasks around your events automatically. Timelines carry a known cost: people underestimate their own tasks badly. Students predicted a thesis in **33.9 days and took 55.5**, and about 30% finished by their own date ([Buehler et al. 1994](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)). Structured's busy days "collapse into a hard-to-read stack of color" ([MakerStack](https://makerstack.co/reviews/structured-review/)).

Reminders follow one rule: nagging is loved by people who chose it. Due re-alerts 5 times by default, up to 10, at intervals of 1 to 60 minutes ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/id390017969)). Its users call "constant nagging" the best feature, and one complains of double alerts that even iOS limits could not "shut up". Apple Reminders adds location and per-reminder time zones ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/stories/ios-and-ipados-26-the-macstories-review/16)). Orby's to-dos have no alert time at all. Orby already schedules goal reminders through local notifications, so the infrastructure is there.

Implementation intentions, the "when and where" plans that a dated to-do partly is, help but less than the famous figure. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis of 94 tests found d = 0.65 ([summary](https://howtostopprocrastinating.org/research/gollwitzer-2006-implementation-intentions/)), and a 2024 update reportedly falls to about 0.15 after bias correction ([summary](https://goalsandprogress.com/implementation-intentions-research/)). No controlled study shows that a cap of three, or any number, raises completion. Orby's top three rests on Todoist's own guidance and on the planning fallacy, not on direct proof.

## Completion should be short, felt and mutable; points stay unproven

Gestures in the category center on Things. Swipe right schedules, swipe left selects, a stroke down the selection circles selects a run of rows, and a long press lifts a row to reorder Today or drop it on a future date ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/things-3-beauty-and-delight-in-a-task-manager/); [iMore](https://www.imore.com/all-things-i-absolutely-love-about-things-3-and-few-things-i-dont)). Todoist makes swipes configurable and in January 2026 added "None" to turn them off ([Todoist](https://todoist.com/help/articles/how-to-change-your-swipe-actions); [changelog](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/2026-changelog-HD3jJAtLd)). The best-documented interaction failure is Todoist's long-press reorder, which a 2024 critique calls **"finicky"**, because the same hold can open details or complete a task by accident ([IXD@Pratt](https://ixd.prattsi.org/2024/01/design-critique-todoist-ios-app/)). Orby uses one hold for both its menu and its drag, and separates them by what happens next: a still hold opens the menu, and a move after the hold lifts the row. That is the threshold fix the critique points toward. It is worth testing on device for accidental lifts, but it needs no redesign. Orby's drop targets (Tomorrow and Delete docks, the goal as "make it a step") apply Things' strongest idea, position as meaning, to rescheduling.

The completion moments people love are about half a second long, combine sight, sound and touch, and can be muted. A third-party reconstruction of Things puts the sequence at roughly **500 ms**: the circle fills, the check springs, the row slides away, with a soft sound and a light haptic. These are an outsider's estimates, not Cultured Code's values ([Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/guides/design/things)). MacStories credits the haptics with making Things feel "that much more real". Every app with a completion sound also ships a switch for it. Microsoft To Do's chime is "absolutely LOVE"d by some users and too loud for others ([Microsoft Tech Community](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/discussions/to-do/microsoft-to-do-problems-with-the-bell-sound/2264104/replies/2878826)). Todoist's Karma is the full game: points, 8 levels from Beginner to Enlightened at 50,000, streaks, and lost points for tasks 5 or more days overdue. It also has every safety valve: days off, vacation mode, goals set to zero, a celebrations switch and a full Karma toggle ([Todoist](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/introduction-to-karma-OgWkWy)). Those valves read as lessons from users who felt punished. The research does not back big claims. Kappen and colleagues note that studies of gamified task managers are lacking and that routine tasks "do not tend to be intrinsically motivating" ([Kappen et al.](https://www.academia.edu/4678172/Deconstructing_Gamified_Task_Management_Applications)), and the one controlled study found ran 12 days with 20 people ([Lessel et al.](https://umtl.cs.uni-saarland.de/paper_preprints/p2026-lessel.pdf)). Orby's Orbs already follow the safe shape: the control's own haptic, one Orb that rises and is gone by 620 ms, an Undo toast, no loss framing, and switches for showing or keeping Orbs. Nothing in the evidence argues for more.

Visual design rewards restraint. MacStories found Things "dominated by white space" with "bold fonts" and "thoughtful splashes of color" ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/things-3-beauty-and-delight-in-a-task-manager/)). An outside study estimates it at about **95% neutral**, with one hue per concept: yellow for Today, red for deadlines, indigo for Evening ([Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/guides/design/things)). Things 3 won the 2017 Apple Design Award ([Apple](https://apps.apple.com/us/story/id1462498106)), and its designer names "unrivaled simplicity" as the key. The 2026 jury made Structured an Inclusivity finalist for being "visually sharp and easy to scan", praised by neurodivergent users for its "simple layout" ([Apple Design Awards](https://developer.apple.com/design/awards/)). Recent awards reward clarity, not decoration. Things has no priority field at all, and gets its order from the When and deadline signals. Todoist and TickTick paint priority on every row, and TickTick users ask to recolor everything ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/170641/TickTick/reviews/)). Orby's flat sky-blue poster, with to-do colors limited to a 10 px dot, already sits on the Things side of that line.

Empty states and accessibility are where the evidence runs out. Todoist redesigned its empty screens to give "helpful, contextual tips" and still illustrates them ([Adobe XD](https://xd.adobe.com/ideas/perspectives/interviews/reimagining-an-app-from-the-ground-up-behind-the-scenes-of-todoists-redesign); [Releasebot](https://releasebot.io/updates/todoist)). Microsoft's empty My Day offers suggestions behind a button, and Structured pre-fills tasks with Apple's on-device models. These are the two models: a reward picture, or a next step. No independent accessibility review of any of the eleven apps from blind or low-vision users turned up, and nothing documents how Things or Todoist expose drag and swipe to VoiceOver. Orby's existing rules (Move up and Move down as the non-drag path, a polite "Moved to second" status, docks hidden from VoiceOver) go further than anything the leaders document, so Orby should keep following Apple's guidelines rather than copy a rival.

## Orby's to-do section: eight changes in priority order

The ranking weighs how much each change helps one person every day, how well it fits a calm poster with one clear next move, and what it costs. The first four close gaps that every serious rival has filled and that Orby's data model can absorb. The fifth is the largest speed gain and the most native work. The last three are refinements.

Several things should stay as they are, because Orby already holds the category's best answer. The due date that surfaces a to-do into Today on its day is Things' When, and it should not grow a Todoist-style due date that turns every deadline into an overdue item. If a hard deadline is ever needed, it should follow Things: a separate flag that counts down without moving the to-do into Today. The poster Today with three stops and the next one lit is a stricter version of Todoist's top-three advice. Hold for the menu, hold and move to drag, the Tomorrow and Delete docks and drop-on-goal-to-make-a-step are Things' position-as-meaning idea applied to rescheduling. "Make it a step" covers what checklists cover in Things for the one case that matters here, a to-do that serves the day's goal, so nested subtasks are not worth their weight. The Orbs, with their switches and no loss framing, already match the safe shape of Todoist's valves without Karma's clutter.

Some things should not be adopted. Labels and filter queries add a ceiling no single person needs. A red Overdue section is the documented start of the avoidance spiral. Hour-by-hour time blocking collides with the planning fallacy and with Structured's busy-day stack. Completion sounds by default split users, so a sound, if ever added, belongs behind an off-by-default switch. Repeat nagging belongs only to a person who asks for it per item. AI that plans the whole day, Sunsama style, conflicts with "one clear next move" chosen by the person, though the assistant can still propose.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question Orby should ask. Orby does not need to catch the leaders on features. It needs to stop the small leaks that make a calm list feel untrustworthy: a mark read wrong with no way back, a chore retyped every month, a time remembered only by luck, a task carried for a week with nothing said. Each of these is cheap next to the structure Orby already has, and each fixes a moment when a person would otherwise quietly stop trusting the list. Capture outside the app is the one large investment that clearly pays, because it is where independent makers converged in 2025 and 2026. Since Apple keeps its inline snippet private, Orby's version will open the app to the field rather than save in place.

Three open questions should shape the build. No one has measured whether a three-item cap, silent carry-forward or a completion reward changes what gets done. Orby can measure them for its one person, by logging how often to-dos are carried, moved to Tomorrow or completed from the poster, and checking the leftovers offer against that baseline. Accessibility claims about rivals are unknowable from public sources, so Orby's own rules, not imitation, are the standard. And because the most cited design reviews are years old, any borrowed gesture should be tried on a current iPhone before it is copied.
